Building blocks

Before verbs, before tenses, Spanish is built from nouns and the words that dress them. This foundation lesson covers gender, plurals, the articles (el / la / un / una), and how adjectives agree — plus possessives and demonstratives. Get these right and every sentence you write clicks into place.

Work through each screen and answer the questions as you go — some ask you to recognise the right form, some ask you to produce it by typing. Collect ⭐ stars and press Start.

Every noun has a gender

Masculine or feminine?

Every Spanish noun is either masculine or feminine — even objects. The ending is your best clue:

  • Most nouns ending -o are masculine: el libro, el coche.
  • Most nouns ending -a are feminine: la casa, la mesa.
  • Nouns ending -ción / -sión / -dad / -tad are feminine: la nación, la ciudad, la libertad.

Learn these exceptions. Some Greek-origin words ending -ma are masculine: el día, el mapa, el problema, el tema, el clima. And a few in -o are feminine: la mano, la foto, la radio. When a noun surprises you, learn it with its article.

People come in two genders

Making the feminine of people

Nouns for people usually have a masculine and a feminine form:

patternexample
-o → -ael amigo → la amiga
add -a to -orel profesor → la profesora
no change: -ista / -ante / -enteel/la artista, el/la estudiante

So: el turista / la turista both stay the same — only the article changes. el estudiante / la estudiante likewise. But el amigo → la amiga and el profesor → la profesora.

One or many?

Making plurals

Four simple rules cover almost every plural:

the noun ends in…plural
a vowel → add -scasa → casas
a consonant → add -esciudad → ciudades, profesor → profesores
-z → -cesluz → luces, lápiz → lápices
-ión: drop accent, add -escanción → canciones, región → regiones

Why the accent drops: in canción the accent marks the stress on the last syllable. Once you add -es, the stress falls naturally, so the written accent is no longer needed: canciones.

The little words: articles

el / la and un / una

Articles must match the noun in gender and number:

Definite — "the"
elmasc. sing.
lafem. sing.
losmasc. plural
lasfem. plural
Indefinite — "a / some"
unmasc. sing.
unafem. sing.
unosmasc. plural
unasfem. plural

Careful: the feminine plural of un is unas (not unos). So "some houses" = unas casas, but "some books" = unos libros.

Two contractions · one article quirk

del, al, and when Spanish keeps "the"

Two contractions are compulsory — you may never write the two words apart:

de + el = del  ·  a + el = alonly with masculine el — never de la or a la

Spanish uses the where English drops it:

  • General likes/nouns: Me gusta el chocolate — I like chocolate.
  • Days of the week: el lunes = "on Monday".
  • Languages & abstract nouns: el español es fácil.

But it drops "a" before an unmodified job after ser: Soy profesor (I'm a teacher) — never soy un profesor.

  • un coche rojo · una casa roja · unas flores rojas.
  • un libro verde · unas mesas verdes.

Nationalities are special. Even ending in a consonant, they add -a for feminine: español / española / españoles / españolas; francés / francesa / franceses / francesas — and the feminine/plural forms drop the accent (francés → francesa).

Position & shortened forms

A few adjectives go first

Most adjectives follow the noun, but a small set goes before it — and some shorten before a masculine singular noun:

full formbefore masc. sing.
buenoun buen amigo
maloun mal día
primeroel primer día
terceroel tercer piso

grande → gran shortens before any singular noun and its meaning shifts to "great", not "big": una gran ciudad = "a great city" (not a big one). Ordinals primer / segundo / tercer are worth knowing.

Whose is it? Possessives

mi, tu, su, nuestro…

Possessive adjectives agree in number (and nuestro/vuestro also in gender):

meaningforms
mymi / mis · mi casa, mis libros
your (tú)tu / tus
his / her / your(formal) / theirsu / sus
ournuestro/a/os/as · nuestra profesora
your (vosotros)vuestro/a/os/as

Watch su: it covers his, her, your (formal) and their. su casa could be "his house", "her house", "your house" or "their house" — context decides.

This and that: demonstratives

este / ese and their family

Demonstrative adjectives also agree in gender and number:

this / these
estemasc. sing.
estafem. sing.
estosmasc. plural
estasfem. plural
that / those
esemasc. sing.
esafem. sing.
esosmasc. plural
esasfem. plural

Tip: este ("this") has a t — think "t for this, here". ese ("that") is further away. E.g. este libro (this book), esas casas (those houses).

Higher only Higher: aquel / aquella / aquellos / aquellas means "that / those over there" (further still). Also higher: the neuter article lo + a masculine singular adjective makes an abstract noun — lo bueno = "the good thing". You don't need to drill these at Foundation.

Recap

Your building-blocks toolkit

Gender: -o usually masc., -a usually fem.; -ción/-sión/-dad/-tad fem. Learn exceptions (el día, el mapa, el problema; la mano, la foto).

Plurals: vowel +s, consonant +es, -z → -ces, -ión drops accent +es (canción → canciones).

Articles: el/la/los/las, un/una/unos/unas; de+el=del, a+el=al; keep "the" for likes/days/languages, drop "a" before a job (Soy profesor).

Adjectives agree: -o = 4 forms, -e/consonant = 2 (but nationalities add -a: francés → francesa). Usually after the noun.

Shortened forms: buen, mal, primer, tercer before masc. sing.; gran = "great" before any singular.

Possessives & demonstratives: mi(s), tu(s), su(s), nuestro/a…; este/ese families.
