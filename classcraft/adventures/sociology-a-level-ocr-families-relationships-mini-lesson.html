Families and relationships

📍 Where this sits: this is Component 01, Section B, Option 1. In Component 01 you sit the compulsory Section A (Introducing socialisation, culture and identity) and then ONE option from Section B: Families and relationships, Youth subcultures, or Media. Check which one your school teaches — you only answer on one.

This lesson covers the two OCR key questions for this option: How diverse are modern families? and To what extent are roles and relationships within families changing?

OCR is explicit: detailed knowledge of statistics on marriage, divorce and demographic change is not required — but you must know the trends over roughly the last 30 years and, crucially, be able to explain them theoretically.

Answer the questions as you go and collect ⭐ stars. Press Start when you are ready.

KQ1 · family and household types

The diversity of families and households

First, a distinction that earns easy marks: a family is a group connected by blood, marriage or adoption; a household is simply people sharing a residence. Not every household is a family.

  • Nuclear family — two generations: parents and dependent children.
  • Extended family — beyond the nuclear unit. Vertically extended (grandparents) or horizontally extended (aunts, uncles, cousins). A modern variant is the beanpole family — a term used by Julia Brannen for families that are long and thin: several living generations, but few people in each, because families are smaller and people live longer.
  • Lone-parent family — one parent with dependent children; the great majority are headed by women.
  • Reconstituted (step) family — formed when partners with children from previous relationships join.
  • Same-sex families — increasingly visible and legally recognised in the UK following civil partnership and same-sex marriage legislation.
  • Non-family households — people living alone (a rapidly growing group at both ends of the age range) and shared houses of unrelated adults.
  • Newer and emerging forms — OCR asks for these: 'living apart together' couples, transnational families kept together by digital communication, multi-generational households formed by housing costs, and 'families of choice'.
KQ1 · trends and demography

Trends: marriage, divorce, cohabitation, demography

Know the direction of travel and, above all, the explanations.

  • Marriage — fewer marriages, and people marry later. Why? Secularisation weakens religious pressure; cohabitation is normalised; women's economic independence removes the financial necessity; and marriage has become a choice rather than an expectation.
  • Divorce — rose substantially across the later twentieth century. Explanations: legal change (the Divorce Reform Act 1969 allowed irretrievable breakdown, making divorce far easier), secularisation, women's growing economic independence (they can afford to leave), declining stigma, and — per Giddens — higher expectations of marriage itself: we now ask it to deliver emotional fulfilment, and are disappointed more easily.
  • Cohabitation — has risen sharply, and for many couples is now either a prelude to marriage or a permanent alternative.
  • Demography — OCR names five: a falling birth rate, smaller family size, rising age at marriage, rising age of childbearing, and an ageing population. Drivers: contraception, women's education and careers, the cost of children, and improved healthcare and life expectancy.

The ageing population consequences you must name: more beanpole families; the pivot or 'sandwich' generation (typically middle-aged women) caring for children and parents at once; a growing role for grandparents in childcare; and rising pressure on pensions and social care. Feminists point out that the burden of this new care falls disproportionately on women — a modern extension of the double shift.

KQ1 · diversity by class, ethnicity, sexuality

Mapping family diversity

Rhona and Robert Rapoport (1982) gave the classic framework — five types of family diversity:

  • Organisational — differences in how roles and the division of labour are organised.
  • Cultural — differences between ethnic and religious groups.
  • Class — differences in resources, child-rearing practices and expectations.
  • Life-course — where a family is in its own cycle (newlyweds, small children, empty nest).
  • Cohort — the historical period a family lived through shapes it.

By ethnicity: Roger Ballard's research on South Asian families in Britain found extended kinship networks remaining strong and important, providing practical and economic support, though households themselves were often nuclear. Richard Berthoud's analysis of family formation in multi-cultural Britain identified contrasting patterns — including a greater tendency towards 'modern individualism' among Caribbean families, alongside more traditional patterns among some South Asian groups. Handle both with care: they are tendencies, not rules, and class cuts across them.

By sexuality: Kath Weston (Families We Choose, 1991) documented lesbian and gay people constructing families of choice — chosen networks of care that do the work families do, without necessarily involving blood or law. Jeffrey Weeks argues these relationships are more likely to be negotiated and egalitarian, precisely because there is no ready-made script.

KQ1 · the ideology of the nuclear family

Five theories of the nuclear family

  • Functionalism. George Murdock (1949), surveying 250 societies, claimed the nuclear family is universal and performs four essential functions: sexual, reproductive, economic and educational. Talcott Parsons (1955) narrowed this to two 'basic and irreducible' functions: primary socialisation and the stabilisation of adult personalities — the family as a 'warm bath' soothing the worker after work. He also described instrumental (breadwinner) and expressive (nurturing) roles.
  • New Right. The nuclear family with a married mother and father is the ideal, and its decline causes social problems. Charles Murray argues welfare has made fatherlessness affordable, producing an underclass of boys with no role model. Policy: strengthen marriage, cut benefits.
  • Marxism. Friedrich Engels (The Origin of the Family, Private Property and the State, 1884) argued monogamous marriage arose to guarantee the legitimate inheritance of private property. Eli Zaretsky (1976) argued the family is an ideological haven — it offers the illusion of a private world of comfort while actually reproducing labour power for free and serving as a unit of consumption.
  • Feminism. Delphy and Leonard argue the family is a patriarchal institution in which women's unpaid labour is expropriated by men. Feminists insist on the dark side of the family — the domestic violence, abuse and coercive control that functionalism's 'warm bath' entirely omits.
  • Postmodernism. Judith Stacey (Brave New Families, 1990) found women actively remaking family life, producing the divorce-extended family in which ex-partners, new partners and their kin form new networks of support. There is no longer one dominant family form — only families, plural and chosen.

KQ1 · the diversity debate

How diverse is the family really?

This is the essay OCR most wants you to be able to argue. Two positions:

  • Diversity is real and profound. Stacey, Giddens and Beck and Beck-Gernsheim (The Normal Chaos of Love, 1995) all argue that in a detraditionalised society the family has become negotiated: it survives only as long as it satisfies its members, and takes whatever shape they choose. The nuclear family is now one option among many.
  • Diversity is exaggerated. Robert Chester (1985) coined the neo-conventional family: yes, family life has changed — most notably because both partners now work — but the overwhelming majority of people still live in, or expect to live in, a couple-based family with children. Many 'alternative' forms are life-course stages, not permanent identities: the lone parent remarries; the young flat-sharer settles down. Chester's point is that a snapshot of households exaggerates diversity, because it freezes people at one moment in a life that is still, mostly, conventional.

The judgement to reach: both are partly right. There is genuine, irreversible structural diversity (same-sex families, reconstituted families, solo living) and a persistent cultural attachment to the couple-with-children ideal. The ideology of the nuclear family has outlived its statistical dominance — and that gap is where the New Right's anxiety and the feminists' anger both come from.

KQ2 · roles between partners

The domestic division of labour

  • Elizabeth Bott (1957) gave us the basic distinction: segregated conjugal roles (clearly separate tasks and leisure) versus joint conjugal roles (shared tasks and leisure).
  • The march of progress. Willmott and Young (The Symmetrical Family, 1973) argued the family had become symmetrical: roles are not identical but are more equal, with both partners working and men doing more at home. They explained its spread through stratified diffusion — the pattern starts in the middle class and filters down.
  • The feminist demolition. Ann Oakley (The Sociology of Housework, 1974) tested this directly and found the claim of symmetry unsupported: she found only a small minority of husbands gave a high level of participation in housework, and childcare help was concentrated in the pleasurable tasks. Her respondents described housework as monotonous, fragmented and isolating. Symmetry, she argued, was a myth — and Willmott and Young's evidence had been too easily satisfied.
  • The modern picture. Change is real but partial: men do more than their grandfathers, but women still carry the larger load, and — decisively — the mental load of planning, remembering and organising. Duncombe and Marsden add emotion work: the triple shift.
  • The comparison that proves the point. Gillian Dunne's research on lesbian households (1999) found a markedly more equal division of labour, and explained it by the absence of gender scripts: with no 'man's job' and 'woman's job' available, tasks were negotiated. If equality appears when gender is removed from the equation, then gender — not practicality — was doing the work all along.

KQ2 · power and the dark side

Power, money and the dark side of the family

  • Money is power. Jan Pahl's research on household finances showed that pooling income does not mean sharing control: who manages the money and who controls it are different things, and in many households men retained control of major decisions and of personal spending money, even where women did the day-to-day managing of a tight budget. Managing poverty is not power.
  • Decision-making. Where one partner's career is treated as the one that matters — usually the higher-earning man's — the household relocates for his job, and hers adapts. Power operates through whose life is treated as the default.
  • The dark side. Dobash and Dobash (Violence Against Wives, 1979) interviewed women in Scottish refuges and found violence was frequently triggered by a husband's perception that his wife had challenged his authority — a wifely 'failure' to perform an expected duty. Their conclusion is structural, not individual: domestic violence is not random pathology, it is the enforcement of patriarchal authority. Marxist feminists add that violence is compounded by the stresses of exploitation and economic dependency, which make leaving hard.
  • The functionalist blind spot. None of this appears in Parsons' 'warm bath'. A family that functions perfectly for society may be a place of fear for the person inside it — which is exactly why OCR names the dark side in the specification.

KQ2 · parents and children

Childhood: constructed, extended, child-centred

  • Childhood is socially constructed. Philippe Ariès (Centuries of Childhood, 1960) argued that in medieval Europe childhood as a separate, protected status did not exist: children were treated as 'little adults' once past infancy. Modern childhood — separate, sentimentalised, protected — is a historical creation. (Later historians dispute his evidence, so present it as an argument, not a fact.)
  • The child-centred family. Smaller families, higher living standards, compulsory education and child protection law have made children the emotional and financial focus of family life. Children are, as one much-quoted formulation has it, economically worthless but emotionally priceless.
  • The extension of childhood. The transition to adulthood has stretched: longer education, later entry to secure work, later home-leaving. Youth is now a prolonged, semi-dependent stage.
  • The counter-arguments. Neil Postman (The Disappearance of Childhood, 1994) argued that television — and now the internet — destroys the information barrier that separated adults from children, so childhood is disappearing rather than extending. Sue Palmer's Toxic Childhood (2006) argues that screens, junk food and marketing are damaging children's development. Both are contested and both are useful in an essay.
  • Not all childhoods are equal. Class, gender and ethnicity shape childhood profoundly, and child poverty means the 'priceless child' is not everyone's experience.

KQ2 · the ageing population

Grandparents, parents and children

OCR asks specifically about the impact of the ageing population on the relationship between the three generations.

  • Grandparents as childcare. With both parents working and formal childcare expensive, grandparents have become a major source of routine, unpaid care — an economic contribution that is largely invisible.
  • The beanpole family (Brannen) — more generations alive at once, fewer people in each. That means more intergenerational contact but fewer siblings and cousins to spread the caring load.
  • The pivot generation. Middle-aged people — overwhelmingly women — caring simultaneously for dependent children and ageing parents. This is where the feminist analysis and the demographic analysis meet: the 'care crisis' produced by an ageing population is being absorbed, quietly and unpaid, mostly by women.
  • Power between generations. Childhood is increasingly negotiated rather than commanded; old age, meanwhile, can bring a loss of autonomy — Townsend's structured dependency. The family is where both shifts are lived out.

Exam craft

Writing the families essay

  • Trends, then explanations. Never just describe a trend. Every trend needs at least two competing explanations.
  • Run the five theories as a debate: functionalism → New Right → Marxism → feminism → postmodernism, each answering the last.
  • Use the diversity debate as your evaluative spine: Stacey and Giddens versus Chester's neo-conventional family.
  • Never forget the dark side. Any answer that treats the family only as a source of support is one-sided, and OCR names the dark side in the specification.
  • Remember what is NOT required: detailed statistics. Trends and explanations are what earn marks.
