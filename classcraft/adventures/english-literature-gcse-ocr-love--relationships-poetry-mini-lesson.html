Mission 1

Her lover sails off to get rich for her. Why is she in tears?

Make a guess: Her lover sails off to get rich for her. Why is she in tears?

Love and Relationships poetry · A SongScreen 1

Context in brief

  • Helen Maria Williams (1759–1827) published "A Song" in her Poems (1786).
  • She later moved to France and wrote Letters Written in France (1790–1796) in support of the Revolution; she also wrote against the slave trade (1788).
  • In 1787 the young Wordsworth published a sonnet about seeing her weep at a sad story.
  • Her age prized sensibility: tender feeling valued above wealth or rank. Her 1786 volume includes a poem "To Sensibility".

Love and Relationships poetry · A SongScreen 6

Form and language

  • Six quatrains in common metre (8 then 6 syllables), rhyming ABAB (store / impart / more / heart). The plain, regular song form suits a simple, sincere love.
  • Contrast: riches against love, wealth against "humble lot", all through the poem.
  • In stanza IV she turns to address him directly ("thee"), as if he could hear.
  • The "faithless waves" means untrustworthy seas, not an unfaithful lover.

?Her lover sails off to get rich for her. Why is she in tears?

She weeps because his search for wealth takes away the love she valued above all riches. The poem contrasts wealth and love throughout, and ends by setting a calm sea against "The storm is in my soul".

Mission 2

He wishes he were like a star. So why does he say 'No'?

Make a guess: He wishes he were like a star. So why does he say 'No'?

Love and Relationships poetry · Bright StarScreen 1

Context in brief

  • John Keats (1795–1821) loved Fanny Brawne, his neighbour in Hampstead; she returned his love.
  • He was ill with consumption (tuberculosis) and died in Rome in February 1821.
  • The sonnet's date is uncertain (1818 or 1819). He copied it into a volume of Shakespeare's poems, probably on his last voyage, to Italy in 1820.
  • So it was long called his "Last Sonnet", though it wasn't. It was first published in 1838.
Extract Love and Relationships poetry · Bright StarScreen 2

The whole sonnet

Love and Relationships poetry · Bright StarScreen 3

First reading

The whole sonnet is one long sentence. The speaker talks to a star (apostrophe).

  • Lines 1–8: he wishes to be "steadfast" (unchanging) like the star, but not in its way. The star hangs alone, eyes always open, like a hermit ("Eremite") watching the sea wash the shores and the snow on the hills.
  • Lines 9–14: "No—": he wants to be steadfast lying close to his love, feeling her breathe, for ever. If he can't, he would rather "swoon to death".

Love and Relationships poetry · Bright StarScreen 5

Form and structure

  • A Shakespearean sonnet: 14 lines of iambic pentameter, rhyming ABAB CDCD EFEF GG (art / night / apart / Eremite).
  • The volta (turn) comes at line 9, "No—", where a Petrarchan sonnet would turn, not before the final couplet.
  • Dashes break the single sentence into a rush of thoughts.
  • Repetition builds to the ending: "steadfast", "still" (four times: twice in line 9, twice in line 13), "for ever", "ever".

Mission checkScreen 9

?He wishes he were like a star. So why does he say 'No'?

"No" rejects the star's way of being steadfast, not steadfastness itself. The star is constant but alone and distant; Keats wants to be just as constant while close to the person he loves, or else to "swoon to death".

Mission 3

Can one moment matter more than a whole life?

Make a guess: Can one moment matter more than a whole life?

Love and Relationships poetry · NowScreen 1

Context in brief

  • Robert Browning (1812–1889) is famous for dramatic monologues: poems spoken by a character.
  • He married the poet Elizabeth Barrett in 1846; she died in 1861.
  • "Now" is from Asolando (1889), his last collection, published on 12 December 1889, the day he died in Venice.
  • Treat the "I" as the poem's speaker, not simply Browning.
Extract Love and Relationships poetry · NowScreen 2

The whole poem

Love and Relationships poetry · NowScreen 3

First reading

The speaker talks to his lover ("you").

  • Lines 1–6: give me just one moment of your life; forget the past and future and put everything, "Thought and feeling and soul and sense", into it.
  • Lines 7–10: that moment gives him what he wants: to be sure, "for once", that she loves him.
  • Lines 11–14: how long can it last? Only a moment, but an "eternal" one, as they kiss.

Love and Relationships poetry · NowScreen 5

Form and structure

  • 14 lines like a sonnet, but with irregular rhyme (moment / before / ignore / condense / endowment…) and no neat quatrains.
  • Long lines in a fast triple rhythm: ALL of your | LIFE that has | GONE be-FORE.
  • Dashes and exclamations make it breathless: one rush of thought, like the "rapture" it asks for.
  • The last line is a list of four short, parallel actions (cheeks, arms, eyes, lips), speeding to the kiss.

Mission checkScreen 9

?Can one moment matter more than a whole life?

For the speaker, yes. He asks his lover to put everything into one moment, and calls it "The moment eternal". He knows it is "just that and no more", and the paradox lets the poem hold both truths at once.

Mission 4

Love is a rose, friendship is holly. Why pick the holly?

Make a guess: Love is a rose, friendship is holly. Why pick the holly?

Love and Relationships · "Love and Friendship"Screen 1

The poet and the poem

  • Emily Brontë (1818–1848), author of Wuthering Heights, published poems as "Ellis Bell".
  • "Love and Friendship" was first printed in 1850, after her death, in a selection of her poems edited by her sister Charlotte.
  • The poem weighs romantic love against friendship and asks which lasts.
  • Your anthology's punctuation differs a little from the 1850 text used here; the words are the same.

Love and Relationships · "Love and Friendship"Screen 5

Form: questions, then an answer

  • Three quatrains (four-line stanzas), four beats to a line.
  • Lines 1 and 2 are built the same way (parallelism), setting love and friendship side by side.
  • Stanzas 1 and 2 end with rhetorical questions; stanza 3 answers with imperatives: "scorn", "deck thee".
  • The rhyme tightens: stanzas 1–2 rhyme only lines 2 and 4, but stanza 3 rhymes every line (now / brow, sheen / green). The ending sounds settled, like the holly.

Mission checkScreen 9

?Love is a rose, friendship is holly. Why pick the holly?

The speaker chooses the holly because it blooms "most constantly". Romantic love is sweet in spring but no one calls it "fair" in winter, while friendship stays green when "December blights thy brow". Because the seasons stand for time, the poem is advice about what will last a lifetime.

Poetry skills · comparing poemsScreen 1

One argument, two poems

  • A comparison is one argument about both poems, not two essays joined by "on the other hand".
  • Compare what the poems say (ideas, attitudes) and how they say it (language, form, structure).
  • Find a link first, then the differences within it.
  • Keep both poems in play in each paragraph, using whereas, similarly, in contrast, both.

Recap

Big ideas

  • "A Song" (1786) is spoken by a woman whose poor but loving partner has gone to sea "in search of gain".
  • Common metre and ABAB rhyme give a plain song form; the poem is built on the contrast of love and wealth, shaped by the age's value of sensibility.
  • The final metaphor puts the storm inside her while the sea stays calm.
  • Probably written in 1818–19 and first published in 1838, "Bright Star" was long thought to be Keats's last poem.
  • A Shakespearean sonnet with the turn at line 9: the star's lonely constancy is rejected, its steadfastness kept.
  • Oxymoron ("sweet unrest") and repetition ("still, still", "for ever") present love as both peace and longing.
  • "Now" (Asolando, 1889) asks a lover to give "but a moment" and shut out past and future.
  • Fourteen lines like a sonnet, but irregular, breathless and fast, ending on a list of actions.
  • The paradox "The moment eternal" is the key: one intense moment can feel outside time.
  • One comparison runs through the whole poem: love is the wild rose-briar (beautiful, brief), friendship the holly (plain, evergreen).
  • The seasons stand for time; the structure moves from two rhetorical questions to the imperative answer of stanza 3, where the rhyme becomes fullest.
  • Useful for part (b) on lasting love, and as a model for comparing how an unseen poem uses nature imagery.
  • A comparison is one argument about both poems: find a link, then explore the differences within it.
  • Compare ideas and methods together (voice, metre, rhyme, imagery), using comparative connectives in every paragraph.
  • Your exam pairs a named poem with a second poem (one you choose, or an unseen one): know which, and write about both.

Key quotations to learn

  • He gave a boon I valued more— / He gave me all his heart! (Stanza I) Love valued above riches.
  • But now for me, in search of gain (Stanza III) The turn: he leaves for wealth, "for me".
  • When love is all I prize? (Stanza III) Her values in one line.
  • That simple fare, that humble lot, / Were more than wealth to me. (Stanza IV) Humble love over wealth.
  • The storm is in my soul. (Stanza VI) Metaphor of inner grief against a calm sea.
  • Bright Star, would I were steadfast as thou art— (Line 1) Apostrophe; the wish to be constant.
  • Not in lone splendour hung aloft the night, (Line 2) What he rejects: lonely distance.
  • No—yet still steadfast, still unchangeable, (Line 9) The volta.
  • Awake for ever in a sweet unrest, (Line 12) Oxymoron: love as calm and longing.
  • And so live ever—or else swoon to death. (Line 14) Love or death: the final choice.
  • Out of your whole life give but a moment! (Line 1) The demand that opens the poem.
  • Thought and feeling and soul and sense— (Line 6) Everything condensed into the moment.
  • This tick of our life-time's one moment you love me! (Line 10) Certainty of love, in the briefest time.
  • How long such suspension may linger? (Line 11) He admits the moment can't last.
  • The moment eternal—just that and no more— (Line 12) Paradox at the heart of the poem.
  • While cheeks burn, arms open, eyes shut and lips meet! (Line 14) A rushed list racing to the kiss.
  • "Love is like the wild rose-briar;" (stanza 1, line 1) the simile that starts the whole comparison
  • "Friendship like the holly-tree." (stanza 1, line 2) parallel line: friendship set against love
  • "But which will bloom most constantly?" (stanza 1, line 4) the poem's real question: what lasts?
  • "And who will call the wild-briar fair?" (stanza 2, line 4) love's beauty fades with winter
  • "Then, scorn the silly rose-wreath now," (stanza 3, line 1) imperative advice; "silly" dismisses love
  • "That, when December blights thy brow, / He still may leave thy garland green." (stanza 3, lines 3–4) personified winter; friendship survives hard times
