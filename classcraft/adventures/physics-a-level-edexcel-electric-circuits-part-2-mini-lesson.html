Mission 1

Mission 1: Stretch a metal wire to twice its length. What happens to its resistance?

Make a guess: Stretch a metal wire to twice its length. What happens to its resistance?

Mission 1

Where resistance comes from (recap)

  • A metal is a lattice of positive ions in a sea of free electrons.
  • A potential difference makes the electrons drift. They keep colliding with the vibrating ions.
  • Each collision passes energy to the ions: they vibrate more, so the metal heats up.
  • More collisions per metre → more resistance.
Mission 1

Resistivity

  • Longer wire → more collisions in the way: R ∝ length.
  • Thicker wire → more paths side by side: R ∝ 1/A.
  • R = ρl/A.
  • Resistivity ρ depends only on the material (and its temperature), not on its shape.
  • Unit: Ω m.
  • Copper: 1.7 × 10⁻⁸ Ω m. Constantan: 4.9 × 10⁻⁷ Ω m.
Quick check
?A uniform copper wire is cut into two pieces of equal length. How do the resistance and the resistivity of one piece compare with those of the original wire?
Mission 1

Worked example

  • Constantan wire: length 0.80 m, diameter 0.30 mm, ρ = 4.9 × 10⁻⁷ Ω m. Find R.
  • Radius = 0.15 mm = 1.5 × 10⁻⁴ m. Halve the diameter first.
  • A = πr² = π × (1.5 × 10⁻⁴)² = 7.07 × 10⁻⁸ m².
  • R = ρ × length ÷ A = (4.9 × 10⁻⁷ × 0.80) ÷ (7.07 × 10⁻⁸) = 5.5 Ω.
  • Trap: 1 mm² = 10⁻⁶ m², not 10⁻³ m².
  • Stretch a wire to twice the length: its volume is unchanged, so A halves. The resistance becomes 2 ÷ ½ = 4 times as large.
Quick check
?A copper cable has length 5.0 m and diameter 1.0 mm. The resistivity of copper is 1.7 × 10⁻⁸ Ω m. What is the resistance of the cable?
Mission 1

Resistance against length

  • Same wire, different lengths: R against length is a straight line through the origin.
  • Gradient = R ÷ length = ρ/A.
  • So ρ = gradient × A.
  • Unit check: Ω m⁻¹ × m² = Ω m.
Quick check
?A copper wire is connected across an ideal battery. The wire is then cooled from 60 °C to 20 °C; its length and cross-sectional area change by negligible amounts. How do the wire's resistance and the current in it change as it cools?
Mission check

?Wire X has resistance R and is made of a material of resistivity ρ. Wire Y is made of the same material at the same temperature, but it is half as long as X and has half the diameter of X. What are the resistance and the resistivity of wire Y?

Stretching a wire to twice its length keeps its volume, so its area halves. R = ρ(2L)/(A/2) = 4 × the resistance, with the same resistivity.

Mission 2

Mission 2: A kettle's element gets very hot, yet the cable carrying the same current stays cool. Why?

Make a guess: A kettle's element gets very hot, yet the cable carrying the same current stays cool. Why?

Mission 2

Power: energy per second

  • Power = rate of energy transfer. 1 W = 1 J s⁻¹.
  • pd = joules per coulomb. Current = coulombs per second.
  • Multiply: joules per second.
  • P = IV.
Mission 2

Three forms of one law

  • Use V = IR: P = IV = I²R = V²/R.
  • Same current (in series): use I²R. Larger R → more power.
  • Same pd (in parallel): use V²/R. Smaller R → more power.
Quick check
?The potential difference across an ohmic resistor is increased so that the current in the resistor becomes three times as large. What is the ratio of the new power dissipated in the resistor, P2, to the original power, P1?
Mission 2

Energy transferred

  • W = VIt.
  • A heater on 12 V takes 3.0 A for 5.0 minutes.
  • P = 12 × 3.0 = 36 W. t = 5.0 × 60 = 300 s.
  • Energy = 36 × 300 = 1.1 × 10⁴ J.
  • Check: R = 12 ÷ 3.0 = 4.0 Ω; I²R = 3.0² × 4.0 = 36 W.
Quick check
?A steady current of 0.25 A flows in a 24 Ω resistor for 2.0 minutes. How much energy is dissipated in the resistor?
Mission 2

The heating effect

  • Electrons collide with the lattice ions and pass on energy. The ions vibrate more: the resistor heats up.
  • Kettle element and its cable are in series: the same current.
  • P = I²R: the element's resistance is far larger, so nearly all the power is dissipated there.
  • The cable's resistance is tiny: very little heating.
  • Wanted heating: kettles, heaters. Unwanted: cables, chargers.
Quick check
?A 60 W lamp and a 100 W lamp are both designed to work on the same 230 V supply. Which lamp has the larger resistance when working normally, and why?
Mission check

?A 60 W lamp and a 100 W lamp are both rated for a 230 V supply. They are connected in series across a 230 V supply. Assume each keeps its normal working resistance. Which lamp is brighter?

Same potential difference: the smaller resistance takes more power. Same current, as in the kettle and its cable: the larger resistance takes more power (P = I²R).

Mission 3

Mission 3: A ruler, a micrometer, an ammeter and a voltmeter. How can they tell you what a wire is made of?

Make a guess: A ruler, a micrometer, an ammeter and a voltmeter. How can they tell you what a wire is made of?

Mission 3

Measuring resistivity

  • Wire taped along a metre rule. A sliding crocodile clip sets the length.
  • Micrometer: measure the diameter at several points, turning the wire each time. Use the mean d.
  • A = πd²/4.
  • Ammeter in series, voltmeter across that length: R = V/I.
  • Repeat for at least six lengths.
Mission 3

From readings to ρ

  • Plot R against length. Gradient = ρ/A, so ρ = gradient × A.
  • Contact resistance at the clips shifts the line up. It does not change the gradient.
  • Keep the current small and switch off between readings: a warm wire has a larger ρ.
  • Check the micrometer for zero error.
  • Example: gradient 4.3 Ω m⁻¹, d = 0.38 mm → A = 1.13 × 10⁻⁷ m² → ρ = 4.9 × 10⁻⁷ Ω m.
Quick check
?A student plots R against length for a uniform wire of diameter 0.40 mm. The best-fit line has a gradient of 4.0 Ω m⁻¹ and crosses the R axis at 0.30 Ω. What is the resistivity of the wire?
Quick check
?A wire has a resistance of 4.0 Ω per metre, and the clips add a constant 0.50 Ω to every reading. A student finds the resistivity from a single reading at a length of 0.50 m, rather than from the gradient of a graph. How does her value compare with the true value?
Mission check

?An unlabelled wire has a mean diameter of 0.32 mm. Its resistance between the clips is 1.72 Ω at a length of 0.20 m and 5.37 Ω at 0.80 m. Resistivities: copper 1.7 × 10⁻⁸ Ω m; iron 1.0 × 10⁻⁷ Ω m; constantan 4.9 × 10⁻⁷ Ω m; nichrome 1.1 × 10⁻⁶ Ω m. Which metal is the wire most likely made of?

Resistance, length and diameter give the resistivity, a property of the material, not of the wire. Use the gradient of R against length, so contact resistance drops out, then match ρ to a table.

Mission 4

Mission 4: A street lamp switches itself on at dusk. How does its circuit sense that it is getting dark?

Make a guess: A street lamp switches itself on at dusk. How does its circuit sense that it is getting dark?

Mission 4

Sharing a pd

  • Two resistors in series across a supply Vin carry the same current.
  • So the pd splits in proportion to resistance. Bigger R, bigger share.
  • Vout = R2/(R1 + R2) × Vin, with Vout taken across R2.
  • V1/V2 = R1/R2.
Mission 4

Worked example

  • 12 V supply, negligible internal resistance. R1 = 2.2 kΩ, R2 = 3.3 kΩ. Find the pd across R2.
  • Vout = 3.3/(2.2 + 3.3) × 12 = 7.2 V.
  • The pd across R1 is 12 − 7.2 = 4.8 V. Ratio 4.8 : 7.2 = 2.2 : 3.3.
Quick check
?An LDR and a 300 Ω resistor are in series across a 6.0 V supply of negligible internal resistance. In the room's light the LDR's resistance is 900 Ω. What does an ideal voltmeter across the 300 Ω resistor read?
Mission 4

Potentiometer

  • A resistance track across the supply, with a sliding contact.
  • Output between one end and the slider: any pd from 0 to Vin.
  • Uniform track: pd ∝ length from that end.
  • Along any uniform wire carrying a current, the potential falls linearly with distance.
Quick check
?A potentiometer has a uniform resistance track connected across a 9.0 V dc supply of negligible internal resistance. An ideal voltmeter is connected between one end of the track and the sliding contact, which is one third of the way along the track from that end. What does the voltmeter read?
Mission 4

Sensors in a divider

  • NTC thermistor: R falls as temperature rises.
  • LDR: R falls as light gets brighter.
  • Put one in a divider: when its R changes, its share of the pd changes.
  • A component's pd rises when its share of the total R rises.
Mission 4

Control circuits

  • Dusk lamp: Vout across the LDR. Darker → LDR's R up → Vout up → switches the lamp on.
  • Heating control: Vout across the thermistor. Colder → R up → Vout up → switches the heater on.
  • Make the fixed resistor variable to set the switching point.
  • Vout goes to a switching circuit, not straight to the lamp or heater.
Quick check
?An LDR and a 10 kΩ fixed resistor are in series across a 6.0 V supply of negligible internal resistance. An ideal voltmeter is connected across the fixed resistor. The LDR's resistance is 50 kΩ in the dark and 1.0 kΩ in bright light. What does the voltmeter read in each case?
Mission 4

Calibrating a thermistor

  • CP12: thermistor and fixed resistor as a divider; thermistor in a water bath with a thermometer.
  • Heat the water slowly and stir. Record Vout at each temperature.
  • Plot Vout against θ: the calibration curve.
  • Then a Vout reading gives the temperature: a thermostat can switch at a set Vout.
Mission check

?An NTC thermistor and a fixed resistor are in series across a supply of constant pd. An ideal voltmeter is connected across the thermistor. The thermistor is warmed. What happens to the voltmeter reading?

Same idea at dusk: the LDR's resistance rises in the dark, so the pd across it rises, and that switches the street lamp on.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Why do a car's headlights dim while its starter motor is turning?

Make a guess: Why do a car's headlights dim while its starter motor is turning?

Mission 5

emf and internal resistance

  • emf: energy transferred to each coulomb of charge by the source.
  • A real cell has internal resistance r. Model: an ideal cell in series with r.
  • With a current, some energy per coulomb is dissipated inside the cell. That pd is the lost volts, Ir.
  • Terminal pd = emf − lost volts.
Mission 5

Terminal potential difference

  • ε = I(R + r). Terminal pd: V = ε − Ir (= IR).
  • No current: terminal pd = emf.
Quick check
?A battery with an internal resistance of 0.50 Ω drives a current of 0.60 A through a lamp, and an ideal voltmeter across the battery's terminals reads 4.2 V. The lamp is then disconnected, so that only the voltmeter remains connected across the battery. What does the voltmeter now read?
Mission 5

Worked example

  • Cell: emf 1.5 V, internal resistance 0.50 Ω. External resistor: 2.5 Ω.
  • Current = emf ÷ total resistance = 1.5 ÷ (2.5 + 0.50) = 0.50 A.
  • Lost volts = Ir = 0.50 × 0.50 = 0.25 V.
  • Terminal pd = 1.5 − 0.25 = 1.25 V.
  • Check: IR = 0.50 × 2.5 = 1.25 V.
Quick check
?A battery of emf 9.0 V and internal resistance 0.50 Ω is connected to a 4.0 Ω resistor. What is the terminal potential difference of the battery?
Mission 5

Consequences

  • More current → more lost volts → lower terminal pd.
  • A starter motor draws a very large current. The battery's terminal pd drops, so the headlights dim.
  • Short circuit (R = 0): current = emf ÷ r. Small r → a very large current; the cell heats up.
  • Car batteries need a small r to deliver large currents. High-voltage lab supplies have a large r so the current stays safe.
Quick check
?Cells P and Q have the same emf. P has a smaller internal resistance than Q. Each cell is connected in turn to the same lamp. Which statement is correct?
Mission 5

Measuring emf and r

  • Cell, variable resistor and ammeter in series. Voltmeter across the cell's terminals.
  • Change the resistance: record terminal pd V and current I each time.
  • V = −rI + emf: plot V against I. Straight line.
  • y-intercept = emf. Gradient = −r.
  • Switch off between readings: a warm cell's r changes.
  • This is core practical 3.
Quick check
?The graph shows how the terminal pd V of a cell varies with the current I that it delivers. What is the internal resistance of the cell?
Mission check

?A battery of internal resistance r is connected to an external resistor whose resistance equals r. The external resistor is then replaced by one of resistance 2r. How does the potential difference across the terminals of the battery change?

Larger external resistance → smaller current → smaller Ir inside the battery → higher terminal potential difference. The starter motor is the reverse: tiny resistance, huge current, so the terminal potential difference drops and the headlights dim.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • R = ρl/A: resistivity is set by the material and temperature; length and area set the resistance.
  • Hotter metal: ions vibrate more, more collisions, more resistance.

Mission 2

  • P = IV = I²R = V²/R; energy = IVt. Same current: larger R, more power. Same pd: smaller R, more power.

Mission 3

  • Measure ρ: mean diameter by micrometer, R against length, ρ = gradient × A.

Mission 4

  • Vout = R2/(R1 + R2) × Vin: series resistors share the pd in proportion to resistance.
  • Thermistor or LDR in a divider: its share of the pd changes with temperature or light.

Mission 5

  • ε = I(R + r); terminal pd V = ε − Ir.
  • Graph of V against I: intercept = emf, gradient = −r.
