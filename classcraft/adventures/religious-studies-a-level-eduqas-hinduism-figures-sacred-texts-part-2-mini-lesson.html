Mission 1

Heard and remembered

  • Shruti ("that which is heard"): texts Hindus believe were heard or seen by ancient sages (rishis) in deep meditation, not composed by them.
  • Shruti = the Vedas, each with its hymns, ritual texts and Upanishads.
  • Smriti ("that which is remembered"): texts composed and handed down by humans: the epics (Ramayana, Mahabharata, including the Bhagavad Gita), the Puranas, and law books such as the Manusmriti.
  • Many Hindus believe shruti is eternal and without a human author (apaurusheya).
Mission 1

Which has more authority?

  • Traditionally, shruti has the highest authority: it is revealed, timeless knowledge.
  • Smriti is authoritative because it explains and applies shruti.
  • Classical rule (Mimamsa school): if a smriti text conflicts with shruti, shruti wins.
  • Manusmriti 2.6 (paraphrase) lists the sources of dharma in order: the Veda, then smriti, then the conduct of good people, then one's own conscience.
  • Hindu schools that accept Vedic authority are called astika ("orthodox"); Buddhists and Jains, who reject it, are nastika.
Mission 1

The Vedas in worship

  • The Vedas are mostly heard in ritual, not read: priests recite them in Sanskrit with exact sound and accent.
  • Samskaras (life-cycle rites): Vedic mantras are chanted at the sacred-thread ceremony, weddings and funerals.
  • The Gayatri mantra (Rig Veda 3.62.10) is recited daily by many Hindus.
  • Havan and temple rites use Vedic verses alongside later ritual texts.
  • Traditionally only "twice-born" men learned the Vedas; reform movements such as the Arya Samaj teach them to everyone, including women.

Mission 1

The great smriti texts

  • Ramayana (attributed to Valmiki): Prince Rama's exile, the rescue of Sita and his rule. Rama is the ideal of dharma.
  • Mahabharata (attributed to Vyasa): the war between cousins, the Pandavas and Kauravas; duty in impossible situations.
  • Bhagavad Gita: Krishna's teaching to the warrior Arjuna, within the Mahabharata. Often treated almost as shruti.
  • Puranas: stories of the gods and avatars; the Manusmriti: rules for varna, stage of life, family and kingship.
  • Smriti is known and loved: through festivals, storytelling, temple art, drama and television.
  • Ramayana: righteous behaviour, selfless caring, courage, friendship, devotion. Mahabharata: doing one's duty. Priests, gurus and families (the community of believers) interpret and apply these texts.

The Gita on the Vedas

Bhagavad Gita 2:46–47 (Edwin Arnold's verse translation, 1885):

> "Look! like as when a tank pours water forth

> To suit all needs, so do these Brahmans draw

> Text for all wants from tank of Holy Writ.

> But thou, want not! ask not! Find full reward

> Of doing right in right! Let right deeds be

> Thy motive, not the fruit which comes from them."

  • Arnold translates freely. Most translations of 2:46 read: for one who knows, the Vedas are as much use as a well in a flood. The criticism of using the Vedas for rewards is in 2:42–45.

Q1 In Arnold's version, what do some Brahmins do with the Vedas ("Holy Writ")?

Show answer

Draw texts from them for every wish, using ritual to gain rewards.

Q2 What does he ask of Arjuna instead?

Show answer

To act rightly for its own sake, not for the fruits.

  • So a smriti text reinterprets how to use shruti: one reason the Gita is so influential.

Mission 1Evaluate

Does smriti matter more than shruti?

  • Claim: For Hindus today, smriti is more important than shruti.
  • For: The epics and Gita are widely known among Hindus; they shape festivals (Diwali, Ramnavami), ethics and devotion.
  • For: Stories teach dharma in situations people recognise.
  • Against: Shruti defines what counts as Hindu (astika) and is present in every samskara.
  • Against: Smriti's own authority rests on agreeing with shruti; the Gita itself is called an Upanishad by tradition.
  • Judgement criteria: "Important" as authority or as influence? For which Hindus (priests, Vedanta scholars, devotees, reformers)?

Mission 2

Smriti in story form

  • Much Hindu smriti is narrative:
  • Itihasa ("so it was"): the two great epics, the Ramayana and the Mahabharata.
  • Puranas ("ancient lore"): stories of creation, the gods and the avatars.
  • They are known through recitation, festivals, temple carvings, dance and drama, comics, film and television (Ramanand Sagar's Ramayan, 1987–88; B. R. Chopra's Mahabharat, 1988–90).
  • Their aim: to show dharma (right conduct and duty) in action.
Mission 2

The Ramayana: the story

  • Attributed to the sage Valmiki: about 24,000 verses in seven books; composed over several centuries before c. 200 CE.
  • Prince Rama of Ayodhya is exiled for 14 years because of a promise his father made to a queen. His wife Sita and brother Lakshmana go with him.
  • The demon-king Ravana abducts Sita to Lanka. With the monkey Hanuman and an army, Rama defeats Ravana and rescues her.
  • Rama returns to rule Ayodhya: Rama-rajya, the ideal kingdom. Many Hindus light lamps at Diwali to welcome him home.
  • Many versions: Tulsidas's Hindi Ramcharitmanas (16th century) is the best known in north India; Tamil, Thai and other tellings differ.
Mission 2

What the Ramayana teaches

  • Rama: the ideal man (maryada purushottama), keeping dharma as son, brother, husband and king. Vaishnavas worship him as an avatar of Vishnu.
  • Sita: faithfulness, courage and strength in captivity; for many Hindus the ideal wife.
  • Lakshmana: loyalty and selfless caring for his brother.
  • Hanuman: devotion (bhakti), courage and service; a model of friendship with Rama.
  • Debates: Sita's trial by fire and later banishment are criticised by many, including feminist readers; some versions retell or omit them.
  • Together they teach righteous behaviour, selfless caring, courage, friendship and devotion.

Mission 2

The Mahabharata

  • Attributed to the sage Vyasa: about 100,000 verses in 18 books, among the longest poems in the world. It calls itself a "fifth Veda".
  • Two sets of cousins: the five Pandavas and the hundred Kauravas, rivals for one kingdom.
  • The Pandavas lose everything in a rigged game of dice; their wife Draupadi is humiliated. After 13 years of exile, war follows at Kurukshetra (18 days).
  • Krishna guides the Pandavas; before the battle he teaches Arjuna: the Bhagavad Gita (book 6).
  • Key theme: doing one's duty (svadharma) when duties clash; "dharma is subtle". Good people make hard choices; victory brings grief.
  • Its central ethical lesson: the importance of doing one's duty.

Why the avatar comes

Bhagavad Gita 4:7–8 (Edwin Arnold's verse translation, 1885), Krishna speaks:

> "When Righteousness

> Declines, O Bharata! when Wickedness

> Is strong, I rise, from age to age, and take

> Visible shape, and move a man with men,

> Succouring the good, thrusting the evil back,

> And setting Virtue on her seat again."

Q1 When does the avatar come?

Show answer

Whenever righteousness, dharma, declines and wickedness grows strong.

Q2 What three things does the avatar do?

Show answer

Protects the good, pushes back evil, re-establishes dharma.

Mission 2Evaluate

Stories or rules?

  • Claim: The epics and Puranas are a better guide to dharma than law books such as the Manusmriti.
  • For: They are widely known among Hindus; they show dharma in real dilemmas; they inspire devotion.
  • For: Their many versions allow adaptation to new times.
  • Against: Stories can be ambiguous: the same episode can justify opposite actions.
  • Against: Some episodes reflect ancient attitudes to women and caste that many Hindus now question; rules can be reformed more openly.
  • Judgement criteria: Is the aim to know the rule, or to become a good person? Who interprets the story (gurus, families, the community of believers)?

Mission 3

Sitting near the teacher

  • Upanishad is often explained as "sitting down near" a teacher to receive hidden teaching.
  • The final part of the Vedas (shruti), so also called Vedanta, "end of the Veda".
  • The oldest (Brihadaranyaka, Chandogya) date from c. 700–500 BCE; others are later. Tradition counts 108; about 13 are "principal".
  • Setting: teachers (gurus) with pupils, often in forest retreats. Teachers include Yajnavalkya and Uddalaka; women such as Gargi and Maitreyi question and debate.
  • A shift: from performing outer sacrifice to gaining inner knowledge (jnana) of reality.
Mission 3

Nachiketas and Yama

  • Katha Upanishad: Vajashravas gives away worn-out cows at a great sacrifice (yajna). His son Nachiketas asks to whom he himself will be given; in anger his father replies that he gives him to Death.
  • Nachiketas waits three days at the house of Yama, god of death. As amends Yama grants three boons.
  • (1) His father's peace of mind. (2) Knowledge of the fire-sacrifice that leads to heaven. (3) What happens to a person after death.
  • Yama offers wealth, long life and pleasures instead; Nachiketas refuses, because such things are fleeting.
  • Yama's answer to the third boon: "This Self is never born, nor does It die. ... It is not slain even though the body is slain." (Katha 2:18, Paramananda)
  • Yajna: the story moves from ritual (the second boon) to knowledge of the self (the third), which ritual cannot give.
Mission 3

Brahman and atman

  • Brahman: the ultimate reality, the ground of everything, beyond all names and forms.
  • Atman: the true self in every living being, beneath body, senses and mind.
  • The Upanishads' central teaching: atman is Brahman, or at least inseparable from it. To know this is liberation (moksha).

> "He who has not turned away from evil conduct, whose senses are uncontrolled, who is not tranquil, whose mind is not at rest, he can never attain this Atman even by knowledge." (Katha 2:24)

Salt in water

Chandogya Upanishad 6:13:1–3 (paraphrase): The teacher Uddalaka tells his son Shvetaketu to put salt in water overnight. In the morning the salt cannot be seen, yet every sip tastes salty. "In the same way," says Uddalaka, "you do not see the Real here, but it is here. That finest essence is the self of all this world. That is the Real. That is the atman. Mission 3

Jnana and the Vedanta schools

  • Jnana (knowledge) is realisation, not information: "This Self cannot be attained by study of the Scriptures, nor by intellectual perception" (Katha 2:23, Paramananda).
  • The path: a guru, ethical life, control of the senses, meditation (e.g. on Om).
  • The Upanishads begin the philosophical traditions of India, especially Vedanta. Its schools read the same texts differently:
  • Advaita (Shankara, c. 8th century): atman and Brahman are identical; the world of difference is maya.
  • Vishishtadvaita (Ramanuja, 11th–12th century): selves are real and distinct, yet part of Brahman, a personal God.
  • Dvaita (Madhva, 13th century): God and selves are eternally different.

Mission 3Evaluate

How important are the Upanishads today?

  • Claim: The Upanishads are the most important texts for Hindus today.
  • For: They are shruti, the source of Vedanta and of key ideas (atman, Brahman, karma, moksha) that most Hindus share.
  • For: Neo-Vedanta reformers (Vivekananda) made them central to modern and global Hinduism.
  • Against: Many Hindus learn through smriti: the epics, the Gita and Puranas; fewer read the Upanishads.
  • Against: Bhakti traditions value love of a personal God over the Upanishads' path of knowledge.
  • Judgement criteria: Importance for thought, or for daily practice? For which Hindus?
  • Further issue: the relative importance of the Katha Upanishad within the Upanishads (its story form, its ethics, and its verses echoed in the Bhagavad Gita).

Mission 4

Atman: the self that is not the body

  • Atman: in Hindu belief, the real, eternal self in every living being.
  • Hindus hold that it is not the body, the mind or the personality: these change; the atman does not.
  • The body is like clothing:

> "Nay, but as when one layeth / His worn-out robes away, / And taking new ones, sayeth, / 'These will I wear to-day!' / So putteth by the spirit / Lightly its garb of flesh" (Gita 2:22, Arnold)

> "Never the spirit was born; the spirit shall cease to be never" (Gita 2:20, Arnold)

  • The atman wearing a body and caught in rebirth is called the jiva or jivatman (embodied self).
Mission 4Read the source

The chariot (Katha Upanishad 3)

Yama, the god of death, teaches the boy Nachiketas (Paramananda translation, 1919):

> "Know the Atman (Self) as the lord of the chariot, and the body as the chariot. Know also the intellect to be the driver and mind the reins." (Katha 3:3)

  • Katha 3:4 (paraphrase): the senses are the horses; the objects of the senses are the roads.

> "He who does not possess discrimination, whose mind is uncontrolled and always impure, he does not reach that goal, but falls again into Samsara" (Katha 3:7)

Q1 Who drives the chariot, and for whom?

Show answer

The intellect drives, holding the reins of the mind; the atman is the lord of the chariot, who rides.

Q2 What happens if the horses run wild?

Show answer

The journey fails: the person is reborn in samsara, 3:7.

Mission 4

Brahman: the reality behind everything

  • Brahman: ultimate reality, the ground of all that exists.
  • Sat-chit-ananda: being, consciousness, bliss. Not three parts: three ways of naming one reality.
  • Neti neti, "not this, not this" (Brihadaranyaka Upanishad): nothing we can point to is Brahman; every description falls short.
  • Brahman as macrocosm (the universal spirit); atman as microcosm (the same spirit in the individual).
Mission 4Read the source

"You are that" (Chandogya VI)

Uddalaka teaches his son Shvetaketu (Chandogya Upanishad VI.11–14, paraphrase):

  • VI.11 A great tree lives as long as the life (jiva) runs through it; when life leaves, the tree dies, but the life itself does not die.
  • VI.12 Break open a tiny fig seed: inside, nothing can be seen. From that unseen subtle essence the great tree grows.
  • VI.13 Salt dissolved in water is unseen, yet every sip tastes salty. So the Real is here though you do not see it.
  • VI.14 A man led blindfold far from home finds his way back by asking; one who has a teacher comes home to the Real.
  • Each teaching ends: that subtle essence is the self of all; that is the Real; that is the atman; you are that (tat tvam asi), Shvetaketu.

Q1 What do the salt and the seed have in common?

Show answer

Each is present but unseen, as Brahman is present in all things.

Q2 What does VI.14 add?

Show answer

You need a teacher, a guru, to realise the truth.

  • Your spec's reference: Chandogya Upanishad 6:13:1–3 (the salt).

  • Paramatman: the supreme self, God, dwelling in every heart as inner controller.

  • Mundaka Upanishad 3.1.1 (paraphrase): two birds on one tree; one eats the fruit, the other watches without eating.
  • The big question: are the two really one, or really two?

Mission 4Diversity

Three Vedanta answers

  • Advaita (non-dual), Shankara (c. 788–820 by tradition): atman is Brahman. The sense of being a separate jiva is due to avidya (ignorance) and maya. Tat tvam asi means strict identity.
  • Vishishtadvaita (qualified non-dual), Ramanuja (trad. 1017–1137): souls are real and distinct, but are the body of Brahman, as a part to its whole. Tat tvam asi: Brahman is the inner self of "you", as a soul is of its body.
  • Dvaita (dual), Madhva (1238–1317): God (Vishnu) and souls are eternally different. Souls depend wholly on God. Madhva reads the text as atat tvam asi, "you are not that".
  • All three are Vedanta: they read the same Upanishads, Gita and Brahma Sutras.
  • Your spec names Shankara (Advaita, an impersonal view of the divine) and Madhva (Dvaita).
Mission 4

Monist or monotheist?

  • Monist: ultimately only one reality; God and the soul are one (Advaita).
  • Monotheist: one God, and souls are different from God (Dvaita; also, in different ways, many Vaishnava bhakti traditions).
  • Vishishtadvaita sits between: one reality, with real internal distinctions.
  • Not "monism good, monotheism bad": each claims the Upanishads, and each shapes worship differently.
  • Spec issue: whether it is possible to worship an impersonal God (worship of saguna Brahman as a step, for Advaita).

Mission 4Evaluate

Is the atman identical with Brahman?

  • Claim: Atman is Brahman: the self is God, not a separate soul.
  • For: "You are that" and "this self ... is Brahman" (Shandilya Vidya) read most naturally as identity.
  • For: If Brahman is infinite and non-dual, nothing can stand outside it, so no second self.
  • Against: Experience: I suffer and do not know everything; Brahman does not suffer. Ramanuja: identity cannot explain this.
  • Against: Bhakti needs two: love and worship require a lover and a beloved (Madhva; much popular practice).
  • Judgement criteria: Which reading fits the most texts? Which explains experience of ignorance and suffering? Which supports the way Hindus actually live and worship?
  • Spec issue: the nature of the relationship between Brahman and atman.
Mission 4Worked answer

Linking belief, source, development

Task: explain how tat tvam asi helps Hindus understand the self.

  • Belief: the true self is not the body or mind but atman.
  • Source: Uddalaka's salt teaching ends "you are that" (Chandogya VI.13, paraphrase).
  • Development: for Shankara this is strict identity: atman is Brahman, and separateness is avidya; for Madhva the text teaches difference ("you are not that").
  • Effect on life: an Advaitin seeks knowledge (jnana) to remove ignorance; a Dvaitin seeks devotion and God's grace.

Mission 5

Ramakrishna: a priest of the Mother

  • Sri Ramakrishna (1836–1886), born Gadadhar Chattopadhyay in a Bengal village; little schooling, no English.
  • From 1856, priest at the Dakshineswar Kali temple near Calcutta.
  • He longed to see the Goddess Kali as his Divine Mother; his disciples record that he had overwhelming visions of her.
  • He then followed many disciplines, guided by different teachers: Tantric practice; Vaishnava devotional moods; and Advaita, under the monk Totapuri, reaching nirvikalpa samadhi (absorption without any sense of difference).
  • His wife Sarada Devi (1853–1920) became his disciple and is revered as the Holy Mother.
  • His sayings were recorded by a disciple, "M" (Mahendranath Gupta), in the Kathamrita (The Gospel of Sri Ramakrishna).
Mission 5

As many faiths, so many paths

  • According to his disciples, Ramakrishna also practised Islam (1866) and Christianity (1874) for short periods, and reported experiences of God through each.
  • His conclusion (paraphrase): "As many faiths, so many paths": each religion, sincerely followed, leads to the same God.
  • His image (paraphrase): one water is called jal, pani and "water" by different people; so one God has many names.
  • God is both formless and with form; Brahman and Shakti are one, like fire and its power to burn.
  • So he held together Advaita (formless Brahman) and devotion to a personal Mother: a step towards Neo-Vedanta.
Mission 5

Images, caste, and the world

  • Image worship: unlike the Brahmos, Ramakrishna worshipped the Mother in her murti; for him the image was a real meeting point with God.
  • He met Brahmo leaders, especially Keshab Chandra Sen, who made him known among educated Bengalis.
  • Roy taught one formless God and rejected images; Ramakrishna accepted both form and the formless.
  • Caste: stories tell of him humbling his brahmin pride by cleaning the latrine of a low-caste servant.
  • Spirit and world: he warned against attachment to lust and money, yet taught that householders can reach God while doing their duties, like a maidservant who cares for her master's children but knows her real home (paraphrase).
  • A saying recorded by his disciples: serve living beings as Shiva, not out of pity (shiva jnane jiva seva). Vivekananda built on it.

Mission 5

Vivekananda

  • Swami Vivekananda (1863–1902), born Narendranath Datta in Calcutta; Western-educated; a member of the Brahmo Samaj, sceptical of visions.
  • Met Ramakrishna in 1881 and became his leading disciple; after Ramakrishna's death (1886) he became a sannyasi and travelled across India, shocked by its poverty.
  • 1893: spoke at the Parliament of the World's Religions in Chicago; became famous overnight.
  • Lectured in the USA and Britain; founded the Vedanta Society of New York (1894).
  • Returned to India a hero; founded the Ramakrishna Mission (1897). Died aged 39.
Mission 5Read the source

Chicago, 11 September 1893

Vivekananda's opening address (paraphrase):

  • He greeted the audience as "Sisters and Brothers of America".
  • He said he was proud to belong to a religion that had taught the world tolerance and universal acceptance: Hindus not only tolerate other religions but accept all religions as true.
  • He quoted a Hindu hymn: as rivers from different sources all reach the sea, so the different paths people take, straight or crooked, all lead to God.
  • He hoped the Parliament would end sectarianism, bigotry and fanaticism.

Q1 Whose teaching is he echoing?

Show answer

Ramakrishna's "as many faiths, so many paths".

Q2 What image of Hinduism did this give Western listeners?

Show answer

A tolerant, universal, philosophical religion.

Mission 5

Neo-Vedanta

  • Neo-Vedanta (term used by Indologist Paul Hacker, 1913–1979): the modern form of Vedanta taught by Vivekananda and his successors.
  • Advaita at the centre: Brahman is the one reality; every soul is potentially divine.
  • Experience (anubhava), not only scripture, is the ground of religion: religion is "realisation".
  • Four yogas (Vivekananda's lectures, 1895–96): karma, bhakti, jnana and raja yoga, suited to different temperaments, all leading to the same goal.
  • Universal: Vedanta is presented as the eternal truth behind all religions, and as friendly to science.
  • Continued by thinkers such as Sarvepalli Radhakrishnan (1888–1975), The Hindu View of Life (1927).
Mission 5

Practical Vedanta and the Ramakrishna Mission

  • Practical Vedanta (lectures in London, 1896): if Brahman is in every being, then serving people is worshipping God.
  • Vivekananda spoke of God in the poor (daridra narayana) and attacked "don't-touch-ism" (untouchability) and neglect of the masses and of women.
  • Shankara's Advaita centred on renunciation and knowledge; Vivekananda reinterpreted it as a call to social service.
  • Ramakrishna Mission (1897), with Belur Math near Kolkata as headquarters. Motto: "for one's own liberation and for the good of the world".
  • Today: hospitals, schools, colleges, rural development, relief after disasters, in India and beyond.
  • A liberationist approach: working to lift the poor and oppressed, seeing service to them as service to God.

Mission 5

Vedanta as the religion behind all religions

  • The view: all faiths, and all traditions within Hinduism, are partial expressions of the one truth Vedanta states most fully.
  • Supporters: it grounds tolerance and interfaith respect; it unites Hindu diversity; it fits Ramakrishna's experience.
  • Hindu critics: theistic traditions (e.g. Vaishnavas, ISKCON) say a personal God, not impersonal Brahman, is the highest truth; the Arya Samaj puts the Vedas, not experience, first.
  • Scholarly critics: Paul Hacker called this inclusivism: other faiths are welcomed only as lower stages of Advaita. Anantanand Rambachan (b. 1951), The Limits of Scripture (1994), argues that Vivekananda moved authority from scripture (as in Shankara) to personal experience.
  • Other faiths: many Christians and Muslims reply that their own claims (e.g. Christ as unique saviour) are not respected by being called "partial".
Mission 5

Influence in India and the West

  • In the West: Vedanta Societies; yoga and meditation; writers drawn to Vedanta. Vedanta became the best-known Hindu philosophy outside India.
  • Popular image: Hinduism as tolerant, mystical and philosophical.
  • Hinduism as a world religion: a universal message, not only an Indian heritage.
  • In India: pride and confidence under colonial rule; a model of service; Vivekananda's birthday is India's National Youth Day.
  • Hindu identity: Ramakrishna's acceptance of images and many paths, and Vivekananda's attack on untouchability, shaped modern Hindu attitudes to worship and caste.
Mission 5Evaluate

Was Ramakrishna shaped by Western ideas?

  • Claim: Ramakrishna's thought was influenced by Western ideas.
  • Against: he knew no English and had little schooling; his practice drew on Tantra, Vaishnava bhakti and Advaita.
  • Against: "many paths" echoes older Hindu ideas: one truth called by many names (Rig Veda 1.164.46, paraphrase).
  • For: he met Brahmos and Christians; Keshab Chandra Sen presented him to a Western-educated public.
  • For: his message reached the world through Vivekananda, whose Western education shaped how it was framed.
  • Judgement criteria: distinguish Ramakrishna himself from how others presented him; what counts as "influence"?

Ramakrishna's experiences gave the idea; Vivekananda gave it words, institutions and a world stage: a tolerant, service-centred Vedanta that became many people's picture of Hinduism.

Mission 6

A life in four places

  • Mohandas Karamchand Gandhi (1869–1948), born in Porbandar, Gujarat, into a Vaishnava family; his mother was deeply devout, with Jain neighbours and friends.
  • London (1888–1891): trained as a lawyer; first read the Bhagavad Gita (in Edwin Arnold's English) with Theosophist friends.
  • South Africa (1893–1914): campaigned for Indians' rights; first satyagraha campaigns; founded communal farms (Phoenix, Tolstoy Farm).
  • India (1915–1948): led the independence movement; Salt March (1930); Quit India (1942); independence and Partition (1947).
  • Shot on 30 January 1948 by Nathuram Godse, a Hindu nationalist who blamed him for concessions to Muslims and Pakistan.
  • Called Mahatma ("great soul") and Bapu ("father").
Mission 6

Truth is God; all life is one

  • Gandhi's central idea: Satya (truth). He moved from "God is Truth" to "Truth is God" (c. 1929–31), so that even atheists seeking truth were included.
  • Advaita Vedanta: ultimate reality is one, Brahman; the self (atman) in each being is not different from it.
  • He drew a moral conclusion: he believed in "the essential unity of man and … of all that lives" (Young India, 4 Dec 1924).
  • So harming another is harming oneself: ahimsa (non-violence) follows from unity.
  • He held Advaita loosely: he also worshipped a personal God as Rama and called himself a sanatani (traditional) Hindu.
  • Your spec: his "conviction and belief in Advaita Vedanta" and how he applied it.
Mission 6

Satyagraha: holding firmly to truth

  • Satyagraha = satya (truth) + agraha (firm grasp): "truth-force" or "soul-force". Coined in South Africa (c. 1906–08).
  • Not "passive resistance": it is active, but never violent.
  • Aim: not to defeat the opponent but to convert them, by accepting suffering rather than inflicting it.
  • Methods: non-cooperation, civil disobedience, strikes, boycotts of foreign cloth, fasting.
  • Rules: openness (tell the authorities in advance), discipline, no hatred.
  • Example: the Salt March (1930): about 390 km to Dandi to make salt in defiance of the British salt tax.
Mission 6Read the source

Brahmacharya and the Gita

> "Dwelling 'mid solitudes, in diet spare, / With body, speech, and will tamed to obey, / Ever to holy meditation vowed, / From passions liberate, quit of the Self, / Of arrogance, impatience, anger, pride; / Freed from surroundings, quiet, lacking nought-- / Such an one grows to oneness with the BRAHM" (Bhagavad Gita 18:52–53, Arnold 1885)

  • Brahmacharya: literally "conduct leading to Brahman". Gandhi took a vow of celibacy in 1906, but used the word for control of all the senses: food, speech, sleep, sex.
  • He saw self-restraint as the source of the inner strength a satyagrahi needs.
  • Gandhi called the Gita his "dictionary of daily reference" (Autobiography, Part IV) and read Kurukshetra as the inner war of good and evil, not a call to fight.

Show answer

Q2 What is the goal the verses promise?

Show answer

Mission 6

Swaraj: rule over yourself

  • Swaraj = self-rule. For Gandhi it meant two things: India governing itself, and each person governing their own desires.
  • Hind Swaraj (1909, written in Gujarati): Indian freedom would be empty if India simply copied Western industrial civilisation.
  • Swadeshi ("of one's own country"): use local goods; the spinning wheel (charkha) and homespun cloth (khadi) became symbols of self-reliance.
  • Ideal: self-governing, simple village communities.
Mission 6

Untouchability, Partition, all religions

  • Untouchability: he called it a sin and not true Hinduism; used the name Harijan ("people of God") and ran temple-entry campaigns.
  • But he defended varna as a division of work by birth without high or low. B. R. Ambedkar (1891–1956) replied that caste must be destroyed, not reformed. Many Dalits find "Harijan" patronising.
  • Partition: he opposed dividing India ("vivisection"); in 1946–48 he walked through riot areas and fasted for Hindu–Muslim peace.
  • Universal religion: all religions are true, and all imperfect; equal respect for all religions (sarva dharma samabhava). His prayer meetings used the Gita, the Qur'an and Christian hymns.

Mission 6Diversity

Who claims Gandhi?

  • Most Hindus in India: "Father of the Nation"; his birthday (2 October) is a national holiday.
  • Hindu nationalists: some honour his patriotism and cow protection; others, like Godse, blamed him for Partition and for favouring Muslims.
  • Orthodox Hindus of his day: opposed his temple-entry campaigns as breaking purity rules.
  • Dalit thinkers: Ambedkar saw him as a defender of caste in a reformer's clothes.
  • The non-Hindu world: an inspiration for Martin Luther King Jr (Montgomery 1955–56), the US civil rights movement and others; the UN marks 2 October as the International Day of Non-Violence.
Mission 6Evaluate

"Gandhi's achievements were more political than religious."

  • For: his lasting results were political: mass non-violent protest, independence, a secular constitution. Many followers used his methods without his religion. He himself was a skilled lawyer and organiser.
  • Against: he said his politics was his religion in action; satyagraha, ahimsa and brahmacharya are religious disciplines. His Gita reading changed how many Hindus see the text. His fasts were religious acts of self-purification.
  • Mixed record: Partition and violence he could not stop; caste reform judged too slow by Dalits; his late "experiments" testing his celibacy are widely criticised.
  • Judge it by: Can "political" and "religious" be separated in Gandhi's world view? Which achievements outlasted him? Whose verdict counts: Hindus, Dalits, or the wider world?

Mission 6Worked answer

Linking belief to action

Question: Examine how Gandhi's religious beliefs shaped his political methods.

  • Belief: all life is one (Advaita); Truth is God.
  • Source: Bhagavad Gita, read as the inner battle; self-restraint in Gita 18:52–53.
  • Development: so he chose satyagraha: open, non-violent resistance that aims to convert the opponent, backed by the self-discipline of brahmacharya.
  • Example: the Salt March (1930) united religious symbolism (pilgrimage on foot, fasting, prayer) with a political target (the salt tax).

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Shruti ("heard": the Vedas, including the Upanishads) has the highest authority; smriti ("remembered": epics, Gita, Puranas, Manusmriti) applies it.
  • The Vedas live mainly in ritual recitation; smriti is better known and shapes festivals and ethics.

Mission 2

  • The epics (itihasa) and Puranas are smriti in story form, widely known among Hindus.
  • The Ramayana shows ideal dharma in Rama, Sita, Lakshmana and Hanuman; the Mahabharata shows duty in conflict, and contains the Gita.
  • The avatar descends "from age to age" when dharma declines (Gita 4:7–8).

Mission 3

  • The Upanishads are the end of the Vedas (Vedanta): teachings of gurus on Brahman and atman, moving from outer sacrifice to inner knowledge.
  • The Katha Upanishad: Nachiketas learns from Yama; the chariot image (Katha 3) shows the intellect controlling the senses.
  • "You are that" (Chandogya 6:13) is read as identity (Advaita), part and whole (Vishishtadvaita), or difference (Dvaita).

Mission 4

  • In Hindu belief, atman is the eternal self; the jivatman is that self embodied and reborn (Gita 2:22; the Katha chariot shows the self as lord, the intellect as driver).
  • Tat tvam asi (Chandogya VI) is read as identity (Shankara), part and whole (Ramanuja) or difference (Madhva).
  • Brahman as sat-chit-ananda, beyond description (neti neti); monist and monotheist readings of the self.

Mission 5

  • Ramakrishna (1836–1886): Kali devotee and mystic; experiences through many paths led him to teach "as many faiths, so many paths"; he accepted both images and the formless.
  • Vivekananda (1863–1902): Chicago 1893; Neo-Vedanta (Advaita, experience, four yogas, all religions true); Practical Vedanta and the Ramakrishna Mission (1897): serving people as God.
  • "Vedanta as religion" grounds tolerance but is criticised as inclusivist and as sidelining theistic Hinduism.

Mission 6

  • Gandhi (1869–1948) joined religion and politics: Truth is God; all life is one; so non-violence (ahimsa) and satyagraha.
  • Brahmacharya (control of all senses, Gita 18:52–53) and swaraj (self-rule of person and nation) gave satyagraha its strength.
  • Hindus, Dalits and the wider world judge him differently: Father of the Nation, defender of caste, or global model of non-violence.
