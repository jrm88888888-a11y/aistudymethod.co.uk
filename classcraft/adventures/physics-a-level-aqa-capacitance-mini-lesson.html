Mission 1

Mission 1: A charged capacitor has no net charge at all. So what is it actually storing?

Make a guess: A charged capacitor has no net charge at all. So what is it actually storing?

Quick check
?Predict first. An uncharged parallel-plate capacitor is connected to a battery and left until it is fully charged. Which statement correctly describes how the capacitor becomes charged?
Mission 1

What a capacitor is

  • Two conducting plates, separated by an insulator (air, a vacuum or a plastic film).
  • Connect a cell: electrons are pulled off one plate, pass round the external circuit, and are pushed onto the other plate.
  • No charge crosses the gap.
  • One plate ends at +Q, the other at −Q. Net charge: zero.
  • "The charge on a capacitor" means Q, the charge on each plate.
Mission 1

Capacitance

  • C = Q/V: capacitance is the charge stored per unit p.d.
  • Unit: the farad. 1 F = 1 C V⁻¹: a capacitor storing 1 C on each plate for every 1 V across it.
  • 1 F is huge. Real values: μF (10⁻⁶ F), nF (10⁻⁹ F), pF (10⁻¹² F).
  • For one capacitor, Q ∝ V. C is fixed by its construction, not by the p.d. you apply.
Mission 1

Worked example

  • A 470 μF capacitor is charged to 9.0 V.
  • Q = CV = 470 × 10⁻⁶ × 9.0 = 4.2 × 10⁻³ C on each plate.
  • Electrons moved from one plate to the other: 4.23 × 10⁻³ ÷ 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ ≈ 2.6 × 10¹⁶.
  • Charge it to 18 V instead: Q doubles; C stays 470 μF.
Quick check
?A parallel-plate capacitor is connected to an ideal 12 V battery. When the capacitor is fully charged, its positive plate carries a charge of 3.6 × 10⁻⁵ C. What is the capacitance of the capacitor?
Mission 1

What it stores: energy

  • Separating +Q from −Q takes work. That work is stored as electric potential energy.
  • Graph of p.d. against charge: a straight line through the origin (V = Q/C).
  • Moving a small charge ΔQ across a p.d. V takes work VΔQ: a thin strip under the line.
  • Add the strips: energy stored = area under the V–Q graph = area of a triangle.
  • E = ½QV
Mission 1

Three forms, one example

  • Use Q = CV: E = ½QV = ½CV² = ½Q²/C.
  • The 470 μF capacitor at 9.0 V: energy = ½ × 470 × 10⁻⁶ × 9.0² = 1.9 × 10⁻² J.
  • At 18 V: the charge doubles and the potential difference doubles, so the energy is four times as much (7.6 × 10⁻² J).
  • Why ½? The cell moves charge Q through p.d. V and transfers QV. The capacitor keeps ½QV; the other ½QV is dissipated as heat in the circuit's resistance.
Quick check
?A 220 μF capacitor is charged to a potential difference of 12 V. How much energy does it store?
Mission check

?The graph shows the magnitude Q of the charge on each plate of a capacitor as a function of the potential difference V across it. What energy is stored in the capacitor when V = 6.0 V?

The plates' charges cancel, so a capacitor stores no net charge. It stores energy: the work done separating +Q from −Q, equal to ½QV.

Mission 2

Mission 2: How can you get more charge onto a capacitor without raising the voltage?

Make a guess: How can you get more charge onto a capacitor without raising the voltage?

Mission 2

What sets C for parallel plates

  • Larger plate area A: the same charge is spread more thinly, so the field and potential difference are smaller. C ∝ A.
  • Smaller gap d → C ∝ 1/d.
  • C = Aε0εᵣ/d (εᵣ = 1 for a vacuum, and very nearly 1 for air).
  • ε0 = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² F m⁻¹: the permittivity of free space.
  • Plate thickness and the charge already stored do not matter.
Mission 2

Worked example

  • Air-filled plates, each 0.040 m², 1.0 mm apart.
  • C = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² × 0.040 ÷ 1.0 × 10⁻³ = 3.5 × 10⁻¹⁰ F (350 pF).
  • Connected to 12 V: Q = CV = 4.2 × 10⁻⁹ C.
  • The field between the plates is uniform: E = V/d = 12 ÷ 1.0 × 10⁻³ = 1.2 × 10⁴ V m⁻¹.
Quick check
?An air-filled parallel-plate capacitor has a capacitance of 16 pF. A second air-filled parallel-plate capacitor has plates with three times the area of the first capacitor's plates, and its plates are twice as far apart. What is the capacitance of the second capacitor?
Mission 2

Dielectrics

  • Fill the gap with an insulator, a dielectric: the capacitance increases.
  • Relative permittivity (dielectric constant) εᵣ = ε/ε0: how many times larger C becomes when the dielectric fills the gap.
  • Each multiplies C by about: air 1.0006 · polythene 2.3 · glass 5 to 10 · water 80.
Quick check
?The diagram shows a charged parallel-plate capacitor, disconnected from its battery, with a slab of dielectric filling the space between its plates. Which statement correctly describes the electric field produced inside the dielectric by the dielectric's own induced charges?
Mission 2

Why a dielectric helps

  • Polar molecules (one end slightly +, the other slightly −; water, for example) rotate in the field to line up with it. In non-polar materials (polythene), the electron clouds shift slightly.
  • Either way: a thin layer of − charge faces the + plate, and + charge faces the − plate.
  • These induced charges make a field opposite to the plates' field. The field in the dielectric is weaker.
  • Same Q, weaker E → smaller p.d. across the gap → C = Q/V is larger.
  • Still connected to the cell? The potential difference is fixed, so more charge flows on.
Mission check

?An air-filled parallel-plate capacitor is charged and then disconnected from the supply. Its plates are then pulled apart to twice their separation. What happens to the charge on each plate, the potential difference across the capacitor and the energy it stores?

Bigger plates, a smaller gap or a dielectric all raise C.

At a fixed p.d., Q = CV: more C stores more charge.

Mission 3

Mission 3: A discharging capacitor never quite empties. So what sets how fast it gets close?

Make a guess: A discharging capacitor never quite empties. So what sets how fast it gets close?

Mission 3

Charging, step by step

  • Cell (emf ε), resistor R and uncharged capacitor C in series. Close the switch.
  • At the start: VC = 0, so the whole emf is across R. The current is largest: I0 = ε/R.
  • Electrons pile onto one plate and leave the other. VC rises.
  • p.d. across R = ε − VC falls, so the current falls, so charging slows.
  • After a long time: VC = ε, current zero. Fully charged.
Mission 3

Discharging

  • Now connect the charged capacitor across R alone.
  • The capacitor's p.d. drives the current: I = VC/R. Electrons flow back to the plate they left. The current is reversed compared with charging.
  • Charge leaves → VC falls → current falls → charge leaves more slowly.
  • The rate of loss is proportional to the charge left. That is exponential decay.
  • It gets ever closer to zero but, in this model, never reaches it.
Mission 3

The graphs

  • Charging: Q and VC rise steeply, then level off at the final value. Current starts at I0 and decays to zero.
  • Discharging: Q, VC and I all decay with the same shape.
  • Q and VC always have the same shape, because Q = CVC.
  • Gradient of a Q–t graph = current.
  • Area under an I–t graph = charge that has flowed.
Quick check
?A capacitor is charged through a resistor by a battery. At time t1 the p.d. across the capacitor is one quarter of the battery's emf. At a later time t2 it is three quarters of the emf. What is the current at t2 divided by the current at t1?
Mission 3

The time constant

  • Time constant τ = RC. Unit: Ω × F = s.
  • Discharging: after one τ, 37% of the starting charge is left.
  • Charging: after one τ, the charge has reached 63% of its final value.
  • After about 5τ: over 99% of the way. Treat as finished.
  • Bigger R → smaller current → slower. Bigger C → more charge to move → slower.
  • The starting p.d. does not change τ.
Mission 3

The equations

  • Discharging: Q = Q0e−t/RC. The same form gives V = V0e−t/RC and I = I0e−t/RC.
  • Charging: Q = Q0(1 − e−t/RC), where Q0 is the final charge. V follows the same form; I still decays: I = I0e−t/RC.
  • At t = RC: e⁻¹ = 0.37, so 37% left; 1 − e⁻¹ = 0.63 reached.
Mission 3

Worked example

  • A 470 μF capacitor, charged to 9.0 V, discharges through 10 kΩ.
  • τ = RC = 10 × 10³ × 470 × 10⁻⁶ = 4.7 s.
  • Initial current: I0 = 9.0 ÷ 10 × 10³ = 0.90 mA.
  • After 5.0 s: V = 9.0 × e−5.0/4.7 = 3.1 V.
  • Time to fall to 1.0 V: e−t/4.7 = 1.0/9.0, so t = 4.7 × ln 9.0 = 10 s.
Quick check
?An uncharged capacitor is charged through a 3.0 kΩ resistor by an ideal battery of emf 9.0 V, all connected in series. What is the current in the resistor at the instant when the potential difference across the capacitor is 6.0 V?
Quick check
?A 50 μF capacitor is charged to 12 V and then discharged through a 200 kΩ resistor. What is the potential difference across the capacitor 15 s after the discharge starts?
Mission check

?An uncharged capacitor is charged through a resistor by a battery. Its charge takes a time t to reach 63% of its final value. The capacitor is emptied. The battery is replaced by one with twice the emf, and a second, identical resistor is added in series with the first. How long does the charge now take to reach 63% of its new final value?

The pace is set by RC alone: the time constant. Every RC, the charge falls to 37% of what it was, so it gets close to empty but never quite there.

Mission 4

Mission 4: How can a straight-line graph reveal the time constant of a curve that never reaches zero?

Make a guess: How can a straight-line graph reveal the time constant of a curve that never reaches zero?

Mission 4

Equal times, equal fractions

  • Exponential decay: in equal time intervals, the charge falls by the same fraction.
  • Every RC: × 0.37. Every 0.69RC: × ½.
  • T½ = 0.69RC: the time to halve, from any starting point. (0.69 = ln 2.)
  • Test for exponential: read V at equal intervals. Equal ratios V1/V0 = V2/V1 = … → exponential.
  • Example: 12.0 V, 8.0 V, 5.4 V, 3.6 V every 2.0 s: each ratio ≈ 0.67.
Mission 4

A straight line from a curve

  • Take natural logs of V = V0e−t/RC: ln V = ln V0 − t/RC.
  • Compare with y = c + mx. Plot ln V against t.
  • Straight line → the decay is exponential.
  • Gradient = −1/RC. Intercept = ln V0.
  • Logs need a pure number: plot ln V with V in volts (the log of the number of volts).
Mission 4

Worked example

  • A capacitor discharges through 100 kΩ. A graph of ln V (V in volts) against t is a straight line.
  • Gradient = −0.20 s⁻¹. Intercept = 2.48.
  • Time constant = 1 ÷ 0.20 = 5.0 s.
  • C = time constant ÷ R = 5.0 ÷ (100 × 10³) = 5.0 × 10⁻⁵ F (50 μF).
  • V0 = e2.48 = 12 V.
  • T½ = 0.69 × 5.0 = 3.5 s.
Quick check
?A student plots ln(I/A) against t for a capacitor discharging through a 20 kΩ resistor. The graph is a straight line of gradient −0.025 s⁻¹. What is the capacitance?
Mission 4

The practical

  • Circuit: d.c. supply, two-way switch, capacitor, resistor. Voltmeter (or data logger) across the capacitor, or an ammeter in series with R.
  • Charge fully. Switch to discharge and start timing.
  • Slow decay (time constant about a minute): read V every 10 s by hand.
  • Fast decay: use a data logger or an oscilloscope.
  • Plot ln V against t; time constant = −1/gradient.
  • A voltmeter across the capacitor is a second discharge path: use a very high-resistance meter.
  • Capacitor values have wide tolerances (often ±20%): expect the time constant to differ from the value worked out from the labels.
  • Required practical 9.
Quick check
?A student measures the time constant of a 10 μF capacitor discharging through a 1.0 MΩ resistor, so the time constant should be 10 s. A voltmeter of resistance 10 MΩ stays connected across the capacitor throughout. How does the measured time constant compare with 10 s?
Mission check

?A student plots ln I (I in amperes) against t for a discharging capacitor. The best-fit straight line passes through ln I = −6.20 at t = 0 and ln I = −7.70 at t = 30 s. How long does the current take to fall to half of any value?

ln V (or ln I, ln Q) against t is a straight line of gradient −1/(time constant). The curve never reaches zero, but the line gives the time constant directly.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • A capacitor's plates carry +Q and −Q; C = Q/V, in farads, set by construction, not by the p.d.
  • Energy stored E = ½QV = ½CV² = ½Q²/C: the area under the V–Q graph.

Mission 2

  • C = Aε0εᵣ/d; a dielectric raises C because its polar molecules line up and weaken the field.

Mission 3

  • Charging: Q and V rise to a limit while I decays; discharging: Q, V and I all decay exponentially.
  • τ = RC: 37% left after one τ (discharging), 63% reached (charging).
  • Discharge: Q = Q0e−t/RC (same form for V and I).

Mission 4

  • ln V = ln V0 − t/RC: plot ln V against t; gradient = −1/RC.
  • T½ = 0.69RC.
