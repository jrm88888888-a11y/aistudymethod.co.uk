Mission 1

Mission 1: Muslim women have argued the veil is oppression, faith and protest. How can one cloth mean all three?

Make a guess: Muslim women have argued the veil is oppression, faith and protest. How can one cloth mean all three?

Mission 1

Words for the veil

  • Hijab: literally "screen" or "curtain". Today usually a headscarf covering hair and neck.
  • Khimar: head-covering (24:31). Jilbab: outer garment or cloak (33:59).
  • Niqab: face-veil, eyes visible. Burqa: full covering with a mesh over the eyes. Chador: Iranian full-length cloak.
  • Awra: the parts of the body to be covered in public.
  • Hanafi and Maliki scholars, the Ja'fari Shi'a school and many others: a woman covers all but face and hands. Many Hanbali and some Shafi'i scholars require the face to be covered too.
  • A small number of modern scholars argue the headscarf itself is not obligatory.
Mission 1Read the source

Modesty for men and women

> "Say to the believing men that they should lower their gaze and guard their modesty: that will make for greater purity for them" (Qur'an 24:30, Yusuf Ali)

> "And say to the believing women that they should lower their gaze and guard their modesty; that they should not display their beauty and ornaments except what (must ordinarily) appear thereof; that they should draw their veils over their bosoms" (Qur'an 24:31, Yusuf Ali)

  • 33:59 asks the Prophet's wives, daughters and the believing women to draw their outer garments around them, "that they should be known (as such) and not molested" (Qur'an 33:59, Yusuf Ali).
  • A hadith: modesty (haya') is a branch of faith (Bukhari, Muslim; paraphrase).

Q1 Who is told to be modest first?

Show answer

Men: 24:30 comes before 24:31.

Q2 What phrase do scholars argue over?

Show answer

"Except what must ordinarily appear": face and hands, or less.

Mission 1

Empire and Qasim Amin

  • Context: Britain occupied Egypt from 1882. Its consul-general Lord Cromer (1841–1917), in Modern Egypt (1908), called the veil and seclusion the main obstacle to Egypt's progress. In England he led opposition to votes for women.
  • Qasim Amin (1863–1908), Egyptian lawyer and judge, studied in France: Tahrir al-Mar'a (The Liberation of Woman, 1899); al-Mar'a al-Jadida (The New Woman, 1900).
  • His argument: face-veiling and seclusion are customs, not required by Shari'a. They keep women uneducated, so the nation stays backward. The veil is a barrier to achievement.
  • He called for girls' education and limits on easy divorce and polygamy. He did not call for abandoning modest dress.
Mission 1

Leila Ahmed

  • Leila Ahmed (b. 1940), Egyptian-American, Harvard: Women and Gender in Islam: Historical Roots of a Modern Debate (1992).
  • Colonial feminism: Cromer used women's rights to justify empire. Amin's book, she argues, repeated that contempt for Egyptian culture (paraphrase).
  • So the veil became a symbol in a fight between West and East, not only a piece of cloth.
  • Two voices in early Islam: an ethical, egalitarian vision in the Qur'an, and an androcentric legal tradition built later. Muslim women can reclaim the first (paraphrase).
  • Anthology extract 4 (the book's Conclusion): she rejects the idea that women must choose between Western values and Islam (paraphrase only).
  • In A Quiet Revolution (2011) she finds that many American Muslim women chose hijab as a sign of faith, identity and activism for justice.

Mission 1

Haleh Afshar

  • Haleh Afshar (1944–2022), Iranian-born British professor of politics and women's studies, University of York; Baroness Afshar from 2007.
  • Islam and Feminisms: An Iranian Case-Study (1998): women in the Islamic Republic used Islamic arguments to win back rights in education, work and family law.
  • Islamic feminism: women can claim equality from within their faith, not only from secular feminism.
  • Her research with young British Muslim women who cover: many chose it themselves, sometimes against family wishes, as faith and as identity (paraphrase).
  • She opposed compulsion both ways: forced veiling in Iran and forced unveiling by bans.

Mission 1Diversity

Why Muslim women cover, or do not

  • Obedience: a duty from God (24:31, 33:59), held by most scholars.
  • Identity: being visibly Muslim, especially as a minority.
  • Freedom: some say covering means being judged by mind, not body.
  • Protest: against Western consumer culture, or against a regime (Iran before 1979).
  • Not covering: some Muslim women hold that modesty is in conduct, not in a scarf; others face pressure or danger if they cover.
  • Culture or religion? Burqa, chador and niqab differ by region: culture shapes how Islam is lived.
Mission 1Evaluate

"The veil is a symbol of oppression."

  • For: some Muslim reformers saw veiling and seclusion as barriers to education and public life. Where the law forces it (Iran), it is imposed by the state. Family pressure is real for some women.
  • For: Amin (1899) made exactly this case.
  • Against: many women choose it freely (Afshar). Bans also take away choice.
  • Against: Ahmed (2011) found the same; calling it oppression can repeat colonial feminism (Cromer).
  • Another view: the cloth has no single meaning: oppression or resistance depends on choice and context.
  • Judge it by: Who chose it? Who is speaking for the women? Is the meaning religious, cultural or political? Does a ban or a law help women?
  • Edexcel's extract question: link Ahmed's argument to these criteria.
Mission check

?In one country the law forces women to cover their hair in public. In another, the law fines women who cover their faces in public. Muslim women protest in both. Which idea, defended by Muslim feminists such as Afshar, links the two protests?

The same cloth can be duty, identity, freedom or a weapon of empire. Context and choice decide its meaning.

Mission 2

Mission 2: How do Muslims keep their faith in a society that treats religion as private, or as nonsense?

Make a guess: How do Muslims keep their faith in a society that treats religion as private, or as nonsense?

Mission 2

Three challenges of secularisation

  • Secularisation: religion losing influence over society, institutions and minds.
  • Displacement: science and reason replace religion as the source of truth and moral values.
  • Privatisation: religion is pushed into the personal sphere: fine at home, out of place in public life.
  • Militant atheism: religion is not just false but irrational and harmful (Richard Dawkins, The God Delusion, 2006; Sam Harris, The End of Faith, 2004).
Mission 2

Why Islam resists privatisation

  • For Muslims, Islam is a din: a whole way of life. Many hold that tawhid (God's oneness) leaves no area outside God's guidance.
  • So prayer five times a day, fasting, diet and dress are public by nature.
  • Reason: the Qur'an repeatedly calls people to reflect: "in the creation of the heavens and the earth … there are indeed Signs for men of understanding" (Qur'an 3:190, Yusuf Ali). Muslims answer "irrational" with this tradition of reason.
  • S. H. Nasr (b. 1933), Knowledge and the Sacred (1981): modern secular thought has lost the sacred; the answer is to recover traditional Islam's spiritual vision, not to copy the West (paraphrase).
  • Responses vary: some Muslims become more visibly devout; some keep faith mainly private; some leave religion.

Mission 2

Identity: dress and diet

  • Dress: modest clothing, hijab or niqab for some women (see Mission 1), beards and caps for some men. A visible sign of belonging.
  • Diet: halal ("permitted") food; no pork, blood or alcohol (2:173, 5:3, 5:90). "Eat of what is on earth, Lawful and good" (Qur'an 2:168, Yusuf Ali).
  • Halal certification for shops and restaurants; halal options in many schools, hospitals and prisons.
  • Debates: slaughter without pre-stunning (campaigners want it banned; Muslim and Jewish groups defend it); whether stunned meat can be halal (certifiers differ).
  • Ramadan: fasting at school and work; employers and schools increasingly make room.
Mission 2

Shari'ah and secularisation

  • Shari'ah: God's path and law. Fiqh: human understanding of it by jurists. Many Muslims say the first is fixed, the second can change.
  • Maqasid al-Shari'ah (aims of Shari'ah): protect faith, life, intellect, family line, property (al-Shatibi, d. 1388). Reformers read rulings by these aims.
  • Traditional: keep the classical rulings of the schools; adapt only where the schools allow.
  • Nasr: Shari'ah is divine and should not be "modernised"; its inner, spiritual dimension answers secular emptiness (paraphrase).
  • Mona Siddiqui (b. 1963), The Good Muslim (2012): classical law was a living debate of moral reasoning; Muslims can enter that debate today, rather than treat law as a fixed code (paraphrase).
  • Liberationist link: some read Shari'ah's demand for justice as a call to resist poverty and oppression (see Mission 3).
Mission 2

Modesty (haya')

  • Hadith: haya' (modesty, shyness) is a branch of faith (Bukhari, Muslim; paraphrase).
  • Both sexes: lower the gaze and guard modesty (24:30–31).
  • Modern pressures: advertising, social media images, fashion, dating culture.
  • Interpretations differ: avoid mixed social settings; mix with care at work and study; modesty as inner attitude more than rules.
  • Questions schools face: PE kit, mixed swimming lessons, sex education.
  • Your spec asks for "debates about different interpretations of the Qur'an and Hadith".
Mission 2

Entertainment

  • Clearly forbidden: alcohol and gambling: "Intoxicants and gambling … are an abomination" (Qur'an 5:90, Yusuf Ali).
  • Enjoyment is not wrong in itself: "Who hath forbidden the beautiful (gifts) of Allah" (Qur'an 7:32, Yusuf Ali).
  • Music is debated. Many classical scholars restricted instruments, citing a hadith in Bukhari and 31:6 on "idle tales". Al-Ghazali (Ihya') allowed listening (sama') under conditions. Today: vocal nasheeds; Muslim pop artists; some avoid all music.
  • TV, film, gaming: judged by content and time: does it lead away from prayer or towards harm?
  • Celebrations: whether to join Christmas or birthday events is debated.
Mission 2

Migration and a British Muslim identity

  • Migration from the 1950s: South Asia, then East Africa, the Middle East, Somalia and elsewhere.
  • First generation: kept village customs and languages; mosques run by community elders; imams from abroad.
  • Later generations: separate religion from culture; English sermons; British-trained imams; "British Muslim" identity.
  • Diversity: Deobandi, Barelwi, Salafi, Shi'a, Sufi, converts; mosques reflect it.
  • Two risks Muslims discuss: assimilation (losing faith) and segregation (cutting off from society).
  • Your spec: "the impact of migration".
Quick check
?What distinction does Mona Siddiqui's work draw on to argue that Muslims today can rethink some rulings?
Mission 2Evaluate

"Muslims in Britain can keep their identity without separating from society."

  • For: dress and diet are largely provided for and protected; many young Muslims hold a British Muslim identity.
  • Against: privatisation and militant atheism press faith inwards; some fear assimilation, others choose separation.
  • Against: music, modesty and dating culture create daily tensions.
  • Another view: identity changes in every age: later generations separate religion from culture.
  • Another view: Siddiqui sees law as a living debate, while Nasr finds strength in tradition.
  • Judge it by: What counts as Muslim identity: belief, practice or culture? Who decides? What does society demand?
Mission check

?A university lecturer says: "Faith is fine as a private hobby, but it should not shape what students wear, eat or do in public on campus." Which challenge of secularisation does this illustrate?

Secularisation pushes faith out of public life; many Muslims answer by keeping it visible and building their own institutions.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Should Muslims fight injustice with a revolution in the streets, or a revolution in the heart?

Make a guess: Should Muslims fight injustice with a revolution in the streets, or a revolution in the heart?

Mission 3

Justice at the heart of revelation

  • 'Adl and qist: justice, fairness, putting things in their right place.
  • Muslims believe God sent prophets with scripture and "the Balance (of Right and Wrong), that men may stand forth in justice" (Qur'an 57:25, Yusuf Ali).
  • "Allah commands justice, the doing of good, and liberality to kith and kin" (Qur'an 16:90, Yusuf Ali).
  • The poor: zakah is their right, not a favour (9:60;). Surah 107 links denying the Judgement with failing to feed the poor.
  • The oppressed (mustad'afun): the Qur'an asks why believers do not act for "those who, being weak, are ill-treated (and oppressed)" (Qur'an 4:75, Yusuf Ali).
Mission 3Read the source

Justice even against yourself

> "O ye who believe! stand out firmly for justice, as witnesses to Allah, even as against yourselves, or your parents, or your kin, and whether it be (against) rich or poor" (Qur'an 4:135, Yusuf Ali)

> "let not the hatred of others to you make you swerve to wrong and depart from justice. Be just: that is next to piety" (Qur'an 5:8, Yusuf Ali)

Q1 Against whom must a believer be ready to give just witness?

Show answer

Themselves, parents, relatives; rich or poor alike.

Q2 What must not bend a Muslim's justice, according to 5:8?

Show answer

Hatred of others, even enemies.

Mission 3

Liberationist approaches

  • Liberationist: reading the Qur'an from the side of the poor and oppressed, and acting to change unjust structures, not only to give charity.
  • Farid Esack (b. 1959), South Africa: fought apartheid with the Call of Islam movement, alongside Christians and others; Qur'an, Liberation and Pluralism (1997): God sides with the oppressed (paraphrase).
  • Asghar Ali Engineer (1939–2013), India: Islam and Liberation Theology (1990): Islam began as a movement for the weak against the rich of Makkah (paraphrase).
  • Nasr warns that justice without its spiritual roots in God becomes another secular ideology (paraphrase).
  • Mona Siddiqui stresses justice as a living ethical demand within Islamic law, not only in revolution (paraphrase).
  • The other side: traditionalist Sufis such as the Cambridge scholar Abdal Hakim Murad (b. 1960) argue that change must begin with purifying the heart (tazkiyah; 13:11): "activism within" (paraphrase).
Quick check
?Which statement best describes a liberationist approach in Islam?
Mission 3Evaluate

"The struggle for justice must begin within the self."

  • For: 13:11; Murad's "activism within": change begins by purifying the heart (tazkiyah).
  • Against: 4:75 demands action for the oppressed now; liberationists such as Esack: waiting for inner perfection serves the oppressor.
  • Another view: inner and outer struggle belong together: justice (4:135) and a purified heart are two sides of one faith.
  • Judge it by: What does the Qur'an put first? What has worked in history (e.g. South Africa)? Who suffers while Muslims wait?
Mission check

?A Muslim food-bank volunteer says: "Food parcels are not enough. The Qur'an sides with the oppressed (4:75), so we must also campaign against the low wages and bad housing that cause poverty, with people of any faith." Which approach is this?

Justice is a command for every Muslim; thinkers differ on whether to start with society or the soul.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Can British Muslims keep their faith whole and still belong fully in Britain?

Make a guess: Can British Muslims keep their faith whole and still belong fully in Britain?

Mission 4

Coming to Britain

  • Small Muslim communities existed earlier: Yemeni and Somali seamen in Cardiff, South Shields and Liverpool; the Shah Jahan Mosque, Woking (1889).
  • 1950s–60s: men from Pakistan (many from Mirpur, Kashmir) and India (Gujarat) came to work in mills and foundries in Lancashire, Yorkshire and the Midlands.
  • Many planned to return. After the Commonwealth Immigrants Act 1962 tightened entry, wives and children joined them: sojourners became settlers.
  • 1970s–80s: families from Bangladesh (Sylhet); East African Asians expelled from Uganda (1972).
  • 1990s onwards: refugees and migrants from Somalia, Bosnia, Iraq, Afghanistan, Syria and elsewhere; also British converts.
Mission 4

Not one community but many

  • Ethnic diversity: South Asian heritage is the largest group; also Arab, Somali, Turkish, Iranian, Malaysian, Nigerian, white British and others.
  • Sunni traditions (most British Muslims): Deobandi (scholarly, reformist; many seminaries), Barelwi (devotional, Sufi-influenced), Salafi, the Tablighi Jamaat (preaching movement).
  • Shi'a: mainly Twelvers (Ithna 'Ashari); also Isma'ilis (the Nizari Isma'ilis are led by the Aga Khan).
  • Mosques often began along lines of village, language and school of thought.
  • Generations differ: many young British-born Muslims separate "culture" from "religion" and choose their own path.
Quick check
?Why did many Muslim migrant workers of the 1950s–60s change from planning to return home to settling permanently in Britain?
Mission 4

Food, dress, practice, education

  • Food: halal meat; school meals and workplace canteens; disputes about whether halal meat must be unstunned.
  • Dress: hijab worn widely; the face veil (niqab) debated in schools, courts and work. Some women choose no headscarf.
  • Practice: Friday prayer (Jumu'ah) in work or school time; prayer rooms; fasting in Ramadan during exams.
  • Education: evening Qur'an classes (madrasas); state-funded Muslim schools since 1998 (Islamia Primary, Brent); RE and sex education debates.
  • Modesty: "Tell the believing men to lower their gaze and be modest." (Qur'an 24:30, Pickthall). 24:31 gives the same duty to women, with a covering; how much covering is debated.
  • The law: UK law protects practising a religion (Article 9 of the European Convention on Human Rights; Equality Act 2010), within limits for safety and others' rights.
Mission 4

Modesty and entertainment

  • Shared principle: entertainment is lawful (halal) unless it involves what is forbidden: alcohol, gambling (5:90), immodesty, or neglect of prayer.
  • Music: some scholars forbid instruments; many permit wholesome music. Yusuf Islam (Cat Stevens) gave up music after converting (1977), then returned to it.
  • Social life: pubs, clubs, mixed parties and dating raise questions for young British Muslims.
  • Responses: Muslim comedians, nasheed singers, modest fashion brands, halal social events.
  • Migration's impact: the questions are sharper where Islam is a minority and the surrounding culture is secular.
  • Responses range from assimilation (absorbed into the majority; distinctiveness lost) to integration (full part in shared life, faith kept).
  • Nasr and Siddiqui on these debates.
Mission check

?A Muslim nurse in a London hospital asks to wear a long-sleeved tunic and a close-fitting headscarf, and to take her prayer break at a set time. The hospital agrees, with hygiene rules followed. What does this show?

Keeping a faith whole in a secular society is often a matter of negotiation: what is essential, and what can adapt?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Qur'an 24:30–31 and 33:59 ask modesty of men and women; most scholars require women to cover all but face and hands.
  • Amin (1899) called the veil a barrier to achievement; Ahmed shows how empire made the veil a symbol ("colonial feminism").
  • Afshar: Islamic feminism and women's own choice, against compulsion in either direction.

Mission 2

  • Secularisation: displacement, privatisation and militant atheism; Islam as din resists privatisation.
  • Shari'a and fiqh: Nasr (tradition and the sacred), Siddiqui (law as living moral reasoning); modesty, entertainment and migration raise new questions.

Mission 3

  • Justice ('adl) is central: 57:25, 4:135, 5:8; the poor have a right to zakah; the Qur'an calls for action for the oppressed (4:75).

Mission 4

  • Muslim migration to Britain grew from the 1950s; family settlement after 1962 built mosques, halal food and schools; British Muslims are diverse in ethnicity and school of thought.
  • Segregation, assimilation and integration are different responses; most Muslim bodies seek integration, protected by freedom of religion (Article 9).
