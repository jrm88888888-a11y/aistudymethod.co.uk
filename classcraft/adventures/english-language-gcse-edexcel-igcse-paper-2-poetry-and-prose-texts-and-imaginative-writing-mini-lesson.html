Mission 1

Why does "Disabled" keep jumping between then and now?

Make a guess: Why does "Disabled" keep jumping between then and now?

Owen · DisabledScreen 1

Owen, 1917

  • Owen served in France until he was invalided home in 1917.
  • At Craiglockhart hospital, Edinburgh, among men like the poem's soldier, he wrote "Disabled" (revised 1918).
  • Recruits had to be 18 to enlist, 19 to serve abroad.
  • Owen was killed on 4 November 1918. His Poems (1920) name their subject: "War, and the pity of War".
  • Context isn't assessed in Language: use it only to explain meaning.

Owen · DisabledScreen 5

Structure: then and now

  • The poem cuts between present (chair, park, institutions) and past (town, football, joining up).
  • Returns are marked: "Now he will never feel again", "Now he is old", then "a few sick years in Institutes".
  • Contrast: once "carried shoulder-high" after matches; now he waits for someone to "put him into bed".
  • Loose rhyme (dark / park; hymn / him); uneven stanzas.
  • It ends on a repeated question, "Why don't they come?": he can't get to bed alone.

Mission checkScreen 9

?Why does "Disabled" keep jumping between then and now?

Owen sets the young man's past (dancing, football, the cheers as he left) against his present in the chair, so every return to "now" shows what the war took. The structure also exposes how he was drawn in by vanity and cheering, which makes the pity at the end, and the waiting for bed, harder to bear.

Mission 2

Told her husband is dead, she goes upstairs alone. What does she feel?

Make a guess: Told her husband is dead, she goes upstairs alone. What does she feel?

Anthology prose · The Story of an HourScreen 1

The story in one hour

  • Mrs Mallard has heart trouble, so her sister Josephine breaks the news gently: her husband Brently has died in a railroad disaster.
  • She weeps, then sits alone in her room facing the open window.
  • A feeling reaches her: she whispers that she is free, and imagines years of her own.
  • She comes downstairs in triumph. Brently walks in, alive. She dies.
  • The doctors say she died of heart disease, of joy.
Anthology prose · The Story of an HourScreen 2

How your exam asks this

  • Paper 2, Section A: one essay on one Part 2 anthology text, 30 marks, about 45 minutes. The text is printed for you.
  • It tests AO1 (ideas and perspectives, 12 marks) and AO2 (how language and structure create effects, 18 marks).
  • Coursework (Component 3): Assignment A is an essay of about 1200 words on three Part 2 texts, at least one poem and one prose text.
  • Essay practice is for class.

Anthology prose · The Story of an HourScreen 5

The open window

  • Her room faces an open window: a symbol of a wider world and a future opening up.
  • Outside it is spring: trees "aquiver with the new spring life", "patches of blue sky". The setting mirrors a rebirth she hasn't yet named.
  • Her grief is "the storm of grief": a metaphor, and the clouds now break into blue sky.
  • The feeling is personified, "creeping out of the sky, reaching toward her", so freedom seems to arrive from outside, unbidden.
Anthology prose · The Story of an HourScreen 6

Inside her head

  • A third-person narrator stays close to Louise's thoughts, so we share feelings no other character knows: "What did it matter!"
  • The story doesn't make Brently cruel: she knows she will weep at his "kind, tender hands".
  • Its target is one will bending another in marriage: "There would be no powerful will bending hers".
  • Context: Chopin wrote it in 1894 for Vogue, a magazine for well-off American readers; a wife's joy at her freedom was a daring subject. Don't read it as Chopin's own life story.

Anthology prose · The Story of an HourScreen 8

The twist and the last line

  • Situational irony: the husband she believes dead walks in "a little travel-stained", unaware of the accident.
  • Dramatic irony: the doctors blame "joy that kills", joy at seeing him. We know her joy was freedom, and that its loss came at that moment.
  • The heart trouble in the first sentence returns at the end: the story is built in a circle.
  • An hour compresses a whole life's change: a dramatic rise and a sudden fall.
Mission checkScreen 9

?Told her husband is dead, she goes upstairs alone. What does she feel?

She grieves first, weeping "with sudden, wild abandonment". Then, alone at the open window, a feeling she tries to beat back takes hold: she is free, and the years ahead will be her own. The ending's irony is that the doctors call it joy at his return.

Mission 3

One borrowed necklace, ten years of debt. Who is really to blame?

Make a guess: One borrowed necklace, ten years of debt. Who is really to blame?

Anthology prose · The NecklaceScreen 1

The plot in brief

  • Mathilde, pretty but poor, is married to a Ministry clerk. She dreams of luxury.
  • Loisel brings an invitation to a ball. He gives up his savings for her dress; she borrows a diamond necklace from her rich friend Madame Forestier.
  • She is the star of the ball, then loses the necklace.
  • They secretly buy a replacement with borrowed money and spend ten years repaying it.
  • Then Madame Forestier tells her the original was fake.
Anthology prose · The NecklaceScreen 2

How your exam asks this

  • Paper 2, Section A: one essay on one Part 2 anthology text, 30 marks, about 45 minutes, text printed for you. It tests AO1 (ideas, perspectives) and AO2 (language and structure).
  • Coursework (Component 3): Assignment A, about 1200 words on three Part 2 texts, including a poem and a prose text.
  • Translation: this lesson quotes an older translation (Jonathan Sturges, 1889) that is free to quote; your anthology's modern one differs. Learn the moments here; quote the anthology in your answer.

Anthology prose · The NecklaceScreen 5

Voice and pace

  • A third-person narrator tells the story with ironic distance and general comments: "since with women there is neither caste nor rank".
  • Lists build her longing: dreams of "silent antechambers", "shining silverware", "delicious dishes".
  • Pace shows what matters to her: the ball gets a rich, detailed paragraph; ten years of labour pass in "And this life lasted ten years."
  • Near the end the narrator exclaims: "How little a thing is needed for us to be lost or to be saved!"
Anthology prose · The NecklaceScreen 6

The twist ending

  • The last line reveals the necklace was paste (imitation jewels), worth at most 500 francs.
  • Situational irony: ten years of sacrifice were for nothing, caused by hiding the truth.
  • The ending reframes what came before: a reader now notices clues, such as the jeweller who had only supplied the case, and Madame Forestier not opening it when it is returned.
  • The story stops at the reveal. We never see Mathilde's reaction: the judgement is left to us.

Anthology prose · The NecklaceScreen 8

Context in brief

  • Maupassant (died 1893) published "La Parure" in the Paris newspaper Le Gaulois in 1884. He is known for twist endings.
  • It is set in 1880s Paris, where class showed in clothes, homes and servants.
  • A minor clerk's wife had few routes up: the opening says she had no means of being "wedded, by any rich and distinguished man".
  • Link context to the text: the story asks whether appearances, or the choices made to keep them, ruin the Loisels.
Mission checkScreen 9

?One borrowed necklace, ten years of debt. Who is really to blame?

There's no single culprit, which is why the question works. Mathilde's longing and pride, the couple's decision to hide the loss, Madame Forestier's silence and a world that judges by appearances all play a part. Strong answers weigh them with evidence and show how the twist ending makes the reader judge.

Mission 4

Many exam stories go wrong in one place. Beginning, middle or end?

Make a guess: Many exam stories go wrong in one place. Beginning, middle or end?

Writing · narrativeScreen 1

A short story is small

  • In about 45 minutes, write a small story: one main character, one place, a short stretch of time.
  • Something must change: a decision, a discovery, a realisation.
  • A simple shape: situation → problem → turning point → aftermath.
  • Choose a point of view (first or third person) and keep it consistent.
Writing · narrativeScreen 2

How your exam asks this

  • Paper 2 Section B: imaginative writing from a choice of three prompts, 30 marks (AO4 18, AO5 12), about 45 minutes; or coursework Assignment B, 30 marks.
Writing · narrativeScreen 3

Where stories go wrong

  • Too many events: a crash, a rescue, a hospital, a funeral. Each gets one line, and none is felt.
  • A rushed ending: the last event is squeezed in as time runs out.
  • A slow start: the alarm clock, breakfast, the walk to school.
  • The fix: plan the ending first, cut events, and spend your words on the turning point, slowing it down.
Rank themScreen 4
?Title: The Last Train. Rank these story plans from strongest to weakest.

Tap the strongest answer first, then the next one. Tap a numbered answer again to undo.

Writing · narrativeScreen 5

Openings and endings

  • Open close to the change: start in the moment, or with one telling detail, not with the alarm clock.
  • End with an action or image, not an explanation. Let the reader work out what has changed.
  • An ending can be open: a character pauses, decides or notices, and the story stops.
  • Avoid endings that cancel the story (it was all a dream).

Mission checkScreen 9

?Many exam stories go wrong in one place. Beginning, middle or end?

Usually at the end, though the problem starts in the plan. Too many events mean every moment is summarised and the ending is squeezed in; a small story with one change, a planned ending and a slowed-down turning point has time to land.

Mission 5

A busy market, without the word 'busy'. Where do you point the camera?

Make a guess: A busy market, without the word 'busy'. Where do you point the camera?

Writing · descriptionScreen 1

Point the camera

  • Good description works like a camera, not a list. You choose where it points.
  • Wide shot: the whole scene in a sentence or two. Mid shot: one group or corner. Close-up: one small detail.
  • Choose three or four shots, not twenty. Each new paragraph can be a new shot.
  • The close-up usually carries the feeling: one exact detail the reader remembers.
Writing · descriptionScreen 2

How your exam asks this

  • Paper 2 Section B: imaginative writing from a choice of three prompts, 30 marks (AO4 18, AO5 12), about 45 minutes; or coursework Assignment B, 30 marks.
Writing · descriptionScreen 3

Show it, don't label it

Label (tells)Detail (shows)
The café was crowded.Two queues for the counter have merged into one argument.
The kitchen was freezing.Her breath clouds over the cereal bowl.
He was nervous.He reads the same line of the menu four times.
  • Pick details only this scene would have.
  • One exact noun ("crate", "awning") beats three adjectives.
  • Use more than sight: one sound, one smell, one texture.
Pick the strongestScreen 4
?Task: describe a busy market. Which opening sentence is strongest?
Writing · descriptionScreen 5

Shape without a plot

  • A description needs structure, even without events. Three reliable shapes:
  • Zoom: wide, then closer, then one detail.
  • Time shift: the same place at different times (dawn and dusk, summer and winter).
  • One moment stretched: a few seconds, described slowly.
  • End on purpose: a quiet close-up, or an image that echoes your first line.

Describe a bus station late at night in three to five sentences. Start wide, then close in on one detail that carries the mood. Don't use the words 'empty' or 'lonely'.

Model answer

The last bus has gone, but the departures board keeps listing towns nobody is travelling to. Under it, the plastic seats are bolted together in fours, all facing the same dark road. One seat has a coat folded on it, neatly, as if its owner has only stepped away. The sleeve hangs down and swings a little each time the automatic doors open on nothing.

Check your own answer

Mission checkScreen 9

?A busy market, without the word 'busy'. Where do you point the camera?

Point it at a few chosen shots and finish close. A wide shot sets the scene, a mid shot finds a corner, and one exact detail (a man turning sideways with a crate and still not getting through) shows the crowd better than any label.

Mission 6

Your teacher can't correct your draft. How do you find the weak bit?

Make a guess: Your teacher can't correct your draft. How do you find the weak bit?

Coursework · drafting and self-editingScreen 1

How your coursework works

  • Component 3: Assignment A is an essay on three Part 2 anthology texts, at least one poem and one prose text (about 1200 words; 12 marks AO1, 18 AO2). Assignment B is imaginative writing (about 700 words; 30 marks). Word counts are advisory.
Coursework · drafting and self-editingScreen 2

What your teacher may and may not do

  • Your teacher can give general feedback on a draft: what works, what to develop.
  • They can't correct, edit or rewrite it for you. Making the changes is your job.
  • Once work is marked, it must not be rewritten. You work independently and sign an authentication sheet.
  • Never pass off others' words, or AI-generated text, as your own: acknowledge any source.

Coursework · drafting and self-editingScreen 4

Redraft from big to small

  1. Task and purpose: does the whole piece do what was asked?
  2. Structure: is the order right? Does each paragraph have one job?
  3. Paragraphs: is any paragraph weak, repetitive or off-task?
  4. Sentences: vary length and openings; cut filler.
  5. Words and accuracy: precise vocabulary, then spelling, punctuation, grammar.
  • Proofreading a paragraph you later cut wastes time: go big first.

Coursework · drafting and self-editingScreen 7

Find your own weak paragraph

  • Reverse outline: beside each paragraph, write its job in five words. A paragraph with no clear job, or the same job as another, is your weak one.
  • Read aloud: you hear repetition and tangled sentences that your eyes skip.
  • Check against the criteria: for each one, point to where you meet it.
  • One focus per pass: structure, then sentences, then accuracy.
Write itScreen 8
?Redraft the first two sentences of the market paragraph (screen 5) so that they show, rather than tell, how crowded it is.
Show the extract (screen 5)

Rewrite 'The market was very busy and loud. There were lots of people everywhere and it was very crowded.' in 2–3 sentences. Use precise sounds and movement instead of 'busy', 'loud' and 'crowded'.

Model answer

Shoulders bumped mine before I was through the gate. A trader bellowed prices over the clatter of crates, and somewhere a radio fought with a crying baby. I turned sideways to squeeze between two prams, and the crowd closed behind me like water.

Mission checkScreen 9

?Your teacher can't correct your draft. How do you find the weak bit?

You find it yourself, systematically. Write each paragraph's job in a few words, check the piece against the task and the criteria, and read it aloud. The paragraph with no clear job, a repeated job or a stumble is the one to redraft first, from big changes down to small.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Written at Craiglockhart in 1917: a young soldier in a wheelchair remembers the life he had before he joined up.
  • The poem cuts between then and now; each "Now" makes the loss sharper, ending on "Why don't they come?"
  • Paper 2: one essay on language and structure (AO1, AO2); for coursework, link it with two other Part 2 texts.
  • Plot: the news, her weeping, the room and the open window, her whisper of "free", the triumphant descent, Brently's return, her death and the doctors' verdict.
  • Methods: symbolic setting (open window, spring), personification of the feeling, a close third-person narrator, the simile "like a goddess of Victory", situational and dramatic irony in a circular structure.
  • In the exam the text is printed for you: quote the anthology, and link each method to its effect on the reader (AO2) and to the story's ideas about freedom and marriage (AO1).
  • Plot: invitation, the dress and the borrowed diamonds, triumph at the ball, the loss and cover-up, ten years of debt, the revelation that the necklace was paste.
  • Methods: an ironic third-person narrator with general comments, lists of longing, pace (the ball in detail, ten years in a line), clues that the twist ending reframes.
  • Your anthology's translation differs from the 1907 one quoted here: in the exam, quote the anthology printed on your paper.
  • Write a small story: one character, one place, a short time, and one change.
  • Shape it (situation, problem, turning point, aftermath), plan the ending first, and slow down at the turning point.
  • Open close to the change and end with an action or image, not an explanation.
  • Description works like a camera: choose wide, mid and close-up shots, and move closer on purpose.
  • Show qualities through exact details and nouns, not labels or piled adjectives.
  • Give a description a shape (zoom, time shift, or one moment stretched) and end on a deliberate close-up or echo.
  • Your teacher can give general feedback on a draft but can't correct or rewrite it; the changes, and the work, must be yours. Acknowledge any source; never present AI or others' text as your own.
  • Redraft from big to small: task, structure, paragraphs, sentences, then words and accuracy. Use a reverse outline and read aloud to find the weak paragraph.
  • Assignment A: about 1200 words on three anthology texts (a poem and a prose text at least); Assignment B: about 700 words of imaginative writing; no rewriting after marking.

Key quotations to learn

  • "He sat in a wheeled chair, waiting for dark," (Line 1) Opens in a passive, waiting present
  • "All of them touch him like some queer disease." (Stanza 2) Simile: others now recoil from him
  • "Poured it down shell-holes till the veins ran dry," (Stanza 3) Metaphor: blood and youth wasted far from home
  • "Smiling they wrote his lie; aged nineteen years." (Stanza 4) Recruiters complicit in his underage enlistment
  • "Some cheered him home, but not as crowds cheer Goal." (Stanza 5) Homecoming without glory, against the football crowd
  • "Why don't they come?" (Last line) Repeated question: helpless and forgotten
  • "free, free, free!" (Alone in her room, the turning point) The repeated word marks the turning point from grief to joy.
  • "she would live for herself" (Alone in her room, imagining the years ahead) Sums up the future she imagines: a life not shaped by another's will.
  • "And yet she had loved him—sometimes. Often she had not." (Alone in her room, just before "Body and soul free!") Short sentences make her feelings honest and mixed, not simple hatred.
  • "a very elixir of life" (Narration, just after her reply to Josephine at the door) Freedom is a life-giving drink; ironic, as she will soon die.
  • "like a goddess of Victory" (Coming downstairs with her sister) Simile of triumph at its height, just before the twist.
  • "joy that kills" (Final line) The doctors' verdict: dramatic irony, since we know what her joy was.
  • "as if by a mistake of destiny" (Opening sentence) Presents her birth as an accident, as she sees it; fate and chance frame the story.
  • "She had no dresses, no jewels, nothing." (Opening, her dissatisfaction) A list falling to "nothing" shows how absolute her sense of lack is.
  • "her heart began to beat with an immoderate desire" (Choosing the necklace) Her desire is beyond measure; the necklace becomes the object of it.
  • "She took her part, moreover, all on a sudden, with heroism." (After the replacement) The narrator credits her courage, which complicates a reading of her as merely vain.
  • "How little a thing is needed for us to be lost or to be saved!" (Near the end, ten years later) The narrator's comment on chance and fragile lives.
  • "Why, my necklace was paste." (Madame Forestier's final speech) The twist: the sacrifice was for an imitation.
