Paper 2: Poetry, Prose & Imaginative Writing

This is a skills lesson, not a memory test. Paper 2 rewards how you analyse a poem or prose extract and how you craft a piece of imaginative writing — the same techniques work on any text you meet.

Work through each screen, answer the questions as you go (technique-spotting, analysis and writing craft) and collect ⭐ stars. Press Start when you're ready.

How Paper 2 works

Two sections, one skill each

Paper 2 is 1 hour 30 minutes and worth 40% of the qualification. You split your time roughly in half:

  • Section A — reading & analysis (30 marks): one essay on a poetry or prose text. You explain what the writer conveys and how their language, form and structure create effects. ~45 minutes.
  • Section B — imaginative writing (30 marks): you choose one of a few creative tasks (narrative or descriptive) and write your own piece. ~45 minutes.

Skills, not set texts: your school may study different anthology texts, and Section A can be poetry or prose. So this lesson trains the transferable moves — reading closely, writing analytically, and crafting original writing — that score marks whatever the text.

Assessment Objectives

What the examiner is marking

Every mark maps to an Assessment Objective (AO). Know which section rewards which:

Section A — Reading

AO1 — read and understand; select and interpret ideas, information and perspectives (with short embedded quotations).

AO2 — analyse how writers use language, form and structure to achieve effects.

Section B — Writing

AO4 — communicate imaginatively, adapting tone, style and register for purpose and audience (content & organisation).

AO5 — write clearly and accurately: varied vocabulary and sentence structures, correct spelling, punctuation and grammar (technical accuracy / SPaG).

Note on AO3 & AO6: AO3 (comparing texts) and AO6 (spelling/punctuation as a separate strand) belong to other papers of the qualification. On Paper 2, reading is AO1–AO2 and writing is AO4–AO5, with technical accuracy assessed inside AO5.

Section A · analysing poetry

The poetry toolkit

When you meet a poem, read for these three layers — form, structure and language:

  • Form — the shape: stanza lengths, line lengths, is it a sonnet / free verse / ballad?
  • Structure — the order of ideas: how the poem develops, a turn or shift (volta), the ending.
  • Rhyme & rhythm — a regular rhyme scheme can feel controlled; a broken one can feel unsettled.
  • Enjambment — a sentence running over a line break, often creating flow, momentum or surprise.
  • Imagery — pictures made with metaphor, simile and personification.
  • Tone & voice — the speaker's attitude (bitter? tender? nostalgic?) and who is speaking.

Golden rule: never just name the device. Always answer "so what?" — what effect does it create and why might the writer want it?

Worked model · poetry

Reading a stanza closely

Here is a short (invented) stanza, annotated the way a strong candidate reads:

The iron sea kept hammering, hammering
the shore that could not answer back —
and still I waited.
  • "iron sea" — a metaphor: the sea feels hard, cold, unforgiving, mirroring the speaker's mood.
  • "hammering, hammering" — repetition + violent verb: the sea seems relentless, almost cruel.
  • enjambment into "answer back" — the line breaks off, enacting the silence the speaker feels.
  • The short final line, "and still I waited," lands with weary resignation — a structural full-stop to the noise.

Notice: every note zooms in on a specific word and links it to a feeling or idea. That is AO2.

Section A · analysing prose

The prose toolkit

If Section A is a prose extract (a story or novel passage), you read for a slightly different set of tools:

  • Narrative voice — first person ("I") feels intimate; third person can feel distant or all-seeing. Is the narrator reliable?
  • Characterisation — how a character is built through actions, speech, thoughts and other people's reactions.
  • Setting & atmosphere — place and weather often mirror mood (a technique called pathetic fallacy).
  • Language — verbs, adjectives, senses, and the connotations of word choices.
  • Structure — pace, where tension builds, and any shift or turning point in the extract.

Same golden rule: pick a short quotation, then explain the effect on the reader. Poetry and prose analysis share the same engine — evidence + effect.

The analytical paragraph

Build a PEA(E) paragraph

Section A answers are built from analytical paragraphs. The reliable shape is Point → Evidence → Analysis → Effect:

Embed quotes: weave short quotations into your sentence — the sea "kept hammering" — rather than dropping a whole line on its own.

Worked model · PEA

A model analytical paragraph

Here is the toolkit applied to our stanza, in one PEA paragraph:

Model paragraph

Point: The writer presents the sea as a cruel, unstoppable force.

Evidence: The sea "kept hammering, hammering" at the shore.

Analysis: The violent verb "hammering", repeated, has connotations of relentless, deliberate blows, as if the sea is attacking on purpose.

Effect: This makes the reader feel the speaker's sense of being worn down, reinforcing the poem's mood of helpless waiting.

Why it scores: one short embedded quote, a zoom-in on a single verb, connotations named, and a clear link to the reader's feeling and the poem's mood.

Match it

Device ↔ effect

Analysis means knowing what an effect a device tends to create. Tap a device, then tap the effect it matches.

Common trap

Don't just spot devices

The biggest Section A trap is feature-spotting: listing techniques without explaining their effect. Compare:

❌ Feature-spotting (low marks)

"The writer uses a metaphor and personification and alliteration. This is effective and makes it interesting."

✅ Analysis (high marks)

"The metaphor 'iron sea' gives the water a cold, unyielding hardness, so the reader feels the speaker is battling something merciless."

Fix: for every device you name, force yourself to finish the sentence "…which makes the reader feel / suggests / emphasises…". No effect = no analysis mark.

Section B · imaginative writing

Now you're the writer

In Section B you choose one creative task and produce your own narrative or descriptive piece. Here, the same techniques you analysed become tools you use.

  • AO4 rewards content & organisation — an engaging, well-shaped piece with a clear tone and register.
  • AO5 rewards technical accuracy — varied, ambitious vocabulary and sentences, and correct spelling & punctuation.

Plan first. Two minutes of planning (an opening image, a middle shift, a closing image) beats a piece that rambles. Examiners reward deliberate structure.

Craft · show, don't tell

Show, don't tell

The single most powerful craft move: instead of naming an emotion, show it through actions, senses and detail so the reader feels it.

❌ Telling

"She was very nervous before the interview."

✅ Showing

"Her thumbnail dug crescents into her palm; the folder trembled as she read the same line for the fourth time."

How: pick the emotion, then ask "what would the body do? what would I see, hear, feel?" Replace the abstract word with concrete, sensory detail.

Craft · sentences & vocabulary

Vary sentences & reach higher

AO5 rewards range. Two easy upgrades that lift a piece:

  • Sentence variety — mix long, flowing sentences with a short, punchy one for impact. The storm tore at the tents, ripped the ropes, flattened the flags. Then silence.
  • Ambitious vocabulary & punctuation — precise verbs and adjectives ("splintered", "brittle"), and controlled use of the semicolon, colon and dash to shape rhythm.
Upgrade in action

Before: "The wave was big and it hit the boat and it was scary."

After: "The wave reared — a wall of green glass — and slammed the hull. We froze."

Careful: ambition must stay accurate. A misused semicolon or misspelt "ambitious" word costs AO5 marks. Reach — but control.

Craft · structure

Shape it: openings, arc & endings

Great imaginative writing is deliberately shaped. A simple, reliable arc:

  • Openings — start in the middle of a moment or with a striking image, not "One day…".
  • Endings — echo the opening image (a "cyclical" ending) or land on a resonant final line.

Exam-day strategy

Splitting your 90 minutes

  • ~45 min · Section A: read the text twice, annotate, then write 3–4 tight PEA paragraphs covering language, structure and form.
  • ~45 min · Section B: plan (2 min), draft with a clear arc, then leave 3–4 minutes to proofread for SPaG.
  • Answer the question set, not "everything you know" — in Section A the third bullet usually points you to language.
  • Quality over quantity: a few well-analysed quotations beat a long list of unexplained devices.

Golden thread: reading and writing are the same skill from two sides — analyse effects in Section A, then engineer those effects in Section B.

Recap

The skills to carry in

Section A · Reading (AO1–AO2): poetry & prose toolkits — form, structure, language, imagery, voice.

Analyse, don't spot: every device → its EFFECT on the reader.

PEA(E): Point → Evidence → Analysis → Effect, with short embedded quotes.

Section B · Writing (AO4–AO5): plan an arc; strong opening & ending.

Show, don't tell: sensory, concrete detail instead of naming emotions.

Range & accuracy: varied sentences, ambitious yet correct vocabulary & punctuation.
