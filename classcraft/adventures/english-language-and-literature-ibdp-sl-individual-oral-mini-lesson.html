Mission 1

What makes an issue 'global', and how can two texts share one?

Make a guess: What makes an issue 'global', and how can two texts share one?

IB Individual Oral · the global issueScreen 1

How your exam asks this

  • The Individual Oral (IO): 15 minutes, 10 of them your talk and 5 of questions from your teacher; 40 marks.
  • You explore one global issue through an extract (up to 40 lines) from each of two texts, and through each work as a whole.
  • One literary work and one non-literary body of work, e.g. a campaign of adverts or a photographer's series.
  • SL: 30% of your grade.
  • Your teacher marks it; the IB moderates a sample.
IB Individual Oral · the global issueScreen 2

Three tests for a global issue

  • Significant on a wide scale: it matters to many people.
  • Transnational: it crosses borders.
  • Felt locally: its impact shows in everyday lives, a kitchen table as well as the globe.
  • Specific enough for a ten-minute talk.
Fields of inquiry (starting points, not issues)
Culture, identity and community
Beliefs, values and education
Politics, power and justice
Art, creativity and the imagination
Science, technology and the environment

IB Individual Oral · the global issueScreen 4

One issue, two very different texts

  • The issue is a thread: each text shows it from a different angle, through its own authorial choices.
  • You analyse how each text presents the issue, in its extract and its work as a whole, not what the issue is like in real life.
  • Differences help: different times, places and forms make the comparison richer.
  • Example pair: The Awakening and an invented body of work: five skincare adverts by Brightmere.
  • Use your learner portfolio to trace possible issues across texts.

Mission checkScreen 8

?What makes an issue 'global', and how can two texts share one?

An issue is global when it is significant on a wide scale, crosses national borders and shows its impact in everyday local lives. Two very different texts can share one because each presents it through its own choices, so your oral follows one thread through two angles.

Mission 2

Ten minutes, two extracts, one teacher. How do you spend them?

Make a guess: Ten minutes, two extracts, one teacher. How do you spend them?

IB Individual Oral · extracts, structure and deliveryScreen 1

Choosing your extracts

  • Each extract is up to 40 lines, continuous, from one work (one literary work; one text from your non-literary body of work).
  • Choose the passage where your global issue is presented most richly through the author's choices, not just mentioned.
  • You also discuss each work as a whole: pick extracts that open out to the rest of the text.
  • You take in clean copies, with no notes on them.
IB Individual Oral · extracts, structure and deliveryScreen 2

A ten-minute shape

PartRoughlyWhat you do
Introduction1 minState the global issue, name both texts, give your line of argument
Extract 13 minAnalyse how its choices present the issue
Work 1 as a whole1 minLink the extract to the rest of the work
Extract 23 minThe same, with links back to text 1
Work 2 as a whole1 minLink the extract to the rest of the work
Conclusion1 minWhat the two angles show together
  • A common plan, not an IB rule. The test is balance: both texts and both works get real time.
IB Individual Oral · extracts, structure and deliveryScreen 3

The outline and delivery

  • You may bring an outline of up to 10 bullet points.
  • Make it prompts, not a script: you may not read it out.
  • Speak to your teacher. Quote words from the extract to show where you are.
  • Signpost: ‘My second text approaches the issue from the other side…’
  • Return to the global issue in every section.
  • Rehearse with a timer, but aim to explain, not recite.

IB Individual Oral · extracts, structure and deliveryScreen 7

How your exam asks this

  • Two extracts: one from a literary work, one from your non-literary body of work.
  • 10 minutes of your talk, then 5 minutes of questions from your teacher; recorded.
  • Four criteria, 10 marks each (40 in all): A Knowledge, understanding and interpretation; B Analysis and evaluation; C Focus and organization; D Language.
  • SL: 30% of your grade.
  • Marked by your teacher, moderated by the IB.

Mission checkScreen 9

?Ten minutes, two extracts, one teacher. How do you spend them?

Spend it in balance. Open with your global issue and both texts, give each extract about three minutes of close analysis and each work a minute, then close by showing what the two angles reveal together. Speak from prompts, not a script, and keep returning to the issue.

Mission 3

Six adverts, one campaign: how do you treat them as one body of work?

Make a guess: Six adverts, one campaign: how do you treat them as one body of work?

IB Language and Literature · bodies of workScreen 1

What is a body of work?

  • A body of work is a collection of non-literary texts by one author or creator, studied together.
  • Examples: an advertising campaign (the agency is the creator), a cartoonist's strips, a photographer's series, a politician's speeches, a columnist's articles.
  • One advert on its own is a text, not a body of work.
  • You choose a body your class studied; there's no set list.
IB Language and Literature · bodies of workScreen 2

How your exam asks this

  • Individual Oral (15 minutes: 10 talking, 5 questions; 40 marks): one extract from a literary work and one from a non-literary body of work, linked by a global issue.
  • Your non-literary extract can be one text, or part of one, chosen to represent the body.
Extract IB Language and Literature · bodies of workScreen 3

The "Empty Seat" campaign (invented example)

IB Language and Literature · bodies of workScreen 4

Read the pattern, then the variation

  • Shared: creator and purpose, the headline, the image type (an empty seat), layout, typeface, call to action.
  • Varied: the setting, the small-print request, where each one appears (bus shelter, phone, magazine).
  • Context: who sees each poster, where, and why.
  • Cumulative meaning: one empty chair is sad; six suggest absence everywhere. Analyse what the series does that a single text can't.

Mission checkScreen 8

?Six adverts, one campaign: how do you treat them as one body of work?

Find the pattern first (one creator, one headline, one kind of image), then the variations, and ask what the series does together that one advert can't. In the IO your extract stands for the whole body, so say how it represents the pattern.

Recap

Big ideas

  • The IO explores one global issue through an extract from each of two texts and their works as a whole: 15 minutes, 40 marks.
  • A global issue is significant on a wide scale, transnational and felt locally; fields of inquiry are starting points, not issues.
  • One literary work and one non-literary body of work, e.g. The Awakening and an advertising campaign.
  • Choose extracts of up to 40 lines where the issue is shown through choices, and that open out to the whole work.
  • Plan for balance: introduction, each extract and its work in turn, conclusion; signpost and return to the issue.
  • Bring an outline of up to 10 bullet points as prompts, never a script; then 5 minutes of questions.
  • A body of work is a collection of non-literary texts by one creator, studied together; one advert alone is a text.
  • Read the pattern, then the variation, then the cumulative meaning.
  • IO: one literary extract and one extract from a body of work, linked by a global issue.

Key quotations to learn

  • the dual life—that outward existence which conforms, the inward life which questions (Chopin, The Awakening, Ch. VII) Names the split between social role and inner self.
  • She had no dowry, no expectations, no means of being known (Maupassant, "The Necklace" (tr. 1907)) Status decided by money, not by the woman herself.
  • She would so have liked to please, to be envied (Maupassant, "The Necklace" (tr. 1907)) Desire shaped by how others will see her.
  • You are burnt beyond recognition (Chopin, The Awakening, Ch. I) Her body judged by her husband's standard.
  • a valuable piece of personal property which has suffered some damage (Chopin, The Awakening, Ch. I) The narrator's simile: a wife as an owned thing.
  • The rings sparkled upon her fingers. (Chopin, The Awakening, Ch. I) Marriage tokens returned and worn as she laughs with Robert.
