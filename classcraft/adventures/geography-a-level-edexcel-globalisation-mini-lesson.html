Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel A-level Geography (9GE0)

  • 3.1a globalisation as widening and deepening connections, interdependence and flows
  • 3.1b 19th- and 20th-century transport and trade developments (railways, telegraph, steamships; jets, containers): a shrinking world
  • 3.1c 21st-century ICT lowering communication costs; time-space compression
Mission 1

What is globalisation?

  • Globalisation: the growing interdependence of places through flows of commodities, capital, information, migrants and tourists.
  • Widening: more places are connected. Deepening: connections become more intense and frequent.
  • DHL's index: globalisation reached a record 25% in 2022 and stayed close to it through 2025 (scale: 0%, nothing crosses borders, to 100%).
Mission 1

A "shrinking world"

  • Time–space convergence: places get closer in travel or communication time as technology improves.
  • Cost–space convergence: moving goods and information gets cheaper.
  • Time–space compression (David Harvey): faster flows speed up economic and social life.
  • The world is not smaller; the friction of distance falls.
Mission 1

The 19th century: steam, rail and telegraph

  • Steamships: by the late 1830s they were regularly crossing the Atlantic; the Suez Canal's shortcut to Asia made them viable on long routes.
  • Railways: the Stockton and Darlington opened in 1825; almost a million kilometres of rail had been built by 1913.
  • Inland transport costs fell by over 90% between 1800 and 1910.
  • Telegraph: the first successful transatlantic message, August 1858, cut Europe–North America communication from ten days "to a matter of minutes".
Quick check
?What did the 1858 transatlantic telegraph shrink most?
Mission 1

The 20th century: jets and containers

  • Jet aircraft cut intercontinental journeys from days by sea to hours by air.
  • Air: by 1980 the real cost of airfreight had fallen to about a quarter of its level at the start of the Second World War. Air passengers rose from 310 million (1970) to 4,456 million (2019).
  • Containerisation: standard steel boxes move ship to train to truck without unpacking.
  • A study of trade flows, 1962–1990, found "economically large" effects, supporting "containerization being a driver of 20th century economic globalization".
  • Container port traffic: 547 million TEU (2010) to 913 million (2024).
Quick check
?Air passengers rose from 310.4 million (1970) to 4,455.7 million (2019). How many times the 1970 figure is the 2019 figure?
Mission 1

The 21st century: ICT

  • "Thanks to fibre optic cables, satellites and digital technology, the cost of overseas telecommunications is approaching zero".
  • More than 600 active and planned submarine cables; over 1.5 million km in service in early 2026.
  • Internet users: 15.6% of people (2005), 73.6% (2025, estimate).
  • Mobile phones, social networking and electronic banking move information and money instantly: time–space compression.
Mission 1

Connected supply chains are exposed

  • A 2023 drought cut Panama Canal transits: daily crossings fell to 27 in January 2024, against a long-term average of 38.
  • Carriers had to reroute "through the Suez Canal and the Strait of Magellan and around the Cape of Good Hope".
  • About 34% of global seaborne exports of oil pass through the Strait of Hormuz.
Quick check
?What does the Panama Canal drought show about global supply chains?
Mission 1

The world shrinks unevenly

  • Internet use: 94% in high-income countries, 23% in low-income countries; 85% in urban areas, 58% in rural areas.
  • Exporting a container cost $3,204 from a landlocked developing country and $1,268 from a transit country (World Bank figures for 2014).
  • Technology shrinks the world most for rich, urban, coastal places.
Mission check

?A firm in a landlocked low-income country can now email buyers in Europe and be paid in seconds. Its goods still travel by road to a foreign port. Has the world "shrunk" for this firm?

The world has shrunk in time and cost: 14 times as many air passengers in 2019 as in 1970, and about three-quarters of people online. But it has shrunk unevenly (94% online in rich countries, 23% in poor ones), and physical geography and conflict still bite: drought at Panama, security concerns at sea.

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel A-level Geography (9GE0)

  • 3.2a WTO, IMF and World Bank promoted globalisation through free trade and FDI
  • 3.2b national governments promote free trade blocs (EU, ASEAN) and policies of liberalisation, privatisation and business start-ups
  • 3.8b attempts to control globalisation: censorship (China, North Korea), limiting immigration (UK, Japan), trade protectionism
Mission 2

IGOs that accelerated globalisation

  • WTO (since 1 January 1995): 166 members "representing 98 per cent of world trade"; negotiates lower trade barriers and settles trade disputes.
  • IMF (1944; 191 members) and World Bank: loans often came with conditions to open economies to trade and foreign investment.
  • Result: more free trade and more FDI, which reached $1.6 trillion worldwide in 2025.
Mission 2

Governments join trade blocs

  • Free trade area: no tariffs between members. Customs union: plus a common external tariff. Single market: plus free movement of services, capital and people.
  • EU: a single market with "free movement of goods, services, capital and persons".
  • ASEAN: founded in 1967 by five states; 11 members since Timor-Leste joined in October 2025.
  • Governments choose to join; blocs then shape trade and investment flows.
Mission 2

National policies that open economies

  • Liberalisation: cutting tariffs and opening sectors to foreign firms.
  • Privatisation: selling state-owned firms to private investors.
  • Business start-ups: tax breaks, grants and simpler rules for new firms.
  • Example: Shenzhen became China's first special economic zone in August 1980; by 2024 it had 17.98 million residents, and its exports had ranked first among mainland Chinese cities for 32 consecutive years.
Quick check
?A government sells its national airline to private investors. Which policy is this?
Mission 2

Immigration as a political issue

  • Documented immigration: entry and residence with permission, such as a work or study visa.
  • Undocumented immigration: entry or stay without permission.
  • UK net migration peaked at 944,000 in the year to March 2023.
  • Year to June 2025: 49,341 detected irregular arrivals, 43,309 of them by small boat; 95% of small-boat arrivals since 2018 have claimed asylum.
  • Both kinds of flow became central issues in UK politics.
Mission 2

Response 1: the points-based system

  • After Brexit, free movement with the EU ended on 1 January 2021 and a points-based system began.
  • "EU and non-EU citizens will be treated equally".
  • Workers needed 70 points, including a job offer at the required skill level, English, and a salary threshold (generally £25,600).
  • The skill threshold for non-EU workers fell "from RQF6 to RQF3" (degree level to A-level equivalent).
  • Stated aim: to "reduce overall migration numbers".
Mission 2

What happened next

  • Net migration rose to its 944,000 peak (year to March 2023).
  • December 2023: the government announced it would raise the overseas workers' earnings threshold from £26,200 to £38,700 from spring 2024, and stop overseas care workers bringing dependants.
  • Net migration fell to 171,000 in the year to December 2025 (provisional).
  • In that year, 77% of immigrants were non-EU+ nationals: 47% came to study, 23% to work and 14% to seek asylum.
Quick check
?UK net migration fell from 944,000 (year to March 2023) to 171,000 (year to December 2025, provisional). What was the percentage fall?
Mission 2

Response 2: undocumented immigration

  • The Illegal Migration Act 2023 received Royal Assent on 20 July 2023.
  • Its stated purposes include "the removal from the United Kingdom of persons who have entered or arrived in breach of immigration control", detention, and "the maximum number of persons entering the United Kingdom annually using safe and legal routes".
  • Its central duty to remove was never brought into force; sections 1–6 were repealed on 2 December 2025.
  • Small-boat arrivals continued after the Act was passed: the year to June 2025 was only 5% below the 2022 peak.
  • Supporters present such laws as deterrence; critics raise asylum rights. These are each side's positions, not findings.
Mission 2

Other controls on globalisation

  • Censorship: some states, such as China and North Korea, restrict the flow of information, media and online services across their borders.
  • Limiting immigration: the UK's visa rules (above); Edexcel also names Japan.
  • Protectionism: tariffs and quotas on imports. Toyota reports that "in 2025, a significant increase in tariffs on exports to the United States" was announced; Apple warns tariffs "can materially adversely affect" its supply chain.
  • Each control changes the terms of flows; firms, migrants and users adapt.
Mission 2

How far can governments control flows?

  • Who moves: the 2021 rules ended EU free movement and opened routes for non-EU workers.
  • How many: numbers rose to a peak before tighter rules followed, then fell; 2025 data are provisional.
  • Which route: irregular arrivals persisted after the 2023 Act was passed, though its removal duty never came into force.
  • Demand for workers and students, and conflict abroad, keep flows going.
  • Exam move: separate a policy's stated aim from its measured outcome, with dates.
Quick check
?Which conclusion about the UK's immigration controls is best supported?
Mission check

?Another government brings in stricter visa rules and a new law against irregular entry. Based on the UK evidence, what is the most likely result?

Ending free movement changed who could come and opened routes for non-EU workers, but UK net migration still peaked at 944,000 (year to March 2023). Tighter rules followed and it fell to 171,000 (2025, provisional); irregular arrivals persisted after the 2023 Act was passed, though its removal duty never came into force. Governments shape global flows; they rarely switch them off.

Mission 3

Urbanisation and urban growth

  • Urbanisation: a rising share of people living in urban areas. Urban growth: a rising number of urban people.
  • Three engines: natural increase in cities, net migration into cities, and reclassification of rural places as urban.
  • Later stages of the urban cycle: suburbanisation, counter-urbanisation, then urban resurgence (re-urbanisation).
Mission 3

Global patterns since 1950

  • 1950: 20% of the world's 2.5 billion people lived in cities; 40% in towns.
  • 2025: 45% in cities, 36% in towns and 19% in rural areas, of 8.2 billion.
  • Cities overtook towns as the most common setting in 1996.
  • Megacities (10 million+): 8 in 1975, 33 in 2025 (19 in Asia), 37 projected by 2050.
  • Regions differ (2025): cities hold 59% in Northern Africa and Western Asia, 38% in sub-Saharan Africa.
Quick check
?In 2025, 45% of the world's 8.2 billion people lived in cities. About how many people is that?
Mission 3

China opens up

  • 1978: China began its "reform and opening up period". This is the Open Door Policy.
  • Special economic zone (SEZ): an area with lower taxes, cheaper land and fewer trade rules to attract foreign firms. These subsidies and incentives lower costs.
  • Shenzhen was established in March 1979 and became China's first SEZ in August 1980.
  • Foreign direct investment (FDI) into China, net inflows: $0.43 billion (1982); $243.7 billion (2010); $344.1 billion (2021); $42.6 billion (2024).
  • Attitudes to FDI change: inflows rise when a country opens up, and can fall sharply later.
Mission 3

Shenzhen

  • 17.98 million permanent residents at the end of 2024.
  • Under the UN's density-based definition, the Shenzhen city area had about 13.9 million people in 2025: a different boundary, a different number.
  • GDP in 2024: 3.68 trillion yuan, up 5.8% on the year. Exports ranked first among Chinese mainland cities for 32 consecutive years.
  • 2015–2025: Beijing, Guangzhou, Shanghai and Shenzhen each grew by more than 0.8% a year, against 0.1% for China as a whole.
Mission 3

Where China's city growth came from

  • China's urban share (national definition): 20.6% in 1982, 63.8% in 2020.
  • Urban growth, 2010–2020 censuses: 56% rural-to-urban migration, 26% administrative changes, 18% natural increase.
  • Push: low farm incomes, few rural jobs. Pull: factory and service work, higher wages.
  • Migration is selective: mostly young adults. Rural areas face "population ageing and out-migration".
Quick check
?In the UN's breakdown, 26% of China's urban growth in 2010–2020 came from "administrative changes". What does that mean?
Mission 3

Global shift: manufacturing to China

  • Global shift: the movement of production from older industrial countries to newly industrialising ones.
  • China's share of world manufacturing value added: 8.5% (2004), 28.0% (2024), calculated from World Bank data.
  • The World Bank: "China's strong growth relied on investment and export-oriented manufacturing".
Mission 3

Global shift: services to India

  • India's exports of ICT (information and communication technology) services: $5.0 billion (2000), $177.7 billion (2024).
  • Drivers: an English-speaking graduate workforce, lower wages, and fast data links.
  • India is still classed as lower-middle income.
Mission 3

Benefits and costs of the global shift

  • Benefits: since 1978, China's GDP growth "has averaged over 9 percent a year", "lifting almost 800 million people out of extreme poverty".
  • Limits, in China: the World Bank says China now needs a "shift in economic structure from manufacturing to high-value services".
  • Limits, in India: India is lower-middle income; across that income group 81.5% of jobs are still informal. The ICT boom sits beside a mainly informal workforce.
  • Costs, in older industrial regions: manufacturing jobs in the Detroit metro area fell from 380,900 (2000) to 174,000 (2009). Automation and the 2008–09 recession also played a part.
Mission 3

Megacity growth: Mumbai

  • Greater Mumbai: 12,442,373 people in Census 2011. Mumbai is the densest city of 1 million or more, at 27,000 people per km².
  • Growth: rural–urban migrants from "different parts of India" since the 1960s, plus natural increase among a young population.
  • Social challenge: 52.5% lived in slums in the city's tables (41.84% in Praja's count); in M/East ward, infant mortality was 66 per 1,000.
  • Environmental challenge: one landfill receives about 4,500 tonnes a day; M/East's air and groundwater are contaminated.
Quick check
?Which is a cost of the global shift of manufacturing, for the countries that lost it?
Mission check

?A city grows from 1.0 million to 2.0 million people in ten years. Births minus deaths add 0.15 million. Its boundary does not change. Where did most of the extra people come from?

Shenzhen is the same story at a larger scale: it calls itself "a young immigrant city", and across China 56% of urban growth in 2010–2020 came from rural-to-urban migration. The source gives no breakdown for Shenzhen itself.

Mission 4

Globalisation is flows

  • Products: world merchandise exports rose from $3.46 trillion (1990) to $24.62 trillion (2024).
  • Services: $0.88 trillion to $8.88 trillion over the same years.
  • Capital: foreign direct investment into low- and middle-income countries was $462 billion in 2024.
  • Labour: 304 million international migrants in 2024, nearly double the 1990 figure; they sent $665 billion in remittances to low- and middle-income countries in 2024.
  • Information: internet users rose from 15.6% of the world's people (2005) to 73.6% (2025).
Quick check
?World merchandise exports were $3.46 trillion in 1990 and $24.62 trillion in 2024. How many times as large were they in 2024?
Mission 4

Measuring globalisation: the KOF index

  • A globalisation index combines many indicators into one score, so countries can be ranked and tracked.
  • The KOF Globalisation Index (ETH Zurich) covers 195 countries, 1970–2023, using 42 variables.
  • Three dimensions: economic, social and political.
  • Each splits into de facto (actual flows, such as trade or migrants) and de jure (rules that enable flows, such as tariffs or trade agreements).
Quick check
?Which of these would count as a de jure measure of globalisation?
Mission 4

The 2025 ranking

  • 1st Netherlands, 2nd Switzerland, 3rd Belgium, 4th United Kingdom (2023 data, published December 2025).
  • Of 195 countries, the USA ranks 28th, China 82nd and India 94th; Somalia is last.
  • On economic globalisation alone, the USA ranks 67th.
  • On political globalisation, the USA ranks 14th.
Mission 4

Why small, open economies top the table

  • Most KOF variables are ratios: trade in goods is "Exports and imports of goods (% of GDP)".
  • Trade as a share of GDP, 2024: Netherlands 154.0%, USA 25.0%.
  • The DHL index explains why: large economies' "large internal markets reduce the relative share of international flows".
  • Some variables are counts: embassies are an "absolute number", which favours large, powerful states, hence the USA's high political rank.
Mission 4

Other indices, other answers

  • The A.T. Kearney index, named in your specification, follows the same method: choose indicators, weight them and add them into one score.
  • The DHL Global Connectedness Index separates depth (flows compared with domestic activity) from breadth (how widely flows spread around the world).
  • The USA is 39th overall on DHL: 2nd for breadth but 131st of 180 for depth.
  • Limits: the choice of variables and weights decides the winner (KOF: Netherlands; DHL: Singapore); data lag (KOF 2025 measures 2023); a national score hides gaps between connected cities and remote regions.
Quick check
?Why can two globalisation indices rank the same country very differently?
Mission 4

Switched off: North Korea

  • The KOF 2025 rankings list North Korea without an overall score: too little data reaches the outside world to measure it.
  • Political reasons: since 2006, UN Security Council resolution 1718 and later measures have imposed an arms embargo, a ban on luxury goods imports, bans on its exports of coal, iron, iron ore, textiles and seafood, financial restrictions and a travel ban on designated individuals.
  • A place can be switched off by its own government's choices and by others' sanctions.
Mission 4

Switched off: rural Niger

  • Niger ranks 177th of 195 on KOF (score 43.6, against the Netherlands' 88.9).
  • 81.6% of its people live in rural areas (2025); only 10.8% of rural people had electricity (2024).
  • 15.6% of people used the internet in 2024, against 71.2% worldwide.
  • GNI per person was $750 in 2025.
  • Environmental: Niger lies "at the heart of the Sahel"; the World Bank lists "climate shocks" among its risks.
  • Physical: landlocked, far from ports. Economic: poverty limits investment in roads, power and phones. Little power and little internet keep flows of information, services and capital small.
Quick check
?Which is a physical reason why rural Niger remains largely switched off from globalisation?
Mission check

?Singapore's trade is far smaller in dollars than the USA's, yet Singapore ranks 1st on KOF economic globalisation and the USA 67th. Why?

The same explains the hook. KOF mostly measures flows relative to size (% of GDP, per person). The Netherlands' trade was 154% of its GDP in 2024; the USA's 25%. The USA trades huge amounts in total, but its home market is so large that international flows are a small share of it.

Mission 5

How TNCs organise production

  • A transnational corporation (TNC) operates in more than one country.
  • Foreign direct investment (FDI): a firm builds or buys a business abroad and keeps a lasting say in running it.
  • Outsourcing: contracting another firm to do part of the work. Offshoring: moving work to another country, owned or contracted.
  • Global production networks: design, parts, assembly and sales spread across many countries and firms.
Mission 5

Apple: outsourcing at global scale

  • Headquarters: Cupertino, California; about 166,000 full-time equivalent employees (September 2025).
  • "A significant majority" of manufacturing is done by outsourcing partners located mainly in China mainland, India, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan and Vietnam.
  • Partners "primarily located in Asia" carry out final assembly of "substantially all" its hardware.
  • Apple keeps design, software, services and marketing.
Mission 5

Apple: markets, gains and risks

  • Net sales, year to September 2025: $416.2 billion; Greater China $64.4 billion, a large new market.
  • Gain: these arrangements "can lower operating costs".
  • Risk: they "reduce the Company's direct control over production and distribution".
  • Tariffs and trade controls "can materially adversely affect the Company's business and supply chain".
Quick check
?Which is an example of FDI rather than outsourcing?
Mission 5

Toyota: spatial organisation

  • Headquarters in Japan. Toyota and its affiliates run more than 50 overseas manufacturing organisations in 27 countries and regions.
  • Vehicle production, year to March 2026 (thousands): Japan 4,149; North America 2,050; Asia 1,802; Europe 815; other 477; total 9,293.
  • Employees, 31 March 2026: 390,927, of whom 207,137 in Japan, 66,331 in Asia, 65,411 in North America and 24,741 in Europe.
Mission 5

Toyota: production and linkages

  • The Toyota Production System: Just in Time, where "each process produces only what is needed for the next process in a continuous flow", and Jidoka, stopping work when a problem occurs.
  • Low stocks need reliable suppliers close to the plant: strong local linkages.
  • In Asia, Toyota has been "developing relationships with local suppliers".
  • Building near customers lets it match local costs to local sales, reducing currency risk.
Quick check
?Why does Just in Time production encourage suppliers to locate near Toyota's plants?
Mission 5

Toyota: trading and marketing

  • Sells in about 200 countries and regions; 9.6 million vehicles in the year to March 2026.
  • Glocalisation: Toyota designs for local "road conditions, climate, and values", an approach it calls "Best in town".
  • Share of vehicles sold that were made in the same region: North America 74.6%, Europe 69.0%, Asia 96.4%.
  • Bulky goods such as cars cost a lot to ship, so building near buyers pays; small, light goods such as phones can be assembled in a few places and shipped worldwide.
  • Building inside a market reduces exposure to tariffs: Toyota notes that "in 2025, a significant increase in tariffs on exports to the United States" was announced.
Mission 5

The global shift of manufacturing: China

  • China's share of world manufacturing value added: 8.5% in 2004 (first year in the data), 28.0% in 2024 (our calculation).
  • In 2010 China (18.0%) passed the USA (16.7%) (our calculation).
  • TNCs drove much of this, through FDI and outsourcing: Apple's partners, including those in China mainland, do its final assembly mainly in Asia.
  • Benefits: waged work, infrastructure investment, poverty reduction, training. Costs: growing inequality, loss of farmland, unplanned settlements, pollution and resource pressure.
Mission 5

Outsourcing services: India

  • TNCs in developed countries outsource services to India: IT support, software, call centres, accounting.
  • Pull factors: many English-speaking graduates, lower wages, and time zones that allow round-the-clock work.
  • India is now also on Apple's list of main manufacturing partner locations.
  • Benefits: skilled urban jobs, export earnings, new infrastructure. Costs: gains concentrated in a few cities; rising inequality between urban graduates and rural workers.
Mission check

?A bicycle maker (bulky products, sold mainly in Europe) and a headphone brand (small, light products, sold worldwide) are choosing production strategies. Which pairing is best supported by this mission?

Apple keeps design and outsources assembly to partners mainly in Asia: lower costs, less control. Toyota uses FDI, building where it sells, with nearby suppliers for Just in Time. Product size, control and trade policy explain the difference, and both strategies network places and markets.

Make a guess: In 2006, more than a third of China's sulfur dioxide emissions came from making goods for other countries. Whose pollution is it?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel A-level Geography (9GE0)

  • 3.4b communities in developing countries face major environmental problems (air and water pollution, land degradation, over-exploitation, biodiversity loss) affecting health and wellbeing
Mission 6

The global shift and pollution havens

  • From the 1970s much manufacturing moved from high-income countries to emerging and developing economies, drawn by lower costs.
  • Pollution haven hypothesis: polluting industries move to places with weaker environmental rules. Evidence for it is mixed, because costs, markets and skills also drive location.
  • Either way, the pollution moved with the factories, and so did the health effects.
Mission 6

China, 2006: pollution for export

  • Lin et al. (2014): in 2006, 36% of China's anthropogenic sulfur dioxide, 27% of its nitrogen oxides, 22% of carbon monoxide and 17% of black carbon were associated with producing goods for export.
  • About 21% of those export-related emissions were linked to exports to the US.
  • China gained jobs and export earnings; people living near the factories breathed the air.
Mission 6

The health cost

  • Zhang et al. (2017): of 3.45 million premature deaths linked to fine-particle (PM2.5) pollution in 2007, about 22% (762,400) were associated with goods and services produced in one region for consumption in another.
  • Consumption in western Europe and the USA was linked to more than 108,600 premature deaths in China.
  • These are model estimates combining four global models, not counted deaths.
Quick check
?Zhang et al. link 108,600 premature deaths in China in 2007 to consumption in western Europe and the USA. What does this figure measure?
Mission 6

Whose pollution? Two ways to count

  • Production-based (territorial) accounting: emissions belong to the country where goods are made.
  • Consumption-based accounting: emissions belong to the country where goods are bought and used.
  • Peters et al.: net emission transfers through trade from developing to developed countries rose from 0.4 Gt CO₂ (1990) to 1.6 Gt CO₂ (2008), more than the Kyoto Protocol's emission reductions.
Quick check
?Under consumption-based accounting, who is responsible for emissions from a Chinese factory making phones sold in the US?
Mission 6

Land degradation and haze: Indonesia, 2015

  • More than 2.6 million hectares of forest, peat and other land burned in Indonesia in 2015; "fires are lit to clear and/or prepare land for agriculture".
  • Peatland fires "cause up to 90% of the haze"; the smoke "causes widespread respiratory, eye, and skin ailments", across Indonesia and in Malaysia and Singapore.
  • The World Bank's early estimate of the cost: more than US$16 billion, about 1.8% of Indonesia's GDP.
Mission 6

Biodiversity loss and land: the Brazilian Amazon

  • Soy for global markets: soybean area in the Brazilian Amazon rose more than 10-fold, from 0.4 to 4.6 million hectares, between 2000 and 2019.
  • Across South America, 9% of forest loss was converted to soy by 2016; much soy expanded onto pasture, pushing cattle further into the forest.
  • Streams in soya bean catchments ran average daily maxima about 3 °C warmer than in forested ones: degraded habitat for stream life.
Mission 6

Wellbeing: who bears the costs?

  • Health: respiratory illness from factory and fire smoke (China, Indonesia).
  • Livelihoods: deforestation can reduce rainfall over large areas, cutting the farm incomes that clearance was meant to raise.
  • Distribution: the costs fall locally and often on poorer communities; the benefits (cheaper goods, feed and food) are spread among distant consumers.
Quick check
?Which is an example of land degradation in a developing country linked to global demand?
Mission 6

Responses

  • Carbon leakage: production moving to places with weaker climate rules, so emissions shift rather than fall.
  • The EU's Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism aims to ensure "the price of imports reflects their embedded carbon emissions"; it has applied in its definitive form since 1 January 2026.
  • National rules in producing countries (fire bans, emission limits) and buyers' supply-chain commitments can also act.
Mission 6

Exam move: judging responsibility

  • Name the place harmed and the community (factory districts, forest-edge villages).
  • Name the driver: global demand, foreign investment, weak regulation.
  • Then judge responsibility: production-based, consumption-based, or shared. Strong answers explain why the choice of method changes the answer.
Mission check

?In 2022 the world produced an estimated 62 million tonnes of e-waste, and only 22.3% was documented as formally collected and recycled. WHO says people in low- and middle-income countries face the greatest risks, and e-waste still moves to them, "frequently illegally". Whose problem is it?

Both. Pollution emitted in China, smoke from Indonesian land clearance and toxic e-waste recycling harm local communities in developing countries, while much of the demand comes from elsewhere. Production-based accounting blames the place; consumption-based accounting blames the buyers.

Mission 7

Moving out from the centre

  • Suburbanisation: people, then shops and offices, move from the inner city to the suburbs. Drivers: cars and commuter rail, cheaper land, more space.
  • Counter-urbanisation: people move beyond the urban edge, to small towns and villages. Drivers: retirement, quality of life, remote working.
  • In parts of Europe, city populations have fallen with suburbanisation or counter-urbanisation, and with boundary reclassification.
Quick check
?A retired couple sell their flat in central Leeds and buy a cottage in a village 50 km away. Which movement is this?
Mission 7

London: in and out at once

  • In the year to mid-2025 London had positive natural change and net international inward migration.
  • But "this was more than offset by net outward migration to other areas of the UK".
  • London's population fell by 0.29%, "the first decrease since the year to mid-2021".
  • A city can gain from births and arrivals while losing more people outwards.
Mission 7

Deindustrialisation and the rise of services

  • Deindustrialisation: a long-term fall in manufacturing jobs and output in a place.
  • Causes: the global shift of factories to lower-cost countries, automation, firms relocating, and recessions.
  • Service economy: UK employment in services rose from 66.8% (1991) to 83.0% (2024); industry fell from 30.8% to 16.1% (ILO modelled).
Mission 7

Detroit's population

  • US census counts for Detroit city:
    • 465,766 (1910); 1,849,568 (1950, the 5th largest US city); 1,027,974 (1990); 713,777 (2010); 639,111 (2020).
  • Most of the loss came early: 821,594 of the 1,210,457 people lost by 2020 had gone by 1990 (our calculation).
Mission 7

Detroit: the jobs left

  • Historian Thomas Sugrue told the Associated Press that Detroit lost 140,000 jobs between 1947 and 1963; "The postwar manufacturing boom was largely over by the mid-1950s" (reported by HNN).
  • Manufacturing jobs in the Detroit metro area: 380,900 (2000), 174,000 (2009).
  • People who could move for work, often younger and better paid, left first: selective out-migration.
Quick check
?By what percentage did Detroit city's population fall between 1950 (1,849,568) and 2020 (639,111)?
Mission 7

A deindustrialised city

  • Depopulation: down about 65% from 1950 to 2020.
  • Unemployment: the metro area's unemployment rate averaged 14.6% in 2009 (calculated from monthly data).
  • Low incomes: median household income (2020–24, in 2024 dollars) $39,938, about half the US $80,734.
  • Housing: 50.3% of homes owner-occupied, against 65.2% in the USA.
  • Edexcel's list also includes dereliction (empty factories and homes), contamination of old industrial land, and crime.
Mission 7

The spiral of decline

  • Factory closes → jobs lost → less local spending → shops and services close → those who can, leave → smaller tax base → fewer services, more dereliction → investors stay away → more closures.
  • England's index finds deprivation concentrated in "areas that have historically had large heavy industry manufacturing and/or mining sectors".
  • Edexcel calls this a spiral of decline, triggered by economic restructuring.
Quick check
?In the spiral of decline, why does a smaller tax base make things worse?
Mission 7

Signs of change?

  • Detroit's estimated population for July 2025 is 649,095, above the 2020 census count.
  • Metro manufacturing jobs were 237,900 in 2025: above 2009, far below 2000.
  • Caution: 2025 is an estimate; the jobs series covers the much larger metro area and starts only in 1990.
  • Exam move: separate structural decline (decades) from cyclical swings (years).
Mission check

?A northern English town loses its last large factory. Over 20 years its population falls, while commuter villages 20 km away grow. Its boundary and birth rate do not change. What best explains the town's decline?

Detroit lost about 65% of its people from 1950 to 2020, two-thirds of that loss before 1990, as factory jobs left from the 1950s and people moved out. Deindustrialisation and centrifugal movement (moving outwards from the centre) worked together.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Globalisation widens and deepens flows; steamships, railways and the telegraph (19th century), then jets and containers (20th century), then ICT (21st century) produced a shrinking world and time–space compression.

Mission 2

  • IGOs, trade blocs and national policies (liberalisation, privatisation, start-ups) accelerated globalisation; censorship, immigration limits and protectionism try to control it.
  • The UK case: a points-based system (2021), later tightening (2023–24) and the Illegal Migration Act 2023 changed who came and how; numbers depended on more than rules.

Mission 3

  • Urbanisation (share) differs from urban growth (number); three engines: natural increase, migration, reclassification.
  • The Open Door Policy and SEZs drew FDI; manufacturing shifted to China and ICT services to India; Mumbai grew by migration and natural increase.

Mission 4

  • Globalisation is flows of products, services, capital, labour and information, all growing since 1990.
  • Indices such as KOF combine many variables; because most are ratios to GDP, small, open economies top the table.
  • Different indices (KOF, A.T. Kearney, DHL) give different rankings; North Korea and rural Niger stay switched off for political, physical and economic reasons.

Mission 5

  • TNCs organise production through FDI (Toyota: plants in 27 countries and regions, 47% of staff outside Japan) or outsourcing (Apple: partners mainly in Asia); both create linkages, new markets and risks.
  • The global shift moved manufacturing to China (28.0% of world manufacturing value added in 2024) and services outsourcing to India, with benefits and costs.

Mission 6

  • The global shift moved polluting production, and its health costs, to emerging and developing economies: in 2006, 36% of China's SO₂ came from export production.
  • Land clearance for global agribusiness degraded land, cut biodiversity and harmed health (Indonesia's haze; the Brazilian Amazon). Who is responsible depends on how emissions are counted.

Mission 7

  • Deindustrialisation (global shift, automation) can trigger a spiral of decline: depopulation, unemployment, low incomes.
