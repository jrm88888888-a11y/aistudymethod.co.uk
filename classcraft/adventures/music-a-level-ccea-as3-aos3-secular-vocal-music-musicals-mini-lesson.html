Secular Vocal Music (Musicals)

This is one of the three compulsory Areas of Study in CCEA AS 3 (Responding to Music). You listen to and appraise numbers from musical theatre from 1900 to the present day, commenting on song structure, style influences, rhythm and harmony, texture and the crucial relationship between music and drama.

CCEA sets numbers from shows such as Showboat, Carousel, West Side Story and Les Miserables as examples of these styles. Your focus is the listening and analysis skills — how songs are built and how music tells the story.

Key idea: in a musical, songs do dramatic work — they reveal character, express emotion and drive the plot forward.

Context · the musical

Numbers and their influences

A musical is built from different kinds of "number". Learn the vocabulary:

  • Solo song and the soliloquy — a character alone, thinking aloud in music.
  • Duet and larger ensemble numbers, where several characters sing together, sometimes with independent lines combined.
  • Chorus numbers for the full company.

The style of the songs draws on several traditions: operetta (light, lyrical, European), and the twin American influences of blues and jazz, which bring syncopation, swing and colourful harmony.

Continuity and change: the musical grew across the twentieth century from operetta-flavoured shows towards jazz-infused, dramatically integrated works where song and story are inseparable.

Structure · song forms

How show songs are built

Many classic musical-theatre songs share standard shapes:

  • 32-bar AABA form — four eight-bar sections: the main tune (A), repeated (A), a contrasting middle "bridge" or "release" (B), then the main tune again (A).
  • Verse-and-chorus — an introductory verse (freer, storytelling) leads into the memorable chorus (the "refrain").
  • Strophic — the same music repeated for several verses of new words.
  • Reprise — an earlier song returns later in the show, often changed in mood to reflect the drama.

Climax trick: composers often modulate up a semitone or tone for the final chorus to lift the emotional temperature.

Elements · rhythm & the jazz feel

Syncopation, swing and blue notes

The rhythmic and harmonic "feel" of many show tunes comes straight from jazz and blues:

  • Syncopation — accents placed on weak beats or off-beats, pushing against the pulse to create drive and swing.
  • Swing rhythm — pairs of quavers played long-short, giving a relaxed, lilting groove.
  • Blue notes — flattened third, fifth or seventh degrees of the scale that bend the melody expressively.
  • Extended/coloured chords — sevenths, ninths and added-note chords, plus jazzy ii–V–I progressions.

Feel the difference: a straight, on-the-beat tune sounds square; syncopation and swing make it groove.

Harmony · cadences

Cadences and harmony in songs

Show songs still use the four standard cadences to punctuate their phrases:

  • Perfect — V–I, a firm, satisfying close, often ending the chorus.
  • Imperfect — ends on V, leaving a phrase hanging, ready for the next line.
  • Plagal — IV–I, a softer, warmer close.
  • Interrupted — V–vi, a surprise that can heighten a dramatic moment.

Jazz colour: the harmony is often enriched with seventh and ninth chords and the ii–V–I progression, but the underlying cadence points are still the ones you know.

Elements · texture & ensemble

From solo song to combined ensemble

Musicals use a range of textures, and the great ensemble numbers are a highlight:

  • Homophonic — a solo melody over accompaniment: the standard song texture.
  • Contrapuntal ensemble — several characters sing different melodies at the same time, their independent lines combined into one number. This lets several viewpoints be heard at once.
  • Antiphony / call and response — a soloist and chorus (or two groups) answering each other.
  • A reprise may re-texture an earlier tune for new dramatic weight.

Listen for the layering: when a quintet combines several tunes at once, that is contrapuntal writing — a powerful dramatic device.

Music & drama

How music tells the story

The most examinable idea in this Area of Study is the relationship between music and drama. Composers use music to:

  • Characterise — give a character a distinctive melodic or rhythmic style.
  • Express emotion — through tempo, dynamics, register and harmony.
  • Use a leitmotif — a recurring theme attached to a character, place or idea, so its return carries meaning.
  • Deploy musical symbolism and dramatic gesture — a sudden key change, silence, or orchestral stab to mark a turning point; underscoring keeps music running beneath spoken dialogue.

Appraising · putting it together

Listening like an examiner

When you appraise a number from a musical, cover four angles:

  • Structure — AABA, verse-chorus, strophic; is there a reprise or a key change for the climax?
  • Style — operetta lyricism, or jazz/blues syncopation, swing and blue notes?
  • Texture — solo song, duet, or a contrapuntal ensemble combining independent lines?
  • Drama — how does the music characterise, express emotion or use leitmotif and gesture to serve the story?

Always justify. "Jazz-influenced, because of the swing rhythm, blue notes and seventh-chord harmony, used to characterise the mood of the scene."
