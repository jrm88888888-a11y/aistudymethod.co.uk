Make a guess: Muslims pray at set times, in set words, facing one place. Is there room left for the heart?

Mission 1

Salah within ibadah

  • Ibadah (worship, service): any lawful act done for God's sake. "Truly, my prayer and my service of sacrifice, my life and my death, are (all) for Allah" (Qur'an 6:162, Yusuf Ali).
  • Salah: the fixed ritual prayer, five times a day; the second pillar.
  • The five daily prayers: Fajr (dawn), Zuhr (after midday), Asr (afternoon), Maghrib (after sunset), Isha (night).
  • Other prayer surrounds it: nafila (extra), tahajjud (night prayer), du'a (personal calling on God), tasbih (glorifying), wird (Sufi litany), dhikr (remembrance).
Mission 1

Preparing to pray

  • Wudu (washing): "wash your faces, and your hands (and arms) to the elbows; Rub your heads (with water); and (wash) your feet to the ankles" (Qur'an 5:6, Yusuf Ali).
  • Ghusl (full washing) after major impurity; tayammum (dry earth) when there is no water (5:6).
  • Niyyah (intention) in the heart: this prayer, for God.
  • Face the qiblah, the Ka'bah in Makkah (2:144); a clean place; modest covering.
  • The adhan (call) and iqamah (call to stand) begin congregational prayer.
  • Purity of body points to purity of heart.
Mission 1

One rak'ah

  • Rak'ah: one cycle of set words and positions.
  • Takbir ("Allahu akbar": God is greatest) opens the prayer.
  • Qiyam (standing): al-Fatiha in Arabic; in the first two rak'ahs, another passage of the Qur'an follows.
  • Ruku' (bowing), then standing again.
  • Sujud (prostration) twice: "bow down in adoration, and bring thyself the closer (to Allah)!" (Qur'an 96:19, Yusuf Ali). A hadith says a servant is closest to God in prostration (Muslim; paraphrase).
  • Tashahhud (sitting, with the shahadah) and taslim ("peace be on you") to right and left end the prayer.

Mission 1Diversity

Sunni and Shi'a salah

  • Same pillar, same five prayers, same core words and movements.
  • Times: many Shi'a combine Zuhr with Asr and Maghrib with Isha, praying at three times a day; Sunnis combine mainly when travelling.
  • Hands: most Sunnis fold them (Hanafi, Shafi'i, Hanbali); Shi'a, and many Maliki Sunnis, pray with arms at their sides.
  • Prostration: Shi'a place the forehead on earth or something natural, often a small clay turbah (frequently from Karbala). It is prostrated on, not to.
  • Wudu: Shi'a wipe the feet; Sunnis wash them. Both cite 5:6.
  • Adhan: Shi'a add "Come to the best of deeds" and often a witness to Ali.
Mission 1

Jumu'ah, the Friday prayer

> "O ye who believe! When the call is proclaimed to prayer on Friday (the Day of Assembly), hasten earnestly to the Remembrance of Allah, and leave off business (and traffic)" (Qur'an 62:9, Yusuf Ali)

  • Replaces Zuhr on Friday: a khutbah (sermon, in two parts) and two rak'ahs in congregation.
  • After it: "disperse through the land, and seek of the Bounty of Allah" (62:10): Friday is not a day of rest.
  • Sunni law: a duty for adult men who are resident and well; women may attend.
  • Twelver Shi'a: in the Imam's absence (occultation), many jurists hold Friday prayer may be prayed instead of Zuhr rather than required; Iran revived it publicly after 1979.
  • Purpose: unity of the ummah, teaching, news of the community.
Mission 1

The imam

  • The imam leads congregational prayer: chosen for knowledge of the Qur'an and good character (a hadith in Muslim gives this order).
  • Any qualified Muslim may lead; women may lead other women (the majority view).
  • Not a priest: no sacraments, no go-between. Every Muslim prays directly to God.
  • In Britain many mosques employ an imam to lead prayers, give the khutbah, teach, conduct marriages and funerals, and act as chaplain.
  • Persian- and Urdu-speaking Shi'a often call a prayer leader pish-namaz; Imam with a capital (the twelve Imams) means something different.
Mission 1

Other prayer

  • Du'a: "When My servants ask thee concerning Me, I am indeed close (to them): I listen to the prayer of every suppliant when he calleth on Me" (Qur'an 2:186, Yusuf Ali). Any language, any time.
  • Tahajjud: voluntary night prayer, "an additional prayer (or spiritual profit) for thee" (Qur'an 17:79, Yusuf Ali).
  • Nafila: extra prayers, many linked to the five (sunnah prayers before or after them).
  • Tasbih: glorifying God, e.g. after salah, often with prayer beads: SubhanAllah, Alhamdulillah, Allahu akbar (Shi'a call it the tasbih of Fatimah).
  • Wird: a daily litany given by a Sufi shaykh to his followers.

Mission 1

Outward act, inward state

  • "The believers must (eventually) win through,- Those who humble themselves in their prayers" (Qur'an 23:1–2, Yusuf Ali). Khushu': humble attention.
  • Warning: "So woe to the worshippers Who are neglectful of their prayers" (Qur'an 107:4–5, Yusuf Ali).
  • Ihsan: worship God as if you see Him; if you do not see Him, He sees you (Hadith of Jibril, Muslim; paraphrase).
  • Effect on life: "Prayer restrains from shameful and unjust deeds" (Qur'an 29:45, Yusuf Ali).
  • The set form supports the heart: the body bows so the self learns to bow.

Mission 2

The fifth pillar

  • Hajj: pilgrimage to Makkah, 8–13 Dhu'l-Hijjah, the last month of the lunar year.
  • "Pilgrimage thereto is a duty men owe to Allah,- those who can afford the journey" (Qur'an 3:97, Yusuf Ali).
  • Once in a lifetime, for adult Muslims who are able: health, money (debts paid, family provided for) and a safe route.
  • Origins: Ibrahim and Isma'il raised the foundations of the Ka'bah (2:127); God told Ibrahim to "proclaim the Pilgrimage among men" (22:27).
  • The Prophet made one Hajj, the Farewell Hajj (10 AH, 632). Its rites are the model.
  • Umrah: the "lesser pilgrimage", at any time, with fewer rites; it does not replace Hajj.
Mission 2

Ihram: the state of consecration

  • At the miqat (boundary points) pilgrims enter ihram with an intention.
  • Men wear two plain white unstitched cloths; women wear simple modest clothes, face and hands uncovered.
  • Forbidden in ihram: perfume, cutting hair or nails, hunting, sexual relations, quarrelling (2:197: "no obscenity, nor wickedness, nor wrangling in the Hajj").
  • The talbiyah: "Here I am, O God, here I am; You have no partner; praise, blessing and sovereignty are Yours."
  • Meaning: rich and poor look the same; ihram is often compared to the burial shroud: the pilgrim leaves the world behind.
Mission 2

The rites, day by day

  • Arrival: tawaf, seven circuits of the Ka'bah anticlockwise; sa'y, seven times between the hills of Safa and Marwah.
  • 8th: to Mina, the tent city.
  • 9th: 'Arafat: wuquf (standing) in prayer from midday to sunset; the Prophet's Farewell Sermon was given here. Night at Muzdalifah, gathering pebbles.
  • The standing at 'Arafat is often seen as a rehearsal for the Day of Judgement: all stand equal before God, asking forgiveness.
  • 10th (Eid-ul-Adha): stoning the largest pillar (Jamrat al-Aqabah); sacrifice; men shave or trim, women cut a lock; tawaf al-ifadah and sa'y.
  • 11th–13th: at Mina, stoning all three jamarat each day.
  • Farewell tawaf before leaving. Many also visit the Prophet's Mosque in Madinah (not part of Hajj).

Signs of God

  • Sa'y recalls Hajar, Ibrahim's wife, running between the hills to find water for the baby Isma'il; the spring of Zamzam appeared.

Mission 2

What Hajj means

  • Tawhid: the whole journey answers God's call ("Here I am").
  • Ibrahim's faith: his family's obedience is re-lived at the Ka'bah, Safa and Marwah, the jamarat and in the sacrifice.
  • Equality: "the most honoured of you in the sight of Allah is (he who is) the most righteous of you" (Qur'an 49:13, Yusuf Ali).
  • Unity of the ummah: Muslims of every nation, in one place, one dress, one prayer.
  • Judgement and forgiveness: a Hajj done without wrongdoing returns the pilgrim "as on the day his mother bore him" (Bukhari; paraphrase).
  • Malcolm X (1925–1965), on Hajj in 1964, wrote that seeing pilgrims of every colour as brothers changed his view of race (letter from Makkah; paraphrase).
Mission 2

Hajj in the 20th century

  • Rule of the Hijaz: Ibn Saud took Makkah and Madinah (1924–25); the Kingdom of Saudi Arabia (1932) took charge of the Hajj.
  • Travel: the Hejaz Railway reached Madinah in 1908 but was wrecked in the First World War; from the mid-century, air travel replaced months-long journeys by land and sea.
  • Building: major expansions of the Grand Mosque from the 1950s; a Jamarat bridge (1963; rebuilt on several levels from 2006); fire-resistant tents at Mina after a fire in 1997.
  • Management: country quotas from 1988 (about one pilgrim per thousand Muslims); visas; health rules.
  • Tensions: the 1979 seizure of the Grand Mosque; a deadly clash with Iranian pilgrims in 1987; crushes in crowds.
  • Heritage: Saudi authorities demolished many historic sites (e.g. tombs at al-Baqi', 1925–26), seeing them as risks of shirk; many Shi'a and some Sunnis regard this as a grievous loss.

Mission 2Diversity

Sunni and Shi'a pilgrims

  • Sunni and Shi'a pilgrims perform the same Hajj together, at the same places and times.
  • Twelver Shi'a add tawaf al-nisa', a further full tawaf (seven circuits, with its two-rak'ah prayer) needed before marital relations are lawful again.
  • Small differences in prayer (see Mission 1) continue during Hajj.
  • Shi'a also value ziyarah (visiting) to the shrines of the Prophet's family: Karbala, Najaf, Mashhad. These are highly honoured but never replace Hajj.
  • Politics have touched Hajj: e.g. Iran's boycott after 1987; disputes over the treatment of Shi'a pilgrims and holy sites.

Mission 3

Sawm: the duty

  • Sawm (fasting): the fourth pillar, kept through the month of Ramadan, the ninth month of the lunar calendar.
  • "O ye who believe! Fasting is prescribed to you as it was prescribed to those before you, that ye may (learn) self-restraint" (Qur'an 2:183, Yusuf Ali).
  • From dawn to sunset: no food, drink or sexual relations (2:187: "until the white thread of dawn appear to you distinct from its black thread").
  • Niyyah (intention) for the fast; suhur (pre-dawn meal); iftar (breaking the fast), often with dates and water, as the Prophet did.
  • Origins: prescribed in Madinah in 2 AH (624).
  • Because the lunar year is shorter, Ramadan moves about 11 days earlier each year: sometimes winter, sometimes summer.
Mission 3

Who fasts?

  • Required of adult Muslims who are sane, healthy and not travelling.
  • Make up later: the sick and travellers (2:184–185); women who are menstruating or recovering from childbirth (from hadith).
  • Fidya: those who can never fast (e.g. long-term illness, frailty in old age) feed a needy person for each day missed (2:184).
  • Pregnant and breastfeeding women may not fast if they fear harm; the schools differ on whether they make up, pay fidya, or both.
  • Children often practise with part-days.
  • Fasting that harms health is not required: the rule is God's ease (2:185).

Mission 3

The nights of Ramadan

  • Many Muslims recite the whole Qur'an during the month.
  • Laylat al-Qadr (the Night of Power), when the Qur'an's revelation began: "The Night of Power is better than a thousand months." (Qur'an 97:3, Yusuf Ali). Sought in the last ten nights.
  • Sunnis often mark the 27th night; Shi'a give special weight to the 19th, 21st and 23rd, which also recall Ali's wounding and death in Ramadan (40 AH).
  • Tarawih: Sunnis pray these extra night prayers in congregation at the mosque.
  • Twelver Shi'a pray extra Ramadan night prayers individually, regarding congregational tarawih as a later practice (introduced under the caliph Umar).
  • I'tikaf: retreat in the mosque in the last ten days.
Mission 3

Eid-ul-Fitr

  • 1 Shawwal: the "festival of breaking the fast". Fasting on Eid day is forbidden.
  • Zakat al-fitr paid before the Eid prayer: a purification for the one who fasted and food for the poor (Abu Dawud; paraphrase).
  • Eid prayer in congregation, often in a park or large hall: two rak'ahs and a sermon; takbir on the way.
  • New clothes, gifts for children, visiting family, graves and neighbours; "Eid Mubarak".
  • Social importance: the poor are included; unity; brotherhood of the ummah.
Mission 3

Eid-ul-Adha

  • 10 Dhu'l-Hijjah, during the Hajj: the "festival of sacrifice", celebrated by all Muslims, not only pilgrims.
  • Recalls Ibrahim's readiness to sacrifice his son: "O my son! I see in vision that I offer thee in sacrifice" (Qur'an 37:102, Yusuf Ali); "We ransomed him with a momentous sacrifice" (Qur'an 37:107, Yusuf Ali).
  • The Qur'an does not name the son; most Muslims say Isma'il.
  • Qurbani (udhiyah): a sheep, goat, cow or camel sacrificed; the meat is often shared in thirds: family, friends, the poor. Many British Muslims pay charities to sacrifice abroad.
  • "It is not their meat nor their blood, that reaches Allah: it is your piety that reaches Him" (Qur'an 22:37, Yusuf Ali).

Mission 4

What Muslims believe the Qur'an is

  • Qur'an means "recitation": a text meant to be heard and recited.
  • Muslims believe it is kalam Allah, the speech of God, not the words of Muhammad.
  • Revealed in Arabic, through the angel Jibril, to Muhammad, over about 23 years (610–632 CE).
  • Muhammad is the recipient and messenger, not the author.
  • It is the final revelation, confirming and completing earlier scriptures.
Mission 4

Wahy: revelation through Jibril

  • Wahy is the revelation God gives to a prophet.
  • Jibril is the medium: "for he brings down the (revelation) to thy heart by Allah's will" (Qur'an 2:97, Yusuf Ali).
  • "With it came down the spirit of Faith and Truth- To thy heart and mind, that thou mayest admonish. In the perspicuous Arabic tongue." (Qur'an 26:193–195, Yusuf Ali)
  • How it came (Bukhari, paraphrase of Aisha's report): sometimes like the ringing of a bell, the hardest form for him; sometimes the angel came as a man and spoke.
  • Companions saw him sweat on a cold day as revelation came.
Mission 4

Revealed in stages

  • "(It is) a Qur'an which We have divided (into parts from time to time), in order that thou mightest recite it to men at intervals: We have revealed it by stages." (Qur'an 17:106, Yusuf Ali)
  • Makkans asked why it did not come "all at once"; the reply: "that We may strengthen thy heart thereby" (Qur'an 25:32, Yusuf Ali).
  • Reasons Muslims give: to strengthen the Prophet; to answer events as they happened (asbab al-nuzul, occasions of revelation); to make memorising easy; to bring in laws step by step.
  • And the Night of Power? Many scholars, following Ibn Abbas, say the whole Qur'an came down to the lowest heaven that night, then to Muhammad in stages.

Mission 4

Memorised and written in the Prophet's lifetime

  • Revelation was first memorised: companions who learned it all were called huffaz (singular hafiz).
  • Scribes, such as Zayd ibn Thabit, wrote verses down as they came: on palm stalks, flat stones, shoulder bones, leather and parchment.
  • Muslims believe the Prophet checked the recitation, and Jibril reviewed it with him each Ramadan.
  • At his death (632) there was no single bound copy: revelation had continued almost to the end.
Mission 4

Abu Bakr and Uthman

  • Abu Bakr (caliph 632–634): many huffaz were killed at the battle of Yamamah. Urged by Umar, he asked Zayd ibn Thabit to gather the written pieces, checked against memory and witnesses.
  • The result, suhuf (sheets), passed to Umar, then to his daughter Hafsah, a widow of the Prophet.
  • Uthman (caliph 644–656), c. 650: Muslims in distant provinces recited differently. A committee led by Zayd copied Hafsah's sheets into a standard mushaf (written copy) in the Quraysh dialect.
  • Copies went to the main cities; other copies were destroyed. Today's Qur'an follows this Uthmanic text.
  • Later, dots and vowel signs were added to make the script easier to read. Recognised readings (qira'at) remain, e.g. Hafs (most widespread) and Warsh (North and West Africa).

Make a guess: Why do Muslims who don't speak Arabic still learn to recite the Qur'an in Arabic?

Mission 5

An Arabic Qur'an

  • "We have sent it down as an Arabic Qur'an, in order that ye may learn wisdom." (Qur'an 12:2, Yusuf Ali)
  • Muslims believe the Arabic words and sounds themselves are revealed, not only the ideas.
  • I'jaz (inimitability): Muslims hold no human could produce its like: "they could not produce the like thereof" (Qur'an 17:88, Yusuf Ali).
  • So Muslims everywhere learn to recite in Arabic, even without understanding every word. Salah is always in Arabic.
  • Arabic unites a worldwide ummah: the same words in Jakarta, Lagos and Leeds.
Mission 5

Translation as interpretation

  • Every translation must choose between meanings, so every translation is an interpretation.
  • Example: khalifah (2:30) can be rendered "vicegerent", "successor" or "steward": each suggests a different role for humans.
  • So many Muslims call an English version a translation of the meaning. Pickthall titled his The Meaning of the Glorious Koran (1930).
  • Translations are valued for understanding and for sharing the message, but they are not used in salah and are not "the Qur'an" itself.
  • Trustworthy interpretation: tafsir (commentary) by qualified scholars, using the Arabic, the hadith and the context of each revelation.

Mission 5

Treating the mushaf with respect

  • "Which none shall touch but those who are clean" (Qur'an 56:79, Yusuf Ali). Most scholars take this to mean wudu (ritual washing) before touching an Arabic copy.
  • Some read 56:79 as describing angels and the heavenly book; they still urge respect.
  • A copy is kept high, often wrapped, nothing placed on top; read on a stand (rehal); never put on the floor or taken into a toilet.
  • Worn-out copies are buried or burned respectfully, never thrown away.
  • Many treat translations with respect but with fewer rules, as they are not the Arabic text.
Mission 5

Recited, memorised, remembered

  • Tilawah (recitation) with tajwid: precise rules of pronunciation and rhythm. A skilled reciter is a qari.
  • Hifz: memorising the whole Qur'an; a hafiz is honoured in family and mosque.
  • Al-Fatiha is recited in every rak'ah (unit) of salah.
  • Ramadan: "the (month) in which was sent down the Qur'an" (Qur'an 2:185, Yusuf Ali). Many read the whole Qur'an across the month; in many mosques it is recited in full in the night prayers (tarawih).
  • Laylat al-Qadr: in the last ten nights, Muslims pray through the night; some stay in the mosque (i'tikaf).
  • Recited at births, weddings, sickness and funerals; written as calligraphy in mosques and homes.
Mission 5Read the source

Surah al-Fatiha

> "In the name of Allah, Most Gracious, Most Merciful. Praise be to Allah, the Cherisher and Sustainer of the worlds; Most Gracious, Most Merciful; Master of the Day of Judgment. Thee do we worship, and Thine aid we seek. Show us the straight way," (Qur'an 1:1–6, Yusuf Ali)

  • Verse 7 asks for the way of those whom God has blessed, not of those who earn wrath or go astray.
  • Called Umm al-Kitab ("mother of the Book"): the whole Qur'an in miniature.
  • Declaration of Allah: merciful, Lord of all worlds, Judge.
  • Submission: the believer worships God alone and depends on Him alone.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Salah: five fixed prayers, with wudu, niyyah and qiblah; each rak'ah moves from standing to prostration.
  • Sunni and Shi'a Muslims share the pillar but differ in timing, hand position, prostration surface and wudu.
  • Other prayer (du'a, tahajjud, nafila, tasbih, wird) gives room for personal devotion beside salah and Jumu'ah.

Mission 2

  • Hajj: a once-in-a-lifetime duty for those able (3:97), retracing Ibrahim, Hajar and Isma'il and the Prophet's Farewell Hajj.
  • Ihram, tawaf, sa'y, 'Arafat, Muzdalifah, stoning, sacrifice: equality, obedience and a foretaste of Judgement.
  • 20th century: Saudi control, air travel, expansion, quotas and safety, and disputes over heritage.

Mission 3

  • Sawm: dawn to sunset in Ramadan, for taqwa (2:183), with exemptions for illness, travel and frailty.
  • Ramadan's benefits: nearness to God, spiritual discipline, moral change, unity and care for the poor.
  • Eid-ul-Fitr ends the fast with zakat al-fitr and shared joy.
  • Eid-ul-Adha recalls Ibrahim's sacrifice (37:102–107; 22:37).

Mission 4

  • The Qur'an: God's speech (kalam Allah), revealed through Jibril (wahy) to Muhammad in Arabic, in stages, 610–632 (2:97, 17:106).
  • Makkan surahs: short, tawhid and judgement; Madinan: longer, law and community. 114 surahs, al-Fatiha first, then roughly by length.
  • Memorised and written in the Prophet's life; gathered under Abu Bakr (Zayd); standardised under Uthman (c. 650); "We will assuredly guard it" (15:9).
