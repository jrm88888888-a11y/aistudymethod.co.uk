Mission 1

Why science challenges belief

  • Modern science stresses evidence (observation, experiment) and reason (testable hypotheses, revisable theories).
  • Many beliefs were once linked to readings of the Bible and to ancient science: an earth-centred cosmos, a young earth, fixed species.
  • Three kinds of challenge:
  • Discoveries that clash with a literal reading of a text (Galileo, Darwin).
  • Explanations that make God seem unnecessary (the "God of the gaps", the later screen).
  • Replacement: the view that science can answer every real question, so a religious interpretation of the universe is no longer needed.
Mission 1

Galileo

  • Copernicus (On the Revolutions, 1543): the earth moves round the sun (heliocentrism).
  • Galileo Galilei (1564–1642) used the telescope (1609–10): moons of Jupiter, phases of Venus. Evidence for Copernicus, though not yet proof.
  • Church authorities read texts such as Joshua 10:12–13 and Psalm 93:1 as teaching a still earth and a moving sun. In 1616 Copernicanism was declared contrary to scripture.
  • Galileo's Dialogue (1632) → trial in 1633: found "vehemently suspected of heresy"; house arrest until death.
  • Galileo's reply (Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina, 1615), quoting Cardinal Baronius: scripture teaches "how one goes to heaven, not how heaven goes".
  • 1992: John Paul II accepted that theologians had erred.
  • Historians note politics, personalities and incomplete evidence too: not simply science against religion.
Mission 1Read the source

A sun that stood still

> "The sun stood still, and the moon stayed, until the nation had avenged themselves of their enemies." (Joshua 10:13, WEBBE)

> "The world also is established. It can't be moved." (Psalm 93:1, WEBBE)

  • Before Galileo, many read these as facts about astronomy.
  • Galileo's approach (and Augustine's before him): scripture speaks in ordinary language about how things appear; its purpose is salvation, not astronomy.

Q1 Why might Psalm 93:1 have seemed to support an earth-centred cosmos?

Show answer

It says the world "can't be moved".

Q2 How could a Christian read Psalm 93:1 without contradicting heliocentrism?

Show answer

As poetry about God's reign and the stability of creation, not as a claim about the earth's motion.

Mission 1

Darwin

  • Charles Darwin (1809–1882), On the Origin of Species (1859); The Descent of Man (1871).
  • Natural selection: variation + inheritance + struggle for survival → species change slowly over vast ages. Humans share common ancestors with apes.
  • Challenges:
  • To a literal reading of Genesis 1–2 (six days; separate creation of kinds; a young earth).
  • To design: William Paley (Natural Theology, 1802) saw an eye as proof of a designer; natural selection offers another explanation.
  • To human uniqueness (made in God's image, Genesis 1:27) and to God's goodness, given waste and suffering in nature.
  • Darwin's own closing words (1859): "There is grandeur in this view of life, with its several powers, having been originally breathed into a few forms or into one". In the 1860 edition he added "by the Creator".
Mission 1

Nineteenth-century Christian responses

  • Rejection: Bishop Samuel Wilberforce reviewed the Origin harshly (1860) and debated T. H. Huxley at Oxford (reports of the exchange differ). American theologian Charles Hodge: "What is Darwinism? It is Atheism" (1874), because it excluded design.
  • Acceptance: Charles Kingsley wrote to Darwin (1859) that it is "just as noble a conception of Deity" to believe God made creatures able to develop themselves; Darwin quoted him in later editions.
  • Frederick Temple (Bampton Lectures, 1884; later Archbishop of Canterbury): God works through evolution.
  • Aubrey Moore (Lux Mundi, 1889): Darwinism, "under the disguise of a foe, did the work of a friend", by ending the picture of an absent God who only intervenes now and then.
  • Catholic Church: no formal condemnation; Pius XII (Humani Generis, 1950) allowed study of the body's evolution, holding that each soul is created directly by God.

Mission 1

The Big Bang

  • Georges Lemaître (1894–1966), Belgian physicist and Catholic priest, proposed an expanding universe (1927) from a "primeval atom" (1931). Edwin Hubble's observations (1929) supported expansion.
  • The universe had a beginning about 13.8 billion years ago (current estimate).
  • Contemporary Christian responses:
  • Welcome: Pius XII (1951) saw it as consistent with creation; many see a fit with creatio ex nihilo (creation out of nothing) and with cosmological arguments.
  • Caution: Lemaître himself warned against using physics to prove creation; theology should not depend on the latest theory.
  • Challenge: Stephen Hawking (A Brief History of Time, 1988): if the universe has no boundary, "What place, then, for a creator?"
  • Reply: creation is about why anything exists at all, not only about a first moment (Aquinas held this).
Mission 1Diversity

Creationist and other views

  • Young Earth creationism: Genesis 1 is literal history; six 24-hour days; earth about 6,000–10,000 years old; Noah's flood explains fossils (Whitcomb and Morris, The Genesis Flood, 1961; Answers in Genesis).
  • Old Earth creationism: earth is ancient; "days" are long ages (day-age view), but God created kinds directly.
  • Intelligent Design: some biological systems are too complex to have evolved step by step (Michael Behe, Darwin's Black Box, 1996). Supporters say it is not creationism; critics (and a US court, 2005) said it is not science.
  • Theistic evolution / evolutionary creation: God creates through evolution (Francis Collins, The Language of God, 2006). The position of most Catholic, Anglican and mainstream Protestant teaching.
  • Most mainstream churches read Genesis 1 as theological truth, not a scientific account.
Mission 1

The God of the gaps

  • God of the gaps: using God to explain what science cannot yet explain.
  • The problem: as science fills the gaps, God is pushed out. Newton thought God corrected planets' orbits; later physics showed this was unnecessary.
  • Christian critics of the idea: Henry Drummond (1894) warned against "gaps" theology; Dietrich Bonhoeffer (prison letters, 1944) said God should be found in what we know, not in what we don't know; C. A. Coulson (Science and Christian Belief, 1955) made the phrase well known.
  • Alternative: God as the ground of the whole natural order, including the parts science explains.
  • This is the root of the "replacement" claim: if God was only an explanation, science may replace him.
Mission 1Evaluate

Has science replaced a religious interpretation of the universe?

  • Claim: Science has replaced a religious interpretation of the universe.
  • For: Each discovery (Galileo, Darwin, the Big Bang) removed a job once given to God; the gaps keep shrinking.
  • For: Science's method (testable evidence) seems more reliable than revelation or tradition.
  • Against: Many leading scientists (Galileo, Newton, Lemaître) were believers; many argue that science asks "how", religion "why".
  • Against: Christian thought developed rather than disappeared: non-literal reading, theistic evolution, creatio ex nihilo.
  • Against: Creationism's persistence shows that for some Christians the conflict is real, but that is about readings of scripture, not belief in God as such.
  • Judgement criteria: Was God ever only an explanation? Can science answer "why is there anything?" Are the conflicts with God or with a reading of Genesis?
  • 15-mark (AO1+AO2): weigh strengths and weaknesses of the challenge, using Galileo and Darwin as named evidence.

Mission 2

Secularisation and secularism

  • Secularisation: a process in which religious thinking, practice and institutions lose social significance (Bryan Wilson, Religion in Secular Society, 1966).
  • Secularism: a view about how society should be: religion should have no special place in public life.
Mission 2

Is Britain secularising?

  • Evidence for decline: falling church attendance, baptisms and church weddings since the 1950s. In the 2021 Census (England and Wales), under half the population (46%) described themselves as Christian for the first time; 37% said "no religion".
  • Secularisation thesis (Steve Bruce, God is Dead, 2002): modernisation steadily erodes religion.
  • Challenges to the thesis: Grace Davie: many people are "believing without belonging" (1994); religion is growing worldwide (Peter Berger changed his mind, 1999); migration brings new, vigorous churches.
  • Decline in belonging is clear; decline in belief and spirituality is debated.
Mission 2

Dawkins: the God delusion

  • Richard Dawkins (b. 1941), The God Delusion (2006).
  • Before him, Sigmund Freud (1856–1939, The Future of an Illusion, 1927) called religion an illusion: a belief driven by wishes, such as an infantile longing for a protecting father, though not necessarily false. He also called it a "universal obsessional neurosis".
  • A delusion (stronger than Freud's illusion): a persistent false belief held against strong evidence.
  • God is a scientific hypothesis, and very improbable.
  • Religion causes conflict: faith that cannot be questioned breeds absolutism and violence.
  • Morality does not need God: moral sense has evolutionary roots and changes over time.
Mission 2

Hitchens: "religion poisons everything"

  • Christopher Hitchens (1949–2011), journalist; God Is Not Great: How Religion Poisons Everything (2007).
  • Religion is man-made: it began in humanity's ignorant, fearful childhood.
  • It is harmful: it sanctions violence, divides people and opposes science and medicine.
  • It is totalitarian: a God who watches every thought is a heavenly dictatorship.
  • "What can be asserted without evidence can also be dismissed without evidence."
  • With Dawkins, Sam Harris and Daniel Dennett, he became one of the New Atheists: confrontational, popular, media-savvy (AQA: militant atheism).

Mission 2Read the source

The McGraths reply (paraphrase)

Alister McGrath (b. 1953) and Joanna Collicutt McGrath, The Dawkins Delusion? Atheist Fundamentalism and the Denial of the Divine (2007), chapter 1, "Deluded about God?" (EDX anthology extract 2; paraphrase only):

  • Dawkins writes as a propagandist more than a scientist: rhetoric and selected examples replace careful evidence.
  • Alister McGrath was once an atheist; he came to faith because Christianity made better sense of the evidence.
  • Faith is not "blind trust": Christian faith is a reasoned response to evidence, not a refusal to think.
  • Science cannot settle the God question either way; Dawkins treats his atheism as if it were scientific.
  • Dawkins ignores serious theology and attacks a caricature of religion.

Mission 2

Disillusionment or hard-line atheism?

  • Hard-line atheism: active rejection: religion is irrational and harmful and should be challenged (Dawkins, Hitchens).
  • Disillusionment: drifting away from institutions, not necessarily from belief:
  • Scandals, especially abuse and its cover-up (e.g. inquiry reports in Ireland, 2009, and in England and Wales, 2020–22).
  • Teaching that some find outdated (on gender and sexuality); worship that feels irrelevant; perceived hypocrisy.
  • Many of the "nones" are not hostile to religion: some still pray or believe in God; some describe themselves as spiritual but not religious (see Mission 3).
  • Your spec asks you to compare the two: which does more to explain decline?
Mission 2Evaluate

Would society be happier without Christianity?

  • Claim: Society would be happier without Christianity.
  • For (Freud): religion keeps people infantile and represses natural instincts through guilt.
  • For (Dawkins, Hitchens): religion causes conflict, harms children and blocks rational inquiry.
  • Against: Christianity has inspired hospitals, schools, abolition of the slave trade, civil rights, and much charity today.
  • Against: 20th-century atheist regimes also caused great harm; violence has many causes, not religion alone.
  • Against (McGrath): the atheists' case depends on selected examples and a caricature of faith.
  • Judgement criteria: What evidence counts (history, psychology, surveys)? Is harm caused by religion as such, or by its misuse? Who decides what "happier" means?

Mission 3

How churches respond to secularisation

  • Three broad responses:
  • Resist: keep traditional forms and teaching; be a faithful, distinct minority.
  • Accommodate: adapt teaching and practice to modern culture (some liberal Protestant approaches).
  • Re-imagine: keep the core faith but create new forms of church for people who would not come to a traditional service.
  • Many churches do all three at once.
  • The new forms raise a question: what is essential to "church", and what is only form?
Mission 3

Fresh Expressions

  • Mission-shaped Church (Church of England report, 2004; working group chaired by Bishop Graham Cray): Britain is now a mission field; the parish alone cannot reach everyone.
  • Fresh Expressions (from 2004; Church of England and Methodist Church): new forms of church mainly for people not yet in any church, shaped by their culture.
  • Rowan Williams spoke of a "mixed economy": traditional parishes and fresh expressions side by side.
  • Examples: Messy Church (from 2004: craft, short celebration and a meal for families); café church; skate church; Forest Church (worship outdoors); church in a pub or online.
  • Aim: not a stepping stone to "proper" church, but church in its own right: worship, community, discipleship and mission.
Mission 3

"Spiritual" and "religious"

  • Religious: belonging to a tradition: shared beliefs, rituals, sacred texts, community and external authority (church, scripture).
  • Spiritual: a personal search for meaning, inner peace or connection, guided by one's own experience.
  • Paul Heelas and Linda Woodhead (The Spiritual Revolution, 2005; the Kendal Project): "life-as" religion (living by external roles and rules) is declining; "subjective-life" spirituality (yoga, meditation, holistic practices) is growing, though from a small base.
  • Many people now say they are "spiritual but not religious".
  • Debate: is this a new form of religion, or a stage of secularisation, since it rarely passes on to the next generation (Steve Bruce)?
  • Your spec asks for definitions of both terms: give each, then a contrast.
Mission 3

New Religious Movements

  • New Religious Movement (NRM): a religious group of recent origin, outside the main traditions (a neutral term, preferred to "cult"; Eileen Barker, 1989).
  • Roy Wallis (The Elementary Forms of the New Religious Life, 1984) sorted NRMs by their attitude to the world:
  • World-rejecting: withdraw from a corrupt society; strong demands (e.g. ISKCON in its early years; the Unification Church).
  • World-affirming: techniques to unlock human potential and success, little worship (e.g. Transcendental Meditation).
  • World-accommodating: mainly a renewal of inner religious life, accepting society (e.g. neo-Pentecostal renewal groups).
  • Meaning for secularisation: NRMs may show continuing spiritual hunger; or their small size shows religion becoming a personal consumer choice.

Mission 3Diversity

Signs of life, or signs of decline?

  • Supporters: new forms reach people with no church background; they are flexible, local, often lay-led; they show Christianity adapting as it always has.
  • Critics within the Church: Andrew Davison and Alison Milbank (For the Parish, 2010) argue Fresh Expressions can weaken the parish, follow consumer tastes, and separate people into groups by preference instead of one community around the sacraments.
  • Questions: Are fresh expressions "church" without regular Communion? Do they make lasting disciples? Can they survive without a founding leader?
  • Sociologists: some (Davie) see vitality and change; others (Bruce) see small growth that cannot offset decline.
  • NRMs and "spiritual but not religious" raise the same question: transformation or decline?
Mission 3Evaluate

Is secularisation an opportunity for Christianity?

  • Claim: Secularisation is an opportunity for Christianity to develop new ways of thinking and acting.
  • For: Freed from privilege, the Church can focus on mission and service (Fresh Expressions; reverse mission).
  • For: A minority church can be more distinct and committed, like the early Church (Acts 2:46–47).
  • Against: Declining numbers mean fewer clergy, closing buildings and lost influence; new forms are small.
  • Against: Adapting to culture risks losing what is distinctive (Davison and Milbank).
  • Judgement criteria: What counts as success: numbers, depth of faith, or service? Is spirituality outside churches a gain or a loss for Christianity?

Mission 4

Why Latin America, why the 1960s?

  • A largely Catholic continent with deep poverty and inequality: land and wealth held by a few.
  • Many countries under military dictatorships (e.g. Brazil from 1964, Chile from 1973).
  • The Second Vatican Council (1962–65) asked the Church to read "the signs of the times" and engage with the modern world.
  • Latin American bishops met at Medellín, Colombia (1968): they spoke of poverty as "institutionalised violence" and called for liberation.
  • In villages and slums, base ecclesial communities (small lay groups) read the Bible together and asked what it said about their lives.
Mission 4

Gustavo Gutiérrez

  • Gustavo Gutiérrez (1928–2024), Peruvian priest who worked in a Lima slum; later a Dominican friar.
  • A Theology of Liberation (Spanish 1971; English 1973).
  • Theology is the second step. The first step is commitment and action with the poor; theology then reflects critically on that action in the light of God's Word (paraphrase).
  • Liberation at three levels (one process): (1) political and economic liberation; (2) human liberation, as people become agents of their own history; (3) liberation from sin, into communion with God and others.
  • Sin is the root of all injustice; but salvation is not only "spiritual": it touches real lives now.
Mission 4Read the source

Good news to the poor

> "The Spirit of the Lord is on me, because he has anointed me to preach good news to the poor. He has sent me … to proclaim release to the captives, … to deliver those who are crushed," (Luke 4:18, WEBBE)

> "I have surely seen the affliction of my people who are in Egypt, and have heard their cry because of their taskmasters" (Exodus 3:7, WEBBE)

  • Jesus begins his ministry in Nazareth with Isaiah's words (Luke 4:16–21).
  • In the Exodus, God hears the cry of slaves and acts to free them.

Q1 Why is the Exodus central to liberation theology?

Show answer

God sides with the oppressed and frees them in history, not only after death.

Q2 How might a traditional reader interpret "release to the captives" differently?

Show answer

As release from sin, spiritually, rather than from political oppression; Gutiérrez holds both together.

Mission 4

The preferential option for the poor

  • The Gospel demands that Christians give priority to the poor and act in solidarity with them.
  • The phrase was adopted by the Latin American bishops at Puebla, Mexico (1979).
  • Preferential, not exclusive: God loves everyone; but where people are crushed, God's love takes their side first.
  • Basis: Jesus' own poverty and his ministry; "you did it to one of the least of these my brothers, you did it to me" (Matthew 25:40).
  • Gutiérrez distinguishes material poverty (an evil, to be fought) from spiritual poverty (openness to God) and voluntary poverty (solidarity with the poor in protest against poverty).

Mission 4

Orthopraxis before orthodoxy

  • Orthodoxy: right belief (official Church teaching). Orthopraxis: right action.
  • Liberation theology puts right action first: faith is tested by what it does for the poor.
  • "Faith, if it has no works, is dead in itself." (James 2:17)
  • It does not reject doctrine: action and reflection feed each other in a hermeneutical circle (act → reflect → act better).
  • Critics: putting practice first could let politics decide what doctrine means.
Mission 4

The Boffs: see, judge, act

  • Leonardo Boff (b. 1938), Brazilian theologian, then a Franciscan priest, Jesus Christ Liberator (1972); and his brother Clodovis Boff (b. 1944).
  • Introducing Liberation Theology (1986; English 1987) sets out three mediations:
  • Socio-analytical (see): why are people poor? Use the social sciences, including some Marxist tools, to find causes.
  • Hermeneutical (judge): what does God's Word say about this? Read scripture from the side of the poor.
  • Practical (act): what must be done? Plan action with the community.
  • Leonardo Boff: Jesus is the liberator who announces the Kingdom as the transformation of the whole of life.

Mission 4

Black theology: James Cone

  • James H. Cone (1938–2018), African American theologian; Black Theology and Black Power (1969), A Black Theology of Liberation (1970), God of the Oppressed (1975).
  • Context: the US civil rights movement, Black Power, Martin Luther King Jr and Malcolm X.
  • God is on the side of the oppressed: the God of the Exodus and of Jesus' ministry (Luke 4:18).
  • "God is black": not a claim about skin colour, but that God identifies with those oppressed for their blackness.
  • Theology written by white churches had ignored or justified racism; theology must start from Black experience.
  • Later: The Cross and the Lynching Tree (2011) compares the cross with the lynching of Black Americans.
Mission 4

Impact on believers and communities

  • Base ecclesial communities: by the 1980s, many thousands across Brazil and beyond; lay people read the Bible and organised for land, water and rights.
  • Óscar Romero (1917–1980), Archbishop of San Salvador, spoke for the poor and against violence; shot while saying Mass (24 March 1980); canonised in 2018.
  • Many priests, nuns and lay catechists were killed in the region's conflicts, among them six Jesuits at the UCA in San Salvador (1989).
  • Wider influence: Black theology (USA), Dalit theology (India), Minjung theology (Korea), feminist theology; aid agencies such as CAFOD speak of the option for the poor.
  • Today: the option for the poor is part of official Catholic social teaching, while base communities have declined and Pentecostal churches have grown among the poor.
Mission 4Evaluate

Is it right to put one group first?

  • Claim: Christians are right to give priority to the poor over other groups.
  • For: The Bible shows God hearing the cry of the oppressed (Exodus 3:7) and Jesus bringing good news to the poor (Luke 4:18).
  • For: Neutrality in an unjust society supports the powerful; love must take sides where people are crushed.
  • Against: God's love is universal; Christians must love rich and poor, oppressor and oppressed (Matthew 5:44).
  • Against: Taking sides risks dividing the Church along class lines.
  • Judgement criteria: Is "preferential" the same as "exclusive"? Does the option serve everyone's good? What does Jesus' ministry show?

Mission 5

Karl Marx in brief

  • Karl Marx (1818–1883), German philosopher and economist; The Communist Manifesto (1848, with Friedrich Engels); Capital, vol. 1 (1867).
  • Economics drives history: who owns the means of production (land, factories) shapes law, politics and ideas.
  • Class struggle: owners (bourgeoisie) and workers (proletariat) have opposed interests.
  • On religion: "the opium of the people" (1844): it eases the pain of oppression but keeps people from changing it.
  • Philosophers have only interpreted the world; "the point is to change it" (Theses on Feuerbach, 1845).
  • An atheist: he expected religion to disappear once oppression ended.
Mission 5

Exploitation

  • Exploitation occurs when humans are treated as objects and used as a means to an end.
  • The worker sells their labour for a wage; the goods they make are worth more than the wage.
  • The difference, surplus value, goes to the owner as profit.
  • So inequality is built into the system, not just caused by greedy individuals.
Mission 5

From personal sin to structural sin

  • Traditional teaching stresses personal sin: wrong acts by individuals.
  • Liberation theology adds social or structural sin: injustice built into systems, so that ordinary participation harms the poor.
  • It uses Marxist analysis to find the deeper causes of poverty, violence and injustice:
  • Capitalism: an economy organised for profit; Latin America kept dependent on rich nations (dependency theory).
  • Institutions: schools, churches, the state can teach the poor to accept their place. Paulo Freire, Pedagogy of the Oppressed (1968): education for conscientisation (critical awareness).
  • Medellín (1968) called unjust structures "institutionalised violence"; John Paul II later used the phrase "structures of sin" (Sollicitudo Rei Socialis, 1987, §36).
Mission 5

Marx as a tool, not a guide

  • Liberation theologians used Marx selectively: his analysis of society, not his atheism or materialism.
  • The Boffs (Introducing Liberation Theology, 1987): Marx can be a companion on the way, but never the guide (paraphrase).
  • Gutiérrez: class struggle is a fact to be recognised, not a call to hate; Christians must love even their enemies.
  • The final authority is the Gospel and the poor's experience of God, not Marxist theory.
  • Critics doubted the tools could be separated from the ideology.
Mission 5

The Vatican's warning (1984)

  • Instruction on Certain Aspects of the "Theology of Liberation" (Libertatis Nuntius), Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith, 6 August 1984; signed by Cardinal Joseph Ratzinger (later Pope Benedict XVI), approved by John Paul II.
  • Affirms: the cry of the poor is real; the Church must make a preferential option for the poor; liberation is a Christian theme.
  • Warns against (paraphrase): borrowing Marxist analysis uncritically, since it cannot be separated from atheist ideology; class struggle as the law of history; reducing salvation to political liberation; a "people's church" set against the bishops.
  • Leonardo Boff was ordered to a period of silence (1985) over his book Church: Charism and Power (1981).
  • John Paul II (Puebla, 1979): Jesus as a political revolutionary does not fit the Church's teaching (paraphrase).
Mission 5

Later responses (1986 to today)

  • Instruction on Christian Freedom and Liberation (Libertatis Conscientia, 1986): a positive account of Christian liberation; "love of preference for the poor".
  • John Paul II adopted structural sin language (1987) and the option for the poor in his social teaching.
  • Pope Francis (2013–2025), from Argentina: wanted "a Church which is poor and for the poor" (2013); met Gutiérrez (2013).
  • Óscar Romero, once viewed with suspicion by some in Rome, was beatified (2015) and canonised (2018).

  • Claim: Liberation theology was right to use Marxist analysis.
  • For: It explains why poverty persists despite charity; the prophets also condemned unjust systems.
  • For: Truth can be learned from anyone; Aquinas used the pagan Aristotle.
  • Against (Vatican): The analysis carries atheism and class hatred with it; salvation risks becoming politics.
  • Against (Marxists): It did not go far enough: it kept religion, which Marx saw as part of the problem.
  • Against (Clodovis Boff, 2007): Later he argued that some liberation theology had put the poor, not Christ, at the centre.
  • Judgement criteria: Can a tool be separated from its ideology? Are the religious foundations primary? What has helped the poor most?

Mission 6

What is feminist theology?

  • Patriarchy: a social system in which men hold power and male experience is treated as the norm.
  • Feminist theology asks how patriarchy has shaped Christian scripture, doctrine, worship and leadership, and what should follow.
  • Context: first-wave feminism (votes for women; Elizabeth Cady Stanton, The Woman's Bible, 1895–98); second-wave feminism of the 1960s–70s.
  • Key themes: women's experience as a source for theology; a hermeneutic of suspicion (reading texts alert to male bias: Elisabeth Schüssler Fiorenza, In Memory of Her, 1983); language for God (McFague).
  • Two paths: reform Christianity from within (Ruether, McFague) or leave it as beyond repair.
Mission 6

Rosemary Radford Ruether

  • Rosemary Radford Ruether (1936–2022), American Catholic theologian; Sexism and God-Talk (1983).
  • Her critical principle: whatever denies or diminishes "the full humanity of women" is not redemptive; whatever promotes it reflects the divine (paraphrase, ch. 1).
  • She is a reformist: she stays within Christianity and recovers its prophetic-liberating tradition, in which God sides with the oppressed.
  • Chapter 5 asks the question of this mission: "Can a Male Saviour Save Women?"
Mission 6

The problem of a male Christ

  • Over centuries, Christ's maleness was used to rank men above women.
  • Aquinas, following Aristotle, wrote that as an individual, "woman is defective and misbegotten" (Summa Theologica I q.92 a.1 ad 1), while insisting that woman is part of God's plan for human nature.
  • Christ's maleness has been used to argue that only men can represent Christ as priests.
  • An early Christian principle: what Christ did not take on, he did not heal (Gregory of Nazianzus, 4th century). If Christ's maleness is what saves, women seem left out.
  • Ruether: Christ's maleness has no ultimate significance; it matters only as a social symbol in patriarchal societies.
Mission 6Diversity

Responses to Ruether

  • Catholic and Orthodox teaching: Christ saves all humans because he took on human nature; maleness is not what saves. But his maleness still matters as a sign, e.g. for priesthood.
  • Many evangelicals: Christ is defined by scripture and the creeds; the historical Jesus cannot be replaced by a community's Christ.
  • Other feminists: Elizabeth Johnson, She Who Is (1992), develops a fuller Sophia (Wisdom) Christology.
  • Daphne Hampson: Ruether picks only the parts she likes; a Christianity tied to a male past cannot be made feminist.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Galileo and Darwin challenged literal readings of scripture; many Christians came to read the Bible as teaching "how one goes to heaven, not how heaven goes".
  • Responses to Darwin ranged from rejection (Wilberforce, Hodge) to welcome (Kingsley, Temple, Moore); today creationism, Intelligent Design and theistic evolution coexist.
  • The Big Bang is welcomed by some as fitting creation; the "God of the gaps" is widely rejected by Christian thinkers.

Mission 2

  • Secularisation is a process (loss of social significance); secularism is a view about public life.
  • Popular atheists (Dawkins, Hitchens) call religion irrational and harmful; many who leave churches are disillusioned rather than atheist.
  • The McGraths reply that faith is reasoned and science is neutral about God.

Mission 3

  • Responses to secularisation include resisting, accommodating and re-imagining church.
  • "Spiritual" is personal and self-guided; "religious" is shared and tradition-led; Wallis sorts NRMs as world-rejecting, world-affirming or world-accommodating.

Mission 4

  • Liberation theology arose in Latin America in the 1960s–70s (Medellín 1968): Gutiérrez (A Theology of Liberation, 1971) and the Boffs (see, judge, act).
  • The preferential option for the poor: priority and solidarity, not exclusion; orthopraxis: right action comes first, tested against faith.
  • Cone's black theology: God sides with the oppressed; "God is black".

Mission 5

  • Marx: exploitation (people used as means to an end), religion as opium.
  • Liberation theology used Marxist tools to analyse structural sin in capitalism and institutions, but not Marx's atheism.
  • The Vatican warned in 1984 and was more positive in 1986; the option for the poor and "structures of sin" entered Catholic teaching.

Mission 6

  • Feminist theology examines patriarchy in Christianity; reformists stay to change it, post-Christians leave.
