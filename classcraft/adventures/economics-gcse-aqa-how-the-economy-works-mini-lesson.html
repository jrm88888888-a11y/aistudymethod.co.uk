Mission 1

Mission 1: If a bank pays you more for saving, why might shops across the country start to struggle?

Make a guess: If a bank pays you more for saving, why might shops across the country start to struggle?

Mission 1

What is an interest rate?

  • Interest: the price of borrowing money, or the reward for saving it.
  • Interest rate: interest as a percentage of the amount, usually per year.
  • Savers lend their money to a bank, so the bank pays them interest.
  • Borrowers pay interest to the bank on loans, overdrafts, credit cards and mortgages.
  • One rate change pulls two ways: a higher rate is good news for savers and bad news for borrowers.
Mission 1

Why interest rates differ

Banks charge and pay many different rates. What sets them?

  • Bank Rate: the Bank of England's rate is the starting point; most other rates move with it.
  • Risk: lenders charge more to borrowers who may not repay.
  • Security: a mortgage is secured on a house, so its rate is usually lower than an unsecured loan or a credit card.
  • Time and access: savers get more for locking money away for a fixed term than for instant access.
  • Amount and competition: rates vary with the size of the loan and how hard banks compete for customers.
  • Say: "the cost of borrowing rises" — not: "money gets more expensive to buy".
Mission 1

Worked example: calculating interest

Illustrative data.

  • Mia saves 2,000 at 4% a year.
  • Interest after 1 year = 2,000 × 4 ÷ 100 = 80. Balance: 2,080.
  • Year 2: interest is paid on 2,080 (interest on interest: compound interest).
    • 2,080 × 4 ÷ 100 = 83.20. Balance: 2,163.20.
  • If the rate rises from 4% to 5%: first-year interest = 2,000 × 5 ÷ 100 = 100, not 80.
  • Borrowing works the same way. A loan of 10,000 at 6% costs 600 in interest a year; at 7%, 700.
  • So a 1 percentage point rise adds 20 a year to Mia's savings, and 100 a year to the loan's cost.
Quick check
?Jo puts 1,500 into a savings account paying 2% a year compound interest and takes nothing out. How much is in the account after 2 years?

Mission 1

Interest rates and households

When interest rates rise:

  • Saving is more rewarding, so people save more.
  • Borrowing costs more, so people borrow less, especially for big items bought on credit: cars, furniture, holidays.
  • Households with variable-rate mortgages pay more each month, leaving less to spend.
  • On a fixed-rate loan, the payments change only when the fixed deal ends.
  • Result: consumer spending usually falls.
  • Illustrative data: on a 150,000 mortgage, interest at 2% is 3,000 a year; at 5% it is 7,500 a year.
  • When interest rates fall, the opposite: less saving, more borrowing, more spending.
  • Winners and losers: savers living on interest (often retired people) gain from a rise; borrowers lose.
Mission 1

Interest rates and firms

  • Investment: firms often borrow to buy machines or build factories.
  • Illustrative data: a new machine is expected to earn a return of 6% a year on its cost.
    • Borrowing at 4%: it pays for itself, so the firm invests.
    • Borrowing at 8%: the interest is more than the return, so the firm does not.
  • Higher rates also mean:
    • firms with existing loans pay more interest, so profits fall
    • firms with spare cash may save it rather than invest it
    • customers spend less, so sales fall
  • Lower rates encourage investment and borrowing.
Quick check
?A central bank raises interest rates. Which outcome is most likely?
Mission 1

Monetary policy and its limits

  • Monetary policy: the central bank changes interest rates to influence spending, borrowing, saving and investment, and so growth, jobs and prices.
  • The Bank of England's Bank Rate is the base for the rates banks set.
  • Real world: the Bank of England cut Bank Rate to 0.1% in March 2020 to support spending in the pandemic. It then raised it in steps to 5.25% by August 2023 to bring down high inflation.
Mission check

?Interest rates fall from 5% to 2%. Which household is most likely to increase its spending in the next few months?

The same rate change helps some households and hurts others; who borrows and who saves decides which way spending moves.

Mission 2: Can an economy keep growing just by people spending more?

Make a guess: Can an economy keep growing just by people spending more?

Mission 2

Measuring output: GDP

  • GDP (gross domestic product): the total value of all goods and services produced in a country in a year.
  • Economic growth: an increase in real GDP over time.
  • Nominal GDP uses the prices of that year, so it rises when prices rise.
  • Real GDP removes the effect of rising prices. It shows whether more is really being produced.
  • GDP per capita (per person) = GDP ÷ population. It is a better guide to average living standards.
  • Say: "real GDP rose" — not: "GDP rose", when you mean the economy produced more.
Mission 2

Worked example: growth rates

  • Illustrative data.
Year 1Year 2
Nominal GDP800 bn856 bn
Real GDP (year 1 prices)800 bn824 bn
Population40 m40.4 m
  • Nominal growth = (856 − 800) ÷ 800 × 100 = 7%. Part of that is just higher prices.
  • Real growth = (824 − 800) ÷ 800 × 100 = 3%.
  • Real GDP per capita: 800 bn ÷ 40 m = 20,000 → 824 bn ÷ 40.4 m = 20,396.
  • Growth per capita = (20,396 − 20,000) ÷ 20,000 × 100 = about 2%.
  • The population grew, so output per person grew less than total output.
Mission 2

Small rates add up

  • Growth builds on growth: each year's rise is on top of last year's.
  • Output per person doubles in about 35 years at 2% a year.
  • At 9% a year, output per person would double in about 8 years.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. Country X: real GDP 1,200 bn, population 300 million. Country Y: real GDP 400 bn, population 50 million. Which statement is correct?
Mission 2

Two kinds of growth on a PPC

  • A PPC shows the most an economy can produce with all its resources fully used.
  • Point A inside the curve: some workers and machines are unemployed (spare capacity).
  • A → B, onto the curve: actual growth. The economy produces more by using spare capacity.
  • PPC1 → PPC2, shifting outwards: potential growth. The economy can produce more.
  • Once the economy is on its PPC, output can only keep rising if the PPC shifts out.
Mission 2Graph lab

Illustrative data. Start: demand for goods = 460, productive capacity = 500, so there is spare capacity. Output = 460; price level = 100.

  • Takeaway: extra spending raises output only while there is spare capacity. After that, it mostly pushes up prices. Lasting growth needs capacity to grow too.
Quick check

Mission 2

Causes of growth

  • Investment: more and better capital (machines, factories, roads).
  • Technology: new methods and inventions let each worker produce more.
  • Workforce: more workers, e.g. migration or a higher retirement age.
  • Education and training: more skilled workers produce more (human capital).
  • Natural resources: new discoveries, such as oil or minerals.
  • Government policy: stable rules, good infrastructure, support for research.
Mission 2

Policies to promote growth

  • Lower interest rates (central bank): cheaper borrowing raises spending and investment.
  • Fiscal policy: more government spending (e.g. on roads, broadband) or lower taxes.
  • Supply-side policies: education and training, help for research, lower taxes on investment.
  • Spending policies work in the short run if there is spare capacity. Supply-side policies raise capacity in the long run.
Mission 2

Benefits and costs of growth

BenefitsCosts
Higher incomes and living standardsPollution and emissions
More jobsUsing up resources such as oil and forests
More tax revenue for schools and hospitalsGains can go mostly to some people
Less povertyPrices may rise if spending grows faster than capacity
  • Real case: since 1978, China's GDP growth averaged over 9% a year. This helped lift almost 800 million people out of extreme poverty (World Bank).
  • Sustainable growth: growth that meets today's needs without harming future generations' ability to meet theirs.
Quick check
?A country's real GDP grows fast for ten years. Over the same years, its rivers become badly polluted and its forests are cut down. Which statement is best?
Mission 2Evaluate

Is growth always good?

It depends on:

  • Who gains: if most of the extra income goes to a few, most people gain little.
  • The environment: cleaner technology can let output grow with less damage.
  • How rich the country is: extra growth matters more for health and poverty in a low-income country.
Mission check

?A small country has no spare capacity: its workers and factories are all fully used. Its government wants output to keep rising for the next ten years. Which plan is most likely to work?

Spending can only use the capacity an economy has. Lasting growth needs that capacity to grow.

Mission 3

What governments aim for

ObjectiveWhat "success" looks like
Economic growthReal GDP rising at a steady, sustainable rate
Full employment (low unemployment)Almost everyone who wants a job has one
Price stabilityLow and stable inflation
Balance of paymentsA current account that is not in large, lasting deficit
  • UK example: the government sets the Bank of England an inflation target of 2% (CPI).
  • Also:
    • Fairer distribution of income (redistribution)
    • Protecting the environment (sustainability)
  • Say: "low and stable inflation" — not: "falling prices". Falling prices (deflation) are not the goal.
Quick check
?Illustrative data for one country this year: real GDP growth 2%, unemployment 9%, inflation 2%, current account deficit 1% of GDP. Which objective is the government furthest from meeting?
Mission 3

When goals help each other, and when they clash

  • Some goals support each other:
    • growth → firms hire more → unemployment falls
    • more people in work → more tax revenue → more to spend on schools or benefits
  • Others conflict. A trade-off means getting more of one goal costs some of another.
  • Main conflicts:
    • unemployment vs inflation
    • growth vs inflation
    • growth or inflation vs the current account
    • growth vs the environment
    • growth vs equality
  • Most conflicts come from total spending in the economy. Push it up and some goals improve while others get worse.
Mission 3

Conflict: unemployment vs inflation

  • To cut unemployment, a government can boost spending (lower taxes, more government spending, lower interest rates).
  • Firms sell more, so they hire more workers.
  • But as the economy gets close to full capacity:
    • firms compete for scarce workers, so wages rise
    • firms pass higher costs and strong demand into higher prices
  • So lower unemployment can come with higher inflation (demand-pull).
  • It works the other way too: raising interest rates to cut inflation reduces spending, so some workers lose their jobs.
Mission 3

Conflict: growth vs inflation

  • If spending grows faster than the economy's ability to produce, output cannot keep up.
  • Too much money chasing too few goods: demand-pull inflation.
  • A fast-growing economy also bids up the price of raw materials and energy, pushing costs up.
  • Growth from more capacity (better skills, new machines, new technology) is different: output can rise without extra inflation.
  • Say: "growth in productive capacity" — not just "growth", when you mean the economy can make more, not just that people are spending more.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. Over two years, unemployment falls from 6% to 4% while inflation rises from 2% to 5%. Which is the most likely cause?
Mission 3

Conflict: inflation, growth and the current account

  • Current account: exports minus imports of goods and services (plus income flows).
  • Inflation higher than in other countries:
    • home-made goods become less competitive abroad, so exports fall
    • foreign goods look cheaper at home, so imports rise
    • the current account worsens
  • Fast growth: people's incomes rise, and part of the extra income is spent on imports.
  • Say: "current account deficit" — not: "budget deficit". One is about trade with other countries; the other is about government spending and taxes.
Mission 3

Conflict: growth vs the environment

  • More output usually means more energy, raw materials, pollution and waste.
  • Some resources (oil, forests, fish stocks) can be used up: growth may not be sustainable.
  • Sustainable growth: growth that meets today's needs without harming future generations.
  • The link can weaken. UK greenhouse gas emissions fell by about 50% between 1990 and 2022, while real GDP grew.
  • Caution: part of that fall came from making fewer goods in the UK and importing more.
  • Ethics: how much growth is worth more emissions, and who should bear the cost (people today or in the future)? That is a value judgement. Economists can show the trade-off; people and governments choose.
Mission 3

Conflict: growth vs equality

  • Growth does not reach everyone equally. Owners of businesses, highly skilled workers and booming regions may gain most.
  • So the gap between rich and poor can widen even as average income rises.
  • Redistribution (progressive taxes, benefits) can narrow the gap.
  • One view: very high taxes and benefits may weaken the reward for working and investing, so growth slows.
  • Another view: some research (IMF, 2014) found countries with lower inequality tended to have longer-lasting growth.
  • Which effect is bigger depends on how the policy is designed.
Mission 3

SituationGroups that loseGroups that may gain

  • So every choice of priority has winners and losers.
Quick check
?A country's inflation rate rises well above that of its trading partners, while their exchange rates stay the same. What is the most likely effect on its current account?
Mission 3

Managing the conflicts

  • Set priorities: tackle the most urgent problem first, e.g. cut very high inflation, accepting slower growth for a while.
  • Use different tools for different goals: interest rates for inflation; training and better transport links for growth.
  • Raise capacity (supply-side policies): then output and jobs can grow with less inflation. But this takes years.
  • Green technology, pollution taxes and regulation aim to keep growth while cutting emissions.
  • Choosing which goal comes first is normative: it depends on values, not just evidence.
Quick check
?A government wants faster growth without higher inflation. Which policy is most likely to achieve both over time?
Mission check

?During a boom, unemployment in a country falls to a record low and household incomes rise quickly. At the same time its current account deficit widens sharply. What is the most likely link?

Pushing one goal (jobs and growth) can make another (the current account) worse.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Where does a government's money come from, and what happens when it spends more than it collects?

Make a guess: Where does a government's money come from, and what happens when it spends more than it collects?

Mission 4

Where the money comes from

  • Taxes: by far the biggest source.
    • on income (income tax, social security contributions such as National Insurance)
    • on spending (VAT, duties on fuel, alcohol and tobacco)
    • on company profits (corporation tax), property and wealth
  • Other income: charges and fees, profits of state-owned firms, sales of state assets.
  • Borrowing: when taxes do not cover spending.
  • Example, UK, 2026–27 (OBR forecast):
    • total receipts about £1,316 billion
    • income tax and National Insurance together about £580 billion; VAT about £186 billion
Mission 4

What the money is spent on

  • Social protection: pensions, unemployment and other benefits.
  • Health and education.
  • Defence, police and courts.
  • Infrastructure: roads, rail, housing.
  • Interest on government debt.
  • Purposes: provide goods markets would not supply enough of (public and merit goods) · help people on low incomes · support growth (skills, transport) · steady the economy in a slump.
  • Example, UK, 2026–27 (OBR forecast): welfare about £353 billion; health department about £212 billion; education department about £95 billion; net debt interest about £124 billion.
Mission 4

Direct and indirect taxes

  • Direct tax: on income or wealth, paid straight to the government by the person or firm taxed.
    • income tax, corporation tax, National Insurance
  • Indirect tax: on spending on goods and services. The seller pays it to the government but passes some or all of it on in higher prices.
    • VAT, excise duties on fuel, alcohol and tobacco, tariffs on imports
  • Say: "VAT is an indirect tax" — not: "VAT is a direct tax because shoppers pay it directly at the till".
Quick check
?Which of these is an indirect tax?
Mission 4

Progressive, regressive, proportional

  • Judge a tax by the percentage of income paid, not the amount.
  • Progressive: the percentage rises as income rises.
  • Proportional: the same percentage at every income.
  • Regressive: the percentage falls as income rises.
  • Indirect taxes such as VAT tend to be regressive: poorer households spend a bigger share of their income.
  • UK data: in the financial year ending 2022, indirect taxes took 28.1% of the disposable income of the poorest fifth of people, and 9.8% for the richest fifth (ONS).
  • Say: "a progressive tax takes a higher percentage from higher incomes" — not: "a progressive tax is one where rich people pay more pounds".
Mission 4

Worked example: a progressive income tax

  • Illustrative data. Tax bands:
    • first 10,000: no tax
    • 10,001 to 40,000: 20%
    • above 40,000: 40%
  • Income 20,000: tax = 20% × 10,000 = 2,000. Share of income: 2,000 ÷ 20,000 = 10%.
  • Income 60,000:
    • 20% × 30,000 = 6,000
    • 40% × 20,000 = 8,000
    • total tax 14,000. Share: 14,000 ÷ 60,000 = 23.3%.
  • The share rises with income, so the tax is progressive.
  • Note: the 40% rate applies only to income above 40,000, not to all of it.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A new tax takes 500 from a person earning 10,000 and 1,500 from a person earning 50,000. What kind of tax is it?
Mission 4

Taxes and spending can redistribute income

  • Redistribution: moving income from richer to poorer households.
  • Tools:
    • progressive taxes
    • benefits (cash for people on low incomes, the unemployed, families)
    • free or cheap education and health care: worth most, as a share of income, to poorer households
  • In the UK, income before taxes and benefits is much more unequal than income after them (ONS).
  • Debate: higher taxes and benefits narrow the gap, but some argue they weaken the reward for working more. How big that effect is depends on the design.
Mission 4

The budget: balanced, surplus or deficit

  • Budget balance = government revenue − government spending.
  • Balanced budget: revenue = spending.
  • Budget surplus: revenue > spending. The government can repay some debt.
  • Budget deficit: spending > revenue. The government borrows the difference.
  • UK, 2026–27 (OBR forecast): spending £1,449 billion − receipts £1,316 billion = a deficit of £133 billion, about 4.2% of national income.
  • National debt: the total the government owes, built up from past deficits.
  • Say: "the deficit is one year's borrowing; the debt is the total owed" — not: "deficit" and "debt" as the same thing.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A government collects 380 billion in revenue and spends 410 billion. The country's GDP is 2,000 billion. What is its budget position?
Mission 4

Fiscal policy

  • Fiscal policy: using government spending and taxes to influence the economy.
  • Expansionary (in a slump): spend more or cut taxes.
    • households and firms have more to spend: total demand rises
    • output and jobs rise; the deficit usually grows
  • Contractionary (in a boom): spend less or raise taxes.
    • total demand falls: inflation pressure eases
    • but growth slows and some jobs may go
Mission 4

Consequences of deficits and surpluses

  • A deficit:
    • can support jobs and incomes in a slump
    • adds to debt, and debt has to be serviced: interest payments
    • very large debts can raise borrowing costs if lenders lose confidence
  • A surplus:
    • lets the government repay debt
    • but means higher taxes or lower spending, which takes demand out of the economy
  • Economists disagree about how fast deficits should be cut: too fast may slow growth; too slow lets debt and interest costs keep rising.
Quick check
?An economy is in a deep recession, with unemployment rising fast. Which fiscal policy is most likely to help in the short run?
Mission 4

Weigh it up: when should a government borrow?

It depends on:

  • The state of the economy: borrowing in a slump supports jobs; borrowing in a boom can add to inflation.
  • What the money buys: borrowing for roads, schools or skills may raise future output; borrowing for day-to-day costs does not.
  • How big the debt already is, and the interest rate on it.
  • Who pays: future taxpayers repay today's borrowing.
Mission check

?Illustrative data. A country runs a budget deficit five years in a row. Each year the deficit is smaller than the year before: 50, 40, 30, 20, then 10 billion. What happens to its national debt over the five years?

A deficit is one year's borrowing; the debt keeps growing until the budget reaches surplus.

Mission 5

Income and wealth

  • Income: money received over a period of time (a flow), e.g. per week or per year.
  • Types of income:
    • wages and salaries: from working
    • rent: from owning property or land
    • interest: from savings
    • profit and dividends: from owning a business or shares
    • benefits: payments from the government (a transfer, not earned)
  • Wealth: the value of what a person owns at a point in time (a stock): a house, savings, shares, a pension.
  • Net wealth = value of assets − debts (such as a mortgage or loan).
  • Wealth can create income (rent, interest, dividends), and saved income builds wealth.
Mission 5

Worked example: same income, different wealth

  • Illustrative data. Two families each have an income of 40,000 a year.
Family AFamily B
House300,000400,000
Second flat (rented out)0200,000
Savings5,00050,000
Mortgage (debt)250,0000
  • Family A: 300,000 + 5,000 − 250,000 = 55,000 net wealth.
  • Family B: 400,000 + 200,000 + 50,000 = 650,000 net wealth: almost 12 times as much.
  • B's flat also earns rent, so B can save more and grow its wealth faster.
  • Say: "income is a flow, wealth is a stock" — not: "a high income means you are wealthy".
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A household owns a house worth 200,000 and has 20,000 in savings. It owes 150,000 on its mortgage and 10,000 on a car loan. What is its net wealth?
Mission 5

How unequal is the UK?

  • Wealth in Great Britain (ONS, April 2018 to March 2020):
    • the wealthiest 10% of households held 43% of all wealth
    • the poorest 50% of households held 9%
  • Wealth is shared out more unequally than income (ONS).
  • Taxes and benefits reduce income inequality: incomes after taxes and benefits are more equal than incomes from work and savings alone (ONS).
  • Say: "the distribution of income" — not: "the amount of income". It is about who gets what share.
Mission 5

Why incomes and wealth differ

  • Skills and qualifications: scarce skills that firms value (doctors, engineers) earn more.
  • Type of job and hours: full-time or part-time; some jobs pay more for risk or responsibility.
  • Age: earnings usually rise with experience, then fall in retirement.
  • Where you live: wages and house prices differ between regions.
  • Inheritance: some people inherit houses, land or savings.
  • Owning assets: wealth earns rent, interest and dividends; the gap can widen over time.
  • Discrimination: unfair treatment by gender, ethnicity or disability can lower pay (illegal in many countries, but it still happens).
  • Bargaining power: workers in trade unions or with rare skills can win higher pay.
Mission 5

Consequences of inequality

  • Arguments that some inequality helps: higher pay rewards skill, effort and risk; it gives people an incentive to train, work and start businesses.
  • Arguments that large inequality harms:
    • relative poverty: some people cannot afford what most take for granted
    • gaps in health and education between rich and poor
    • less social mobility: children from poorer families find it harder to get on
    • possible social tension and crime
  • Economists disagree about how much inequality is too much. It depends on its causes and on what people value: fairness, freedom and rewards for effort.
Quick check
?Two friends earn exactly the same wage for 30 years. One inherits a house and rents it out; the other inherits nothing. What is most likely to happen to the gap in their wealth?
Mission 5

Progressive taxes: worked example

  • Progressive tax: people with higher incomes pay a larger share of their income in tax.
  • Proportional: everyone pays the same share. Regressive: people with lower incomes pay a larger share.
  • Illustrative income tax:
    • first 10,000: no tax
    • income from 10,000 to 40,000: taxed at 20%
    • income above 40,000: taxed at 40%
  • Income 20,000: tax = 20% × 10,000 = 2,000. Share of income = 10%.
  • Income 60,000: tax = 20% × 30,000 + 40% × 20,000 = 6,000 + 8,000 = 14,000. Share = 23.3%.
  • The higher earner pays a bigger share: the tax is progressive, so it narrows the gap after tax.
  • Taxes on spending, such as VAT, are often regressive: poorer households spend a larger share of their income.
Quick check
?Use the illustrative tax bands from the worked example (0% on the first 10,000; 20% from 10,000 to 40,000; 40% above 40,000). How much tax is paid on an income of 100,000?
Mission 5

Other ways to narrow the gap

  • Benefits: payments to people on low incomes, unemployed, disabled or retired.
  • Free or cheap education and health care: give poorer children and families the same chances; better skills raise future earnings.
  • Trade-offs:
    • high taxes and benefits may reduce the incentive to work, save or invest (economists disagree on how much)
    • education works slowly: the benefits take years to show
Mission 5

Conflicts with other aims

  • Policies to reduce inequality can conflict with other aims:
    • higher taxes on the rich may reduce incentives, so growth may be slower
    • growth can widen inequality if most gains go to people with skills or assets
    • green taxes (on fuel or energy) protect the environment, but can be regressive: poorer households spend a larger share of income on energy
  • Every policy affects groups differently: workers, savers, pensioners, firms, taxpayers.
Mission check

?A government needs to raise more tax revenue. It also wants to narrow the gap between rich and poor households. Which change does both?

A progressive tax takes a larger share from higher incomes, so it raises revenue and narrows the gap at the same time.

Make a guess: Can a country keep growing richer without harming its future?

Mission 6

What is sustainability?

  • Sustainable development: meeting the needs of people today without reducing the ability of future generations to meet their own needs (UN Brundtland Report, 1987).
  • Three kinds of sustainability:
    • Economic: incomes and jobs that can last, not a short boom.
    • Social: fair treatment of people, with health, education and communities that can last.
    • Environmental: using resources no faster than they can be renewed, and limiting pollution.
  • A choice can be good for one kind and bad for another.
Mission 6

Types of environmental damage

  • Pollution:
    • air: smoke and fumes from factories, power stations and vehicles
    • water: waste and chemicals in rivers and seas
    • land: rubbish, landfill, chemicals in soil
    • noise and visual pollution: traffic and aircraft noise; ugly sites
  • Using up resources: oil, gas and minerals are non-renewable. Fish and forests are renewable only if not taken faster than they regrow.
  • Climate change: greenhouse gases from burning fossil fuels.
  • Much of this is an external cost: a cost to third parties, not paid by the producer or consumer.
Quick check
?A fishing fleet catches 50,000 tonnes of fish a year. The fish stock can regrow by only 30,000 tonnes a year. Why is this unsustainable?
Mission 6

Growth and the environment: a conflict?

  • Benefits of growth: higher incomes and more jobs; more tax revenue for schools and hospitals; money to invest in cleaner technology.
  • Costs of growth: more pollution and waste; non-renewable resources used up; congestion; climate change.
  • The conflict: producing more usually means using more resources and creating more pollution.
  • But it depends on how growth happens: more output from renewable energy, recycling and more efficient technology can cut the damage per unit of output.
  • Unsustainable growth: growth today that leaves future generations less able to produce.
Mission 6

Globalisation and sustainability

  • Possible costs:
    • more transport of goods by sea and air, which adds to emissions
    • firms may move production to countries with weaker environmental rules
    • pressure to extract resources quickly in less developed countries
  • Possible benefits:
    • cleaner technology and ideas spread faster between countries
    • countries can specialise in what they produce most efficiently, which can use fewer resources
    • higher incomes in less developed countries can pay for clean water and less polluting energy
  • Ethics: are workers in supply chains paid fairly and safe? Some buyers choose Fairtrade or check where goods come from.
  • Economists disagree about how big each effect is; it varies by industry and country.
Quick check
?A clothing firm moves its factories to a country with weaker pollution rules and lower wages. Which is a sustainability concern about this move?
Mission 6

The government's toolkit

  • Taxes on polluting goods or activities: raise their price, so less is bought and produced.
  • Subsidies for cleaner choices (renewable energy, public transport): lower their cost, so more is used.
  • Regulation and legislation: laws that set limits or ban harmful activities (e.g. limits on vehicle emissions).
  • Fines: penalties for breaking the rules.
  • Pollution permits: the government caps total pollution and issues permits that firms can buy and sell.
  • Parks and protected areas: land where building, farming or mining is limited.
  • Positive externalities (e.g. public transport, insulation) can be encouraged with subsidies or state provision.
  • Real case: in England, large shops had to charge 5p for each single-use plastic bag from October 2015; the charge rose to 10p in May 2021, and spread to all shops.
Mission 6

Worked example: trading pollution permits

  • Illustrative data. The government sets a cap of 100 tonnes of pollution. Firms A and B each pollute 60 tonnes (120 in total).
  • Each firm gets 50 permits (1 permit = 1 tonne). Together they must cut 20 tonnes.
  • Cost of cutting 1 tonne: Firm A $20; Firm B $80. A permit sells for $50.
  • Firm A cuts 20 tonnes (cost 20 × $20 = $400) and sells its 10 spare permits to B (+$500).
  • Firm B cuts nothing and buys 10 permits ($500), cheaper than cutting 10 tonnes itself ($800).
  • Total cut: still 20 tonnes. Cost of cutting: $400, not $1,000 if each firm had cut 10 tonnes (10 × $20 + 10 × $80).
  • Permits get the cut done by the firms that can cut most cheaply. Lowering the cap each year cuts pollution further.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A permit to pollute 1 tonne sells for $50. Firm C can cut 1 tonne of its pollution for $30. What should Firm C do to keep its costs as low as possible?

Pros and cons of each policy

PolicyProsCons
TaxPolluter pays; raises revenueHard to set the right rate; can hit poorer households hardest
SubsidyEncourages cleaner choicesCosts taxpayers; money has an opportunity cost
Regulation and finesClear; quick to act on dangerous pollutionCosts to check and enforce; no reward for cutting below the limit

Parks and protected areasProtect nature and wildlifeLimit farming, building and jobs in that area
  • No single policy is best for every problem. Governments often combine them.
Mission 6

Ethics and sustainability in government objectives

  • Governments aim for growth, jobs and stable prices. Sustainability asks: at what cost to the future?
  • Choices involve value judgements: how much should people today give up for people in the future?
  • Who bears the cost? A fuel tax may hit people in the countryside who must drive; closing a coal mine costs local jobs.
  • Policies can be designed to share costs more fairly: e.g. retraining workers or helping poorer households.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • An interest rate is the price of borrowing and the reward for saving, as a percentage per year.
  • Higher rates encourage saving and discourage borrowing, spending and investment; lower rates do the opposite.
  • Interest = amount × rate ÷ 100; compound interest pays interest on interest.

Mission 2

  • Economic growth is a rise in real GDP. GDP per capita = GDP ÷ population.
  • Actual growth uses spare capacity; potential growth shifts the PPC out.
  • Growth comes from investment, technology, more and better-skilled workers, resources and good policy.
  • Growth raises living standards but can harm the environment and may not be shared fairly.

Mission 3

  • Governments aim for growth, full employment, low and stable inflation and a sustainable balance of payments; also fairer incomes and sustainability.
  • Boosting spending can cut unemployment and raise growth, but near full capacity it adds to inflation and pulls in imports.
  • Growth can harm the environment; raising capacity can ease some conflicts, but slowly.
  • Every priority has winners and losers, and choosing between goals involves value judgements.

Mission 4

  • Governments raise money mainly from direct and indirect taxes, and spend it on welfare, health, education, defence, infrastructure and debt interest.
  • Progressive taxes take a rising percentage of income; regressive ones a falling percentage.
  • Deficit: spending > revenue, so the government borrows and its debt grows. Surplus: revenue > spending.
  • Fiscal policy uses spending and taxes to steer the economy, with costs: debt interest, time lags and opportunity cost.

Mission 5

  • Income is a flow; wealth is a stock. Net wealth = assets − debts.
  • Incomes differ with skills, jobs, age, region, inheritance and owned assets; wealth gaps can grow over time.
  • Progressive taxes, benefits and free education and health care narrow the gap, with trade-offs.

Mission 6

  • Sustainable development meets today's needs without harming future generations' ability to meet theirs.
  • Growth brings income and jobs, but can bring pollution and use up resources; how it is produced decides how big the conflict is.
  • Governments use taxes, subsidies, regulation, fines, permits and protected areas, each with pros and cons.
