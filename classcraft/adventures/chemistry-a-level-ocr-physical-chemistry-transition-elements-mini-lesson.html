Mission 1

Mission 1: How does a colourless dye suddenly turn pink at the end of a titration?

Make a guess: How does a colourless dye suddenly turn pink at the end of a titration?

Mission 1

Indicators are weak acids

  • An indicator is a weak acid: HInd ⇌ H⁺ + Ind⁻.
  • HInd and Ind⁻ have different colours.
  • Add acid: [H⁺] rises, the equilibrium shifts left: HInd's colour.
  • Add alkali: OH⁻ removes H⁺, it shifts right: Ind⁻'s colour.
  • Phenolphthalein: HInd colourless, Ind⁻ pink.
  • A few drops only, so the indicator barely affects the pH.
Mission 1

Where the colour changes

  • Ka = [H⁺][Ind⁻]/[HInd], so [Ind⁻]/[HInd] = Ka/[H⁺].
  • pH < pKa: HInd dominates. pH > pKa: Ind⁻ dominates.
  • At pH = pKa, [HInd] = [Ind⁻]: the in-between colour.
  • The eye sees the change over about pKa ± 1: the indicator's range.
  • You'll be given each indicator's pH range.
Quick check
?An indicator, HInd, has pKa = 3.7: HInd(aq) ⇌ H⁺(aq) + Ind⁻(aq). HInd is red and Ind⁻ is yellow. What colour does the indicator show in a solution of pH 6.0, and why?
Mission 1

Why not universal indicator?

  • Universal indicator is a mixture of indicators with different pKa values.
  • Their changes follow one another: a different colour at most pH values.
  • Good for estimating pH.
  • Useless for a titration: it changes gradually, with no single sharp change to mark the end.
Mission 1

End point vs equivalence point

  • Equivalence point: acid and base have reacted in the stoichiometric ratio.
  • End point: the indicator changes colour.
  • A good indicator changes colour within the steep section, so the end point matches the equivalence point.
  • In the steep section, one drop (about 0.05 cm³) moves the pH by several units, right across the range.
Mission 1

Which indicator?

  • Weak acid + strong base: equivalence above 7 → phenolphthalein (range about 8–10).
  • Strong acid + weak base: equivalence below 7 → methyl orange (range about 3–4.5).
  • Strong acid + strong base: steep section about 3–11 → either.
  • Weak acid + weak base: no steep section → no indicator is suitable.
Quick check
?25.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ ethanoic acid (pKa = 4.76) is titrated with 0.100 mol dm⁻³ NaOH(aq) at 298 K. Three indicators are available, with these pH ranges: methyl orange 3.1–4.4; bromothymol blue 6.0–7.6; phenolphthalein 8.3–10.0. Which choice of indicator, with its reason, is correct?
Quick check
?25.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ ammonia is titrated with 0.100 mol dm⁻³ hydrochloric acid. Which indicator is the most suitable? Ranges: methyl orange 3.1–4.4; phenolphthalein 8.3–10.0; thymol blue 8.0–9.6; alizarin yellow 10.1–12.0.
Mission check

?Methyl orange can be written HInd(aq) ⇌ H⁺(aq) + Ind⁻(aq), with HInd red and Ind⁻ yellow. A few drops are added to aqueous ammonia: the solution is yellow. Hydrochloric acid is run in. Near the end it turns orange, then red. Why?

Phenolphthalein in reverse. Removing H⁺ pulls HInd ⇌ H⁺ + Ind⁻ right (pink); adding H⁺ pushes it left (red, for methyl orange).

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why does a medicine's level in the blood halve every few hours, however high it starts?

Make a guess: Why does a medicine's level in the blood halve every few hours, however high it starts?

Mission 2

Concentration–time graphs

  • Zero order: a straight line sloping down. The rate stays constant.
  • First order: a curve. [A] falls by the same fraction in equal times.
  • Second order: steeper at first, then a longer, flatter tail.
  • Rate at any moment = gradient of the tangent (made positive).
  • Initial rate: the tangent at t = 0.
  • You identify only orders 0 and 1 from this graph. The second-order curve is background: you find order 2 from a rate–concentration graph.
Mission 2

The halving-time test

  • First order: rate = k[A]. Halve [A] and the rate halves too.
  • So losing half of what's left always takes the same time, whatever [A] you start with.
  • That constant time is the half-life, t½.
  • Zero order: each halving takes less time than the last.
  • Second order: each halving takes longer than the last.
  • The medicine: a fixed halving time means its removal is first order.
Mission 2

Rate–concentration graphs

  • Plot rate (y) against [A] (x). Data: initial rates of separate runs, or tangents to one curve.
  • Zero order: a horizontal line. Rate does not depend on [A].
  • First order: a straight line through the origin. Gradient = k, when A is the only species in the rate equation.
  • Second order: a curve rising more and more steeply from the origin.
  • Don't carry shapes across: a falling straight line on a concentration–time graph is zero order, but a straight line on a rate–concentration graph is first order.
Quick check
?A reaction is zero order with respect to reactant X. Which statement about a graph for this reaction is correct?
Mission 2

Zero order: k from the gradient

  • Zero order: rate = k, so the concentration–time line has gradient −k.
  • Example (illustrative): [A] falls from 0.800 to 0.200 mol dm⁻³ in 300 s.
  • Gradient = (0.200 − 0.800) ÷ 300 = −2.00 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹.
  • k = 2.00 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹. Units of k = units of rate.
Mission 2

Half-life from a graph

  • Half-life, t½: the time for [A] to fall to half its value.
  • Read it from the graph: 0.80 → 0.40, then 0.40 → 0.20. Check the times match.
  • Constant t½ shows first order.
  • k = ln 2 / t½. If t½ = 40 s, k = 0.693 ÷ 40 = 1.73 × 10⁻² s⁻¹.
Quick check
?A concentration–time graph shows [A] falling from 0.120 to 0.060 mol dm⁻³ in the first 35 s, and from 0.060 to 0.030 mol dm⁻³ in the next 35 s. Which statement is correct?
Mission 2

Two ways to measure rate

  • Continuous monitoring: follow one run over time; plot a concentration–time (or volume–time) graph.
  • Monitor gas volume, mass loss, or colour with a colorimeter.
  • Initial-rate method: several runs, changing one starting concentration at a time; find each initial rate.
  • Compare initial rates: double [A] → rate ×1 (order 0), ×2 (order 1), ×4 (order 2).
  • Plot initial rate against [A]: a rate–concentration graph.
Mission 2

Clock reactions

  • A clock reaction times how long a fixed, small amount of product takes to form.
  • Iodine clock: H2O2 oxidises I⁻ to I2. A little S2O3²⁻ turns the I2 back into I⁻ at once.
  • When the S2O3²⁻ runs out, I2 builds up and starch turns blue-black suddenly.
  • Same amount of product each run, so initial rate ∝ 1/t.
  • Valid only while little reactant is used: an approximation to the initial rate.
  • Repeat with a different [I⁻] (or [H2O2]); keep everything else the same.
Quick check
?In an iodine clock, the blue-black colour appears after 48 s. A second run uses double the concentration of I⁻, with everything else the same, and the colour appears after 24 s. What is the order with respect to I⁻?
Mission check

?A hypothetical compound X decomposes at constant temperature.
Results: t = 0, 200, 400, 600 s; [X] = 0.640, 0.320, 0.160, 0.080 mol dm⁻³. A new run at the same temperature starts at [X] = 0.320 mol dm⁻³. How long does [X] take to fall to 0.080 mol dm⁻³?

The level halves in a fixed time because the rate of removal is proportional to the level: first order. Most medicines are cleared this way.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Is water an acid or a base?

Make a guess: Is water an acid or a base?

Mission 3

Acids give protons, bases take them

  • A Brønsted–Lowry acid is a proton (H⁺) donor.
  • A Brønsted–Lowry base is a proton acceptor. It uses a lone pair to take the H⁺.
  • HNO3 + NH3 → NH4⁺ + NO3⁻: HNO3 donates H⁺; NH3 accepts it.
  • An acid–base reaction is a proton transfer.
  • H⁺(aq) and H3O⁺(aq) are two ways of writing the same hydrated proton.
Mission 3

Base or alkali?

  • An alkali is a base that dissolves in water, releasing OH⁻(aq): NaOH, KOH, NH3(aq).
  • Every alkali is a base; not every base is an alkali.
  • Iron(III) oxide neutralises acids, so it is a base. It is insoluble, so it is not an alkali.
  • A base need not contain OH: NH3, O²⁻ and CO3²⁻ all accept H⁺ with a lone pair.
Quick check
?Which statement about bases and alkalis is correct?
Quick check
?Ammonia reacts with water: NH3(aq) + H2O(l) ⇌ NH4⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq). Which species acts as a Brønsted–Lowry acid in the forward reaction?
Mission 3

Conjugate acid–base pairs

  • A conjugate pair differs by exactly one proton.
  • Conjugate base: remove one H⁺; the charge falls by one. HSO4⁻ → SO4²⁻.
  • Conjugate acid: add one H⁺; the charge rises by one. NH3 → NH4⁺.
  • Every proton transfer has two pairs: HNO3/NO3⁻ and NH4⁺/NH3.
  • The acid and base that react are not a pair: each one's partner is across the arrow.
Quick check
?Which pair of species is a conjugate acid–base pair?
Mission 3

Acids and alkalis to know; how many H⁺?

  • In water, acids release H⁺(aq); alkalis release OH⁻(aq).
  • Common acids: HCl, HNO3, H2SO4, CH3COOH. Common alkalis: NaOH, KOH, NH3.
  • Monobasic: one H⁺ per molecule (HCl, HNO3, CH3COOH: only the H of COOH).
  • Dibasic: two (H2SO4). Tribasic: three (H3PO4).
  • In reactions, H⁺ is what reacts: H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H2O(l); Mg(s) + 2H⁺(aq) → Mg²⁺(aq) + H2(g). More in the neutralisation mission.
Mission 3

Amphiprotic species

  • Amphiprotic: can act as a Brønsted–Lowry acid or a base, depending on its partner.
  • It needs an H it can lose and a lone pair to accept H⁺.
  • Examples: H2O, HSO4⁻, H2PO4⁻, HPO4²⁻.
  • In any one reaction it plays one role only.
Mission 3

Water both ways: writing the equations

  • Water as a base: HNO3 + H2O → H3O⁺ + NO3⁻.
  • Water as an acid: NH3 + H2O ⇌ NH4⁺ + OH⁻.
  • HPO4²⁻ as a base: HPO4²⁻ + H3O⁺ ⇌ H2PO4⁻ + H2O.
  • HPO4²⁻ as an acid: HPO4²⁻ + OH⁻ ⇌ PO4³⁻ + H2O.
  • Check: as a base it gains one H; as an acid it loses one. Charges balance.
Quick check
?Which equation shows the hydrogensulfate ion, HSO4⁻, acting as a Brønsted–Lowry acid?
Mission check

?Water takes part in two reactions: (1) CH3NH2 + H2O ⇌ CH3NH3⁺ + OH⁻ and (2) HBr + H2O → H3O⁺ + Br⁻. What role does water play?

Water is both, but one at a time. Faced with an acid it acts as a base; faced with a base it acts as an acid.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Lemon juice is about pH 2; black coffee is about pH 5. How many times more H⁺ is in the lemon juice?

Make a guess: Lemon juice is about pH 2; black coffee is about pH 5. How many times more H⁺ is in the lemon juice?

Mission 4

What pH measures

  • pH = −log10[H⁺], with [H⁺] in mol dm⁻³. pH has no units.
  • Rearranged: [H⁺] = 10⁻ᵖᴴ.
  • Lower pH means higher [H⁺].
  • One pH unit is a tenfold change in [H⁺]; two units, a hundredfold.
  • Very concentrated solutions can have pH below 0 or above 14.
Mission 4

Non-integer pH: worked examples

  • [H⁺] = 3.2 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³: pH = −log10(3.2 × 10⁻⁴) = 3.49.
  • pH 4.60: [H⁺] = 10⁻⁴·⁶⁰ = 2.5 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³.
  • Use the log key (base 10), not ln.
  • Sense check: pH 3.49 lies between 3 and 4, so [H⁺] lies between 10⁻⁴ and 10⁻³.
Calculate
?A sample of rainwater has [H⁺] = 2.0 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³. Calculate its pH to two decimal places.
Quick check
?Hydrobromic acid, HBr, is a strong acid.
What is the pH of 4.0 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ HBr(aq)?
Mission 4

Measuring pH two ways

  • Universal indicator (paper or solution): match the colour to a chart to estimate pH, to about 1 unit.
  • pH meter or data-logger probe: a precise reading, usually to 0.01.
  • Calibrate the meter first with buffer solutions of known pH (for example pH 4, 7 and 10).
  • Rinse the probe with distilled water between solutions; let the reading settle before you record it.
  • This is your pH practical (PAG 11).
Quick check
?A student needs the pH of a solution. Which statement is correct?
Mission 4

Water ionises, a little: Kw

  • Water dissociates slightly: H2O(l) ⇌ H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq).
  • [H2O] is effectively constant, so it is folded into the constant: Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻].
  • Kw = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ mol² dm⁻⁶ at 298 K. pKw = −log10Kw = 14.00.
  • Every aqueous solution contains both H⁺ and OH⁻. Neutral means [H⁺] = [OH⁻].
  • At 298 K, neutral means both are 1.00 × 10⁻⁷ mol dm⁻³: pH 7.00.
Mission 4

Strong base: pH from Kw

  • The product is fixed, so [H⁺] up tenfold means [OH⁻] down tenfold.
  • 0.020 mol dm⁻³ KOH dissociates fully: [OH⁻] = 0.020 mol dm⁻³.
  • [H⁺] = Kw ÷ [OH⁻] = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ ÷ 0.020 = 5.0 × 10⁻¹³ mol dm⁻³; pH = 12.30.
  • Sense check: a base, so pH is above 7 at 298 K.
Quick check
?At 298 K, solution P has [H⁺] = 1.0 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³ and solution Q has [H⁺] = 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³. Kw = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ mol² dm⁻⁶. How does [OH⁻] in P compare with [OH⁻] in Q?
Calculate
?Lithium hydroxide is a strong base. Calculate the pH of 0.0050 mol dm⁻³ LiOH(aq) at 298 K. Kw = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ mol² dm⁻⁶.
Mission check

?Two samples of rain are tested with a pH meter. Sample A has pH 4.2; sample B has pH 5.7. How does [H⁺] in sample A compare with [H⁺] in sample B?

Back to the video. Lemon juice (pH ≈ 2) and coffee (pH ≈ 5) differ by 3 units: 10³, so about 1000 times more H⁺ in the lemon juice.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Air is mostly nitrogen and oxygen, which can react to form NO. Why isn't the air turning into NO?

Make a guess: Air is mostly nitrogen and oxygen, which can react to form NO. Why isn't the air turning into NO?

Mission 5

Dynamic equilibrium

  • A reversible reaction can go both ways: ⇌.
  • In a closed system, start with reactants only.
  • Forward rate falls as reactants are used up. Backward rate rises as products form.
  • At dynamic equilibrium the two rates are equal, and not zero.
  • Concentrations are then constant, but not usually equal.
Mission 5

Signs of an equilibrium

  • Observable properties (colour, pressure, density) stay constant.
  • Reactants and products are both present.
  • It needs a closed system: nothing enters or leaves.
  • Physical changes reach equilibrium too: Br2(l) ⇌ Br2(g) in a stoppered flask.
  • The same equilibrium can be reached from either direction (same T, same overall amounts).
Quick check
?Hydrogen and iodine are heated in a sealed flask at constant temperature until the amount of hydrogen iodide no longer changes: H2(g) + I2(g) ⇌ 2HI(g). Which statement must be true of the mixture at this point?
Mission 5

The equilibrium constant expression

  • For aA + bB ⇌ cC + dD:
  • Kc = [C]ᶜ[D]ᵈ / ([A]ᵃ[B]ᵇ), where [X] is the equilibrium concentration of X in mol dm⁻³.
  • Products on top. Each coefficient becomes a power. Terms are multiplied.
  • Worked: CH4(g) + H2O(g) ⇌ CO(g) + 3H2(g) gives Kc = [CO][H2]³ / ([CH4][H2O]).
  • Steam, H2O(g), is a species like any other.
Mission 5

Homogeneous and heterogeneous

  • Homogeneous equilibrium: all species in the same phase.
  • Heterogeneous equilibrium: species in more than one phase.
  • Solids and pure liquids are left out of K: their concentrations are constant.
  • CaCO3(s) ⇌ CaO(s) + CO2(g): Kc = [CO2].
  • C(s) + H2O(g) ⇌ CO(g) + H2(g): Kc = [CO][H2] / [H2O]. Steam is a gas here, so it stays in.
Quick check
?Which is the equilibrium constant expression for 4NH3(g) + 5O2(g) ⇌ 4NO(g) + 6H2O(g)?
Mission 5

Calculating Kc, with units

  • 2SO2(g) + O2(g) ⇌ 2SO3(g). Equilibrium (example values): [SO2] = 0.20, [O2] = 0.10, [SO3] = 1.8 mol dm⁻³.
  • Kc = [SO3]² / ([SO2]²[O2]) = 1.8² ÷ (0.20² × 0.10) = 3.24 ÷ 0.0040 = 810.
  • Units: put the units into the expression and cancel.
  • (mol dm⁻³)² ÷ [(mol dm⁻³)² × mol dm⁻³] = 1 ÷ mol dm⁻³ = dm³ mol⁻¹.
  • Kc = 8.1 × 10² dm³ mol⁻¹ (2 s.f., as the data).
  • Same total power top and bottom: the units cancel, so Kc has no units.
Quick check
?N2(g) + 3H2(g) ⇌ 2NH3(g). At equilibrium, [N2] = 0.50, [H2] = 0.40 and [NH3] = 0.30 mol dm⁻³. What is Kc?
Mission 5

What the size of K tells you

KAt equilibrium
K ≫ 1almost all products: reaction nearly complete
K > 1products favoured
K ≈ 1significant amounts of both
K < 1reactants favoured
K ≪ 1almost all reactants: hardly any reaction
  • K tells you how far a reaction goes, not how fast.
  • Air: N2(g) + O2(g) ⇌ 2NO(g) has K ≈ 4 × 10⁻³¹ at 298 K. At equilibrium, air holds hardly any NO.
  • Large Kc: position of equilibrium lies to the right. Small Kc: to the left.
Quick check
?For the reaction X(g) ⇌ Y(g), K = 1.0 × 10¹⁵ at 298 K. Which statement about this reaction at 298 K is correct?
Mission 5

What changes K?

  • At a fixed temperature, K is constant.
  • Changing a concentration or the pressure, or adding a catalyst: K is unchanged.
  • Only temperature changes K.
  • Forward reaction endothermic (ΔH > 0): K increases as T rises.
  • Forward reaction exothermic (ΔH < 0): K decreases as T rises.
  • When K changes, the mixture reacts until the concentration ratio equals the new K. That is how K controls the position.
Quick check
?CO2(g) + H2(g) ⇌ CO(g) + H2O(g). The forward reaction is endothermic. Which change increases the value of Kc?
Mission check

?A mixture of hydrogen and chlorine can be kept in the dark for days with no visible change. Yet for H2(g) + Cl2(g) ⇌ 2HCl(g), K ≈ 2 × 10³³ at 298 K. What do these facts tell you?

K tells you how far, not how fast. Air is the opposite case: for N2 + O2 ⇌ 2NO, K ≈ 4 × 10⁻³¹ at 298 K, so air at equilibrium holds hardly any NO. The reaction is endothermic, so K is larger at high temperature: hot car engines do make some NO.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • An indicator is a weak acid, HInd ⇌ H⁺ + Ind⁻, with two differently coloured forms.
  • It changes colour around pH = pKa; pick one that changes within the steep section.
  • Weak acid + strong base: phenolphthalein. Strong acid + weak base: methyl orange. Weak + weak: none.

Mission 2

  • Concentration–time: straight line = zero order; constant halving time = first order.
  • Rate–concentration: horizontal = 0; straight through origin = 1; upward curve = 2.
  • Clock reactions: rate ∝ 1/t.

Mission 3

  • Acid = proton donor; base = proton acceptor (using a lone pair). Conjugate pairs differ by one H⁺.
  • Water, like HSO4⁻ and HPO4²⁻, is amphiprotic: its partner decides its role.
  • Mono-, di- and tribasic acids release one, two and three H⁺ per molecule.

Mission 4

  • pH = −log10[H⁺]; one unit is a tenfold change in [H⁺].
  • Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻] = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ mol² dm⁻⁶ at 298 K: for a strong base, [H⁺] = Kw ÷ [OH⁻].

Mission 5

  • Dynamic equilibrium: equal forward and backward rates, constant concentrations, closed system.
  • K = products over reactants, coefficients as powers. K ≫ 1: mostly products; K ≪ 1: mostly reactants.
  • Kc units come from cancelling mol dm⁻³. Only temperature changes Kc.
