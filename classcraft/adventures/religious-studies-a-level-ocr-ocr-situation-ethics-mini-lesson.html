Situation Ethics

Joseph Fletcher Situation Ethics is a relativist, consequentialist Christian theory with a single absolute rule: in every situation, do the most loving thing. That love is agape — selfless, unconditional goodwill toward others.

Work through each screen, weigh the range of scholarly views, answer the questions as you go and collect ⭐ stars. Press Start when you are ready.

The middle way

Three approaches to ethics

Fletcher places his theory between two extremes:

  • Legalism — following fixed rules whatever the situation (he thinks this is heartless).
  • Antinomianism — having no rules or principles at all (he thinks this is chaotic).
  • Situationism — bringing the moral wisdom of the community to each situation, but always ready to set a rule aside for love.

The one and only intrinsic good is agape love; everything else is good only if it serves love.

Four working principles

The four working principles

Fletcher gives four working principles for applying love:

  • Pragmatism — the loving course must actually work in practice.
  • Relativism — there are no absolute rules except love; everything else is relative to it.
  • Positivism — we freely choose to put faith in love as our starting point; it is not proven by reason.
  • Personalism — people come first, not laws; ethics serves persons.

Six propositions

The six fundamental principles

Fletcher also states six fundamental principles. Key ones to know:

  • Only love is intrinsically good.
  • Love is the only norm — it replaces all other laws.
  • Love and justice are the same: 'justice is love distributed'.
  • Love wills the neighbour's good whether we like them or not.
  • The end (the most loving outcome) justifies the means.
  • Love decides there and then, situationally, not by prescribed rules.

Conscience and scripture

Conscience and biblical roots

For Fletcher, conscience is not a faculty or a store of rules but a verb — the act of making loving decisions in the moment.

He appeals to Jesus, who set aside religious rules for the sake of love — for example healing on the Sabbath and putting people before legalistic observance. Love, not law, is the heart of Christian ethics.

Contrast with Natural Law: Natural Law is absolutist and rule-based; Situation Ethics is relativist and outcome-based. Both claim to be genuinely Christian.

Evaluation

Strengths and criticisms

Strengths: flexible, compassionate, person-centred, and fits Jesus emphasis on love.

Criticisms:

  • Too subjective and individualistic — people can rationalise almost anything as 'loving'.
  • Consequences are hard to predict, so we cannot be sure what love requires.
  • It could justify harmful acts if they seem loving in the moment.
  • The Catholic Church (Pope Pius XII) condemned it as undermining moral law; 'agape' is left vague.

Recap

The big ideas to take into the exam

Middle way: between legalism and antinomianism; agape is the only absolute

Working principles: pragmatism, relativism, positivism, personalism

Six propositions: love is the only norm; justice is love distributed; the end justifies the means

Conscience: a verb: the act of deciding lovingly; rooted in Jesus example

Evaluation: flexible and compassionate, but subjective, hard to predict and open to abuse
