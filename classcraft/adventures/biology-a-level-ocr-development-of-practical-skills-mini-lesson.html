Mission 1

Three kinds of variable

  • Independent variable: the one you change on purpose.
  • Dependent variable: the one you measure. It may depend on the independent variable.
  • Controlled variables: everything else that could affect the result. Keep them constant.
  • Example: lamp distance (independent) → bubbles of gas per minute from pondweed (dependent). Temperature, CO2 supply and the piece of pondweed stay the same.
  • Change one variable at a time. If two change together, you cannot tell which caused the effect.
  • The hook: fertiliser and light changed together. The result is confounded.
Quick check
?A student places a lamp at different distances from a beaker of pondweed in sodium hydrogencarbonate solution kept at 25 °C, and counts the bubbles of gas released per minute at each distance. Which correctly identifies the variables?
Mission 1

Controls

  • A control is a set-up identical to the others except that the factor being tested is absent (or at a standard level).
  • It shows what happens without the factor, so any difference can be put down to that factor.
  • Negative control: no effect expected (e.g. boiled enzyme, or water in place of an extract).
  • Positive control: an effect is known to occur. It shows the method can detect one.
  • A control need not show "nothing". It gives the baseline for comparison.
  • Say: "temperature was a controlled variable" — not: "temperature was the control".
Quick check
?A student tests the claim that an extract from a hypothetical plant contains an enzyme that speeds up the breakdown of compound P. When P breaks down, the solution turns yellow. Tube 1 contains P, buffer and the extract. Tube 2 contains P and buffer, with water in place of the extract. Both tubes are kept at 25 °C, and the time each takes to turn yellow is recorded. Which statement best justifies including tube 2?
Mission 1

Questions and hypotheses

  • A testable question names what you will change and what you will measure: "Does light intensity affect the rate of photosynthesis in pondweed?"
  • A hypothesis is a provisional explanation that can be tested: "Rate rises with light intensity, because light drives the light-dependent reactions."
  • A prediction says what you expect to measure if the hypothesis is right.
  • The null hypothesis says the independent variable has no effect (or there is no difference). Statistical tests check whether the data let you reject it.
  • Data can support a hypothesis. They never prove it.
Quick check
?A student plans to test whether the number of double bonds in a fatty acid affects its melting point, using fatty acids with 0, 1, 2 and 3 double bonds that all have the same chain length. Which statement is an appropriate null hypothesis for this investigation?
Mission 1

Measuring the dependent variable

  • Biologists often measure a rate: change ÷ time.
  • Product formed over time: change in amount ÷ time taken.
  • e.g. 15 cm³ of oxygen in 30 s → 15 ÷ 30 = 0.50 cm³ s⁻¹.
  • Time for a fixed change (a disc rises, a colour appears): rate ∝ 1 ÷ time. A disc that rises in 25 s → 1 ÷ 25 = 0.040 s⁻¹.
  • A shorter time means a faster rate.
  • Measure the initial rate where you can: the reaction slows as substrate is used up.
  • Choose apparatus that suits the size of the change: a gas syringe or a colorimeter beats judging by eye.
Quick check
?A filter-paper disc soaked in catalase solution sinks in hydrogen peroxide and rises when enough oxygen has formed on it. In 1% hydrogen peroxide the disc took 50 s to rise; in 2% it took 20 s. Which statement about the reaction rate is correct?
Mission 1

Experiment, observation or model?

  • Experiment: you manipulate the independent variable and control the rest. Best evidence for cause and effect.
  • Observation (survey, field sampling): you record what is already there. Shows patterns and correlations, not cause.
  • Example: barnacle A grows only where barnacle B is absent (observation). Scrape B off marked plots and A moves in (field experiment).
  • Even a good experiment only indicates a cause; other differences may remain.
Quick check
?Ecologists want to test the hypothesis that two limpet species compete for algae on a shore. Which approach tests the hypothesis by experiment rather than by observation?
Mission 1

Repeats, samples and validity

  • Living things vary. One measurement may be unusual, so repeat and use the mean.
  • Repeats also expose anomalies: results far from the others.
  • Sample size: more individuals make the mean more likely to be near the true population mean.
  • Sample selection: choose at random, so the sample represents the population and no one picks "good" specimens.
  • Valid: the method answers the question asked. It needs controlled variables and a suitable dependent variable.
  • Results that come out the same when repeated are repeatable; when someone else repeats them with other equipment, reproducible.
  • Repeats cannot rescue an invalid design: the hook's plants could be measured 100 times and still be confounded.
Mission 1

Tables and graphs

  • Table: independent variable in the first column; repeats, then mean. Units in the headings, never in the cells. Same number of decimal places down each column.
  • Graph: independent variable on the x-axis, dependent on the y-axis.
  • Label each axis with the quantity and its unit.
  • e.g. "Rate / cm³ s⁻¹".
  • Choose a scale that uses over half the grid, in even steps.
  • Line graph: continuous independent variable. Bar chart: categories.
  • Scatter graph: looking for a correlation between two measured variables.
  • Do not extend a line beyond your data.

Mission 2

Accurate or precise?

  • Accurate: close to the true value.
  • Precise: repeats agree closely, with little spread about the mean.
  • Random error: unpredictable scatter (judging an end point, natural variation). It lowers precision. Repeats and a mean reduce its effect.
  • Systematic error: shifts every reading the same way (a balance not zeroed, a thermometer reading 2 °C high). It lowers accuracy. Repeats do not remove it; calibrating does.
  • Resolution: the smallest change an instrument can show.
  • Say: "precise but not accurate" — not: "precise, so accurate".
Quick check
?Two students each measured the angle of curvature of the same seedling shoot five times with a protractor. Student A recorded 28°, 29°, 28°, 29°, 28°. Student B recorded 22°, 35°, 30°, 26°, 37°. A later check showed that the true angle was 35° and that Student A had aligned the protractor with the edge of the pot instead of the vertical part of the stem. Which conclusion is justified?
Mission 2

Uncertainty and percentage error

  • Every reading has an uncertainty. On a scale: ± half the smallest division (thermometer marked every 1 °C: ±0.5 °C). Use the value in the question if one is given.
  • OCR form: % error = 2 × absolute uncertainty ÷ quantity measured × 100%.
  • The 2 is there because the value comes from two readings (start and end), each with its own uncertainty.
  • Worked example: water warms from 20.0 °C to 35.0 °C; each reading ±0.5 °C.
  • Change = 15.0 °C. % error = 2 × 0.5 ÷ 15.0 × 100 = 6.7%.
  • A larger measured quantity gives a smaller percentage error: measure bigger changes, or over longer times.
Quick check
?A potato cylinder's mass falls from 3.62 g to 3.40 g in a sucrose solution. The balance reads to ±0.01 g. What is the percentage error in the change in mass?
Mission 2

Anomalies, limitations, improvements

  • Anomaly: a result that does not fit the pattern. Look for a cause; repeat it; leave it out of the mean only with a stated reason.
  • Limitation: a feature of the method that reduces validity, accuracy or precision.
  • Match the improvement to the problem:
    • random scatter → more repeats, then a mean;
    • systematic error → zero or calibrate the instrument;
    • low resolution → an instrument with finer divisions;
    • an uncontrolled variable → control it (water bath, buffer).
  • Evaluate: does the method actually meet the aim of the investigation?

Mission 2

Safety, records and sources

  • Hazard: something that could cause harm (e.g. hydrogen peroxide is an irritant).
  • Risk: how likely harm is, and how bad. A risk assessment lists hazard → risk → control measure (goggles, low concentration).
  • Keep a dated lab book: method, raw data, any changes made.
  • Research from reliable sources: peer-reviewed papers, textbooks, official data.
  • Cite each source: author(s), year, title, where published (journal, publisher or web address and date accessed).

Mission 3

Three averages and a range

  • Mean = sum of values ÷ number of values.
  • Median = the middle value when the values are in order (for an even number, halfway between the middle two).
  • Mode = the most frequent value.
  • Range = largest − smallest.
  • One extreme value pulls the mean towards it. The median hardly moves.
Quick check
?The heights in centimetres of nine students are: 150, 152, 152, 155, 158, 160, 163, 170 and 175. What is the median height?
Mission 3

Standard deviation: the typical spread

  • Standard deviation (SD): how far values typically lie from the mean.
  • Small SD: values cluster tightly. Large SD: values are widely spread.
  • Two samples can share a mean and differ hugely in SD: the hook.
  • For data with a normal (bell-shaped) distribution, about 68% of values lie within ±1 SD of the mean, and about 95% within ±2 SD.
  • SD has the same unit as the data.
  • A large SD is not a "mistake". In biology it often reflects real variation between individuals.

Mission 3

SD or range?

  • The range uses only two values: the largest and the smallest.
  • One outlying result sets the range on its own.
  • The SD uses every value, so it describes the spread of the whole data set.
  • So SD is usually the more useful measure of dispersion, especially where there is an outlying result.
  • Still check an outlier: is it an anomaly (a mistake) or real variation?
  • Say: "SD shows the spread of the data about the mean" — not: "SD shows how many errors were made".

Mission 3

Percentage change and percentage difference

  • Percentage change = (final − initial) ÷ initial × 100.
  • Example: stomatal density rises from 120 to 150 per mm². (150 − 120) ÷ 120 × 100 = +25%.
  • A fall gives a negative value.
  • Always divide by the initial value for a change.
Quick check
?In a country, the number of new COVID-19 cases recorded per day fell from 2000 in one week to 1600 in the following week. What is the percentage change in the daily number of cases?
Mission 3

Reading graphs and reporting numbers

  • Gradient = change in y ÷ change in x: a rate.
  • Oxygen rises from 4.0 to 16.0 cm³ between 10 s and 40 s: 12.0 ÷ 30 = 0.40 cm³ s⁻¹.
  • Intercept: where the line crosses an axis. Where % change in mass = 0, the solution is isotonic with the tissue.
  • Describe a trend with data: "rises from 4.0 to 16.0 over 30 s, then levels off", with units.
  • Significant figures: give a calculated answer to the same number as the least precise data used.
Mission check

Same mean, different spread: the SD is the number that tells two such data sets apart.

Mission 4

What an error bar can show

  • A bar or point shows a mean. The error bar shows uncertainty or spread.
  • Error bars can show different things. Read the legend first.
  • ±1 SD: the spread of the individual values.
  • Bars from the range (min to max) are sometimes drawn too. They depend on only two values.
Mission 4

Reading SD bars

  • Compare two means, each with ±1 SD bars.
  • Bars overlap widely: many individuals in the two groups are alike. The difference in means may well be due to chance.
  • Bars do not overlap: the difference may be real.
  • SD bars ignore sample size, so they cannot settle it. A statistical test (e.g. a t-test) decides.
  • A small SD means repeats agree closely: a more precise, more repeatable measurement.
Quick check
?Five potato cylinders were bathed in 0.4 mol dm⁻³ sucrose for 24 hours. The mean percentage change in mass was −3.0% with a standard deviation of 1.0%; the mean percentage change in length was −2.0% with a standard deviation of 2.5%. What do these data indicate?
Mission check

?A student grew bean seedlings with fertiliser X or fertiliser Y, 10 plants each. Mean root length ± 1 standard deviation: X, 42 mm (bar from 33 to 51 mm); Y, 47 mm (bar from 39 to 55 mm). Which conclusion is best supported?

Bar heights alone mean little. The spread around each mean decides whether a difference could just be chance.

Mission 5

Could it just be chance?

  • Samples vary, so two groups almost never match exactly.
  • Start with the null hypothesis: there is no difference (or no association, or no correlation).
  • A statistical test turns the data into one number, the test statistic.
  • Compare it with the critical value at p = 0.05.
  • Above the critical value: if the null hypothesis were true, a result this extreme would happen by chance less than 5% of the time. The result is significant: reject the null hypothesis.
  • Below it: not significant. The difference could be due to chance.
  • Then accept the null hypothesis.
  • Significant does not mean proved; not significant does not prove "no effect".
Mission 5

Which test?

  • Chi-squared (χ²): counts in categories, compared with expected counts.
  • Student's t-test: two means of continuous data (e.g. lengths, masses).
  • Correlation coefficient: is there a relationship between two variables measured on the same individuals or sites?
  • OCR names Spearman's rank correlation coefficient.
  • Ask: Counts or measurements? Difference or relationship?
  • t-tests and correlation coefficients are worked through in Mission 6.
Quick check
?A student measures the lengths of 20 leaves from plants growing in the sun and 20 from plants growing in the shade. She wants to know whether mean leaf length differs between the two. Which statistical test should she use?

Mission 5

Correlation

  • Plot two measured variables on a scatter graph (scattergram).
  • Positive correlation: both rise together. Negative: one rises as the other falls.
  • A correlation coefficient runs from −1 to +1.
  • The sign gives the direction; the size gives the strength. 0 means no correlation.
  • −0.85 is as strong as +0.85.
Mission 5

Correlation is not cause

  • A correlation, even a strong one, can arise from:
    • chance (small samples);
    • a third variable linked to both (a confounding variable);
    • cause running the other way.
  • Evidence for cause: a mechanism; controlled experiments or randomised controlled trials; the effect grows with the dose; many studies agree.
  • Say: "there is a positive correlation between X and Y" — not: "X causes Y", unless there is evidence for cause.

Mission 6

Student's t-test

  • Use it to compare two means of continuous data that are roughly normally distributed.
  • Null hypothesis: there is no significant difference between the two means.
  • t gets bigger when the means are further apart, the SDs are smaller, or the samples are larger.
  • t = |x̄1 − x̄2| ÷ √(s1²/n1 + s2²/n2)
  • Degrees of freedom = n1 + n2 − 2.
  • t ≥ critical value at p = 0.05: reject the null hypothesis. The difference is significant.
  • The formula (unpaired) is provided in the exam, with the critical values table.

  • Why it differs (wave action? predators?) needs a separate hypothesis and investigation.

Mission 6

Paired t-test

  • Use when the same individuals are measured twice (before and after), or in matched pairs.
  • For each pair, find the difference, d. Then d̄ = mean difference; sd = SD of the differences.
  • t = d̄√n ÷ sd, where n = number of pairs.
  • Degrees of freedom = n − 1.
  • Pairing removes the variation between individuals, so a real effect is easier to detect.
Mission 6

Spearman's rank correlation

  • Tests whether two variables measured on the same individuals or sites are correlated.
  • OCR names Spearman's rank correlation coefficient; its formula is provided.
  • Null hypothesis: there is no correlation between the two variables.
  • Rank each variable separately (smallest = 1). Tied values share the mean of their ranks.
  • d = difference between the two ranks for each pair.
  • rₛ = 1 − 6Σd² ÷ n(n² − 1).
  • Compare |rₛ| with the critical value for n pairs. Equal or above: significant correlation.

Mission 7

Measuring the rate

  • Rate = amount of product formed (or substrate used) per unit time.
  • Measure product appearing (e.g. oxygen from catalase) or substrate disappearing (e.g. starch, with iodine).
  • A product–time curve is steep at first, then flattens as substrate is used up.
  • Initial rate: the gradient at time zero, before the substrate runs down. The fairest way to compare conditions.
  • Timing a fixed end point (a colour change, a disc rising)? Rate is proportional to 1 ÷ time.
Mission 7

Worked example: initial rate from a tangent

Illustrative data: oxygen released by catalase, plotted against time.

1. Draw a straight line touching the curve at t = 0: the tangent.

2. Pick two points far apart on the tangent: (0 s, 0) and (40 s, 36).

3. Gradient = change in y ÷ change in x = 36 ÷ 40.

  • Initial rate = 0.90 cm³ s⁻¹.
  • Same method at any point on a curve: tangent there, then gradient.
  • Fixed end point: a disc took 25 s to rise. Rate = 1 ÷ 25 = 0.040 s⁻¹.
Quick check
?Catalase was added to hydrogen peroxide and the volume of oxygen collected was recorded: 0 s, 0 cm³; 20 s, 12 cm³; 40 s, 20 cm³; 60 s, 24 cm³ (illustrative data). What is the best estimate of the initial rate of reaction from these data?
Mission 7

Temperature

  • Warmer → molecules have more kinetic energy → they move faster.
  • More frequent collisions between substrate and active sites, and more with enough energy → more enzyme–substrate complexes per second → faster rate.
  • Above the optimum, vibrations break hydrogen and ionic bonds that hold the tertiary structure.
  • The active site changes shape; the substrate no longer fits. The enzyme is denatured.
  • Denaturation is usually permanent: cooling does not restore the shape.
  • In the cold, enzymes are slow, not denatured.
  • Optima differ: many human enzymes ≈ 37 °C; DNA polymerase from a hot-spring bacterium ≈ 75 °C.
  • Say: "the enzyme is denatured" — not: "the enzyme is killed".
Mission 7

Q10, the temperature coefficient

  • Q10 = R2 ÷ R1: the rate at (T + 10 °C) divided by the rate at T.
  • Illustrative: rate 0.42 at 20 °C; 0.86 at 30 °C. Q10 = 0.86 ÷ 0.42 = 2.0. No units.
  • Below the optimum, Q10 for an enzyme-controlled reaction is often about 2: the rate roughly doubles per 10 °C.
  • Above the optimum: rate 1.50 at 40 °C; 0.45 at 50 °C. Q10 = 0.30. Below 1: denaturation.
Quick check
?A student measured the rate of an enzyme-catalysed reaction at six temperatures. The rates, in arbitrary units, were: 10 °C, 2; 20 °C, 4; 30 °C, 8; 40 °C, 15; 50 °C, 9; 60 °C, 1 (illustrative data). Which conclusion is supported by these data?
Mission 7

pH

  • pH measures the concentration of hydrogen ions (H⁺).
  • H⁺ ions change the charges on R groups (e.g. –COO⁻ + H⁺ → –COOH).
  • Ionic and hydrogen bonds change → the active site's shape and charges change → substrate binds less well.
  • Far from the optimum the enzyme is denatured.
  • Optima differ: pepsin (stomach) ≈ pH 2; salivary amylase ≈ pH 7; trypsin (small intestine) ≈ pH 8.
  • Say: "pH changes the charges on R groups" — not: "pH slows the molecules down".
Quick check
?Pepsin has an optimum pH of about 2. When it passes with food into the small intestine, where the pH is about 8, its activity falls almost to zero. Which explanation is correct?
Mission 7

Substrate and enzyme concentration

  • More substrate: more collisions with active sites → faster rate.
  • At high substrate concentration, every active site is occupied almost all the time: the enzyme is saturated. The rate levels off; enzyme concentration now limits it.
  • More enzyme: more active sites → rate rises in proportion, as long as substrate is in excess.
  • A rate–concentration plateau (saturation) is not the same as a product–time curve flattening (substrate used up).
  • Say: "the active sites are saturated" — not: "the enzyme is used up".

Mission 7Practical

Rate of an enzyme-controlled reaction

  • PAG4: effects of pH, temperature, enzyme and substrate concentration.
  • Method (catalase and hydrogen peroxide): put substrate and enzyme in separate tubes in a water bath to reach the set temperature.
  • Mix; collect oxygen in a gas syringe; read the volume every 10 s.
  • Repeat at five or more values of the independent variable, three times each.
  • Plot volume against time; tangent at t = 0 gives the initial rate.
  • Control: temperature (water bath), pH (buffer), volumes and concentrations not being tested. Control tube: boiled enzyme.
  • Serial dilution for concentrations: 5.0 cm³ of 2.0% solution + 5.0 cm³ water → 1.0%; repeat → 0.50%, 0.25%, 0.125%.
Mission check

?An enzyme from a cold-water fish has an optimum temperature of about 15 °C. A sample was kept at 45 °C for 10 minutes and then cooled back to 15 °C. Its activity at 15 °C was only 5% of that of an unheated sample tested at the same time (illustrative data). Which explanation is correct?

Heat speeds enzymes by speeding collisions, until it shakes apart the bonds that shape the active site. Then the rate falls, and usually stays down.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Change one variable, measure one, keep the rest constant; a control gives the baseline for comparison.
  • A null hypothesis says "no effect"; data can support a hypothesis but never prove it.
  • Experiments manipulate a variable; observations record patterns. Random, repeated samples make a mean trustworthy.

Mission 2

  • Precise = repeats agree; accurate = close to the true value. Repeats reduce random error; calibration removes systematic error.
  • % error = 2 × absolute uncertainty ÷ quantity measured × 100%: the 2 covers the two readings.

Mission 3

  • Mean, median and mode describe the centre; SD describes the typical spread about the mean, using every value.
  • SD is usually more useful than the range, which depends only on the two extreme values.

Mission 4

  • Read the legend: SD bars show the spread of individuals; SE and confidence bars show how precisely each mean is known.
  • Overlapping SD bars: the difference may be due to chance; a statistical test decides.

Mission 5

  • Null hypothesis → test statistic → compare with the critical value at p = 0.05 → significant or not. Neither outcome is proof.
  • χ² compares counts with expected counts; degrees of freedom = categories − 1.
  • A correlation coefficient measures direction and strength (−1 to +1); correlation alone never shows cause.

Mission 6

  • t-test: two means; t = |x̄1 − x̄2| ÷ √(s1²/n1 + s2²/n2); df = n1 + n2 − 2.
  • Spearman's rank: rₛ = 1 − 6Σd² ÷ n(n² − 1); compare |rₛ| with the critical value for n pairs.
  • At or above the critical value at p = 0.05: significant. Never "proved".

Mission 7

  • Temperature and substrate concentration raise the rate by increasing collisions, until denaturation (temperature) or saturation (substrate) takes over; pH acts on the charges that hold the active site's shape.
  • Compare initial rates, found from the tangent at time zero.
  • Q10 = R2 ÷ R1, about 2 below the optimum.
