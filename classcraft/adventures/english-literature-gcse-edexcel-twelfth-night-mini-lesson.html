Mission 1

A love story and a prank on a steward. Why put both in one play?

Make a guess: A love story and a prank on a steward. Why put both in one play?

Twelfth Night · structure and the double plotScreen 1

The play in five acts

Twelfth Night was probably written in 1601; a law student saw it at Middle Temple on 2 February 1602. It was first printed in the First Folio of 1623.

ActWhat happens
1Duke Orsino woos Countess Olivia, who is mourning her brother. Shipwrecked Viola, thinking her twin Sebastian drowned, disguises herself as a young man, Cesario, and serves Orsino. He sends "Cesario" to woo Olivia, who falls for "him".
2Sebastian is alive, cared for by Antonio. Malvolio, Olivia's steward, stops Sir Toby's midnight party; Maria forges a love letter, and Malvolio believes Olivia loves him.
3Olivia declares her love to Cesario. Malvolio appears smiling in yellow stockings and is thought mad. Sir Toby sets Sir Andrew to duel Cesario. Antonio is arrested.
4Olivia mistakes Sebastian for Cesario; they marry in secret. Feste, disguised as a curate, torments Malvolio in a dark room.
5The twins meet. Orsino will marry Viola. The trick is revealed; Malvolio storms off vowing revenge.

How your exam asks this

  • Edexcel Paper 1 Section A (closed book): (a) the language of an extract of about 30 lines, 20 marks (AO2); (b) how a theme from that extract is explored elsewhere in the play, 20 marks, with context (AO3) counting for 5 of them.
  • Essay practice is for class. These missions build what every answer needs: knowing the play, its methods and its key lines.
Twelfth Night · structure and the double plotScreen 3

Two plots in one house

  • Main plot (love): Orsino loves Olivia; Olivia loves Cesario; Cesario (Viola) loves Orsino. A love triangle built on disguise.
  • Subplot (the gulling): Sir Toby, Sir Andrew, Maria, Fabian and Feste trick Malvolio. To gull someone is to fool them.
  • Both plots live in Olivia's household, so her servants and kin keep running into the love plot.
  • A double plot lets Shakespeare show the same ideas, such as desire and self-deception, from two angles.

Twelfth Night · structure and the double plotScreen 7

How the knot comes undone

  • In Act 5 Scene 1 every plot meets on one stage. Orsino sees the twins together: "One face, one voice, one habit, and two persons!"
  • Viola can't dress as a woman yet: the Captain who has "my maid’s garments" is "in durance, at Malvolio’s suit". The love plot's ending waits on the gulling plot's victim.
  • Malvolio leaves vowing "I’ll be revenged on the whole pack of you." One thread stays knotted.
  • Feste ends alone, singing that "our play is done".

Mission 2

Viola could just tell Orsino the truth. Why stay in disguise?

Make a guess: Viola could just tell Orsino the truth. Why stay in disguise?

Twelfth Night · Viola and disguiseScreen 1

Alone in Illyria

  • Viola is shipwrecked on the coast of Illyria. Her father is dead and she thinks her twin, Sebastian, has drowned (Act 1 Scene 2).
  • She has no family to protect her in a strange country.
  • She asks the Captain: "Conceal me what I am, and be my aid / For such disguise as haply shall become / The form of my intent."
  • As the young man Cesario she can serve Duke Orsino; "What else may hap, to time I will commit;".

Twelfth Night · Viola and disguiseScreen 4

Dramatic irony

  • Dramatic irony: the audience knows something a character doesn't.
  • Orsino praises Cesario's "Diana’s lip" and voice "as the maiden’s organ" without knowing he is describing a woman.
  • An aside (lines other characters don't hear) lets Viola share her secret with us.
  • So each Orsino–Viola scene works on two levels: what he hears, and what we hear.
  • "a woman’s part" also means a stage role: more on boy actors in mission 5.

Twelfth Night · Viola and disguiseScreen 7

Why keep the disguise?

  • The truth would end her place at Orsino's side while he loves Olivia.
  • Disguise lets her speak her love in code: "I am all the daughters of my father’s house".
  • She trusts time: "O time, thou must untangle this, not I," (2.2).
  • But disguise costs her: "Disguise, I see thou art a wickedness" (2.2). Facing a duel she nearly confesses "how much I lack of a man." (3.4)

Mission 3

Malvolio is tricked, mocked and locked in the dark. Funny or cruel?

Make a guess: Malvolio is tricked, mocked and locked in the dark. Funny or cruel?

Twelfth Night · MalvolioScreen 1

The steward

  • Malvolio is Olivia's steward: the senior servant who runs her household.
  • Olivia tells him: "O, you are sick of self-love, Malvolio," (1.5).
  • He stops Sir Toby's midnight party: "Is there no respect of place, persons, nor time, in you?" (2.3).
  • Maria calls him "a kind of Puritan", then a "time-pleaser" sure that "all that look on him love him." (2.3)
  • Puritans were strict Protestants who attacked plays and festive excess.

Twelfth Night · MalvolioScreen 4

"To be Count Malvolio"

  • Before he finds the letter, he dreams aloud: "To be Count Malvolio." (2.5)
  • He pictures himself "in my branched velvet gown", telling Toby: "You must amend your drunkenness."
  • He even cites a precedent: "The lady of the Strachy married the yeoman of the wardrobe."
  • The letter feeds this: "Some are born great, some achieve greatness, and some have greatness thrust upon ’em."
  • So the trick works on his vanity and ambition, not his Puritanism.

Twelfth Night · MalvolioScreen 6

Comic or cruel?

  • Comic: his pride invites the fall. Fabian hopes the "sportful malice" will "rather pluck on laughter than revenge," (5.1).
  • Cruel: he is shut in darkness and told he is mad. Even Toby wants out: "I would we were well rid of this knavery." (4.2). Olivia: "He hath been most notoriously abus’d." (5.1)
  • He leaves: "I’ll be revenged on the whole pack of you."
  • Charles Lamb (1822), recalling a performance, felt Malvolio was not naturally ridiculous and his fall had tragic interest.

?Malvolio is tricked, mocked and locked in the dark. Funny or cruel?

The trick starts as comedy, because Malvolio's own vanity and ambition make him believe the letter. But locking him in the dark and calling him mad goes further, and his furious exit leaves the ending uneasy. Strong answers argue both and show where the tone turns.

Twelfth Night · love and desireScreen 3

Love as melancholy

  • Elizabethans used the theory of the four humours: too much black bile caused melancholy. Sighing, solitary lovesickness was a familiar pose.
  • Orsino plays the part: music, flowers, and "I myself am best / When least in company." (1.4)
  • Olivia's grief is melancholy too: Valentine reports she will stay veiled "till seven years’ heat" (1.1).
  • Feste teases Orsino: "Now the melancholy god protect thee", for "thy mind is a very opal." (2.4)

Twelfth Night · love and desireScreen 6

Olivia: love at first sight

  • Olivia has refused all men, yet falls for Cesario in one meeting: "Even so quickly may one catch the plague?" (1.5)
  • She feels his "perfections" "creep in at mine eyes": love enters through sight, like an illness.
  • In Act 3 Scene 1 she woos him openly: "Love sought is good, but given unsought is better."
  • Her love is real but mistaken. Sebastian, whom she takes for Cesario, accepts at once: "If it be thus to dream, still let me sleep!" (4.1)
Twelfth Night · love and desireScreen 7

Who is left out?

  • Viola's love is patient and constant: "After him I love / More than I love these eyes, more than my life," (5.1).
  • Orsino turns from Olivia to Viola within minutes of the truth. Some readers find this too sudden; others see a love for "Cesario" growing all along.
  • Antonio, who told Sebastian "I do adore thee so," (2.1), falls silent once the twins find each other.
  • Malvolio, who loves himself most, leaves alone.

Orsino's speeches are full of his own feelings: love as appetite, music, melancholy. Olivia herself is barely there. Many readers argue he is in love with being in love, though his closeness to Cesario hints at a truer feeling that the ending lets him name.

Mission 5

A boy plays a girl playing a boy. What does Shakespeare do with that?

Make a guess: A boy plays a girl playing a boy. What does Shakespeare do with that?

Twelfth Night · gender and identityScreen 1

Layers of disguise

  • On Shakespeare's stage, boys or young men played women's parts; women didn't act on the public stage until 1660, after the Restoration.
  • So audiences saw a boy playing Viola, who plays the boy Cesario.
  • The play jokes about it: Orsino says "all is semblative a woman’s part." (1.4)
  • At the end Viola is still in "my masculine usurp’d attire" (5.1).

Twelfth Night · gender and identityScreen 4

What are men and women like?

  • Many Elizabethan writers called women the weaker, less constant sex. Orsino says no woman's heart is "So big, to hold so much; they lack retention." (2.4)
  • Viola answers: "In faith, they are as true of heart as we."
  • Olivia rules her own household and does the wooing; Maria's wit drives the subplot.
  • Manhood is mocked too: the knight Sir Andrew dreads a duel, and Viola fears it will show "how much I lack of a man." (3.4)

Twelfth Night · gender and identityScreen 6

Desire across the disguise

  • Olivia desires Cesario, a woman she thinks is a man; Orsino grows close to "Cesario" before he knows the truth.
  • Antonio's love for Sebastian is intense: "I do adore thee so, / That danger shall seem sport" (2.1).
  • Some critics read hints of same-sex desire here, helped by disguise and the boy actor; others argue the play steers firmly to marriage.
  • Sebastian tells Olivia: "You are betroth’d both to a maid and man." (5.1)

The layers (boy actor, Viola, Cesario) let Shakespeare joke about gender and test it: characters argue over what men and women are like, desire crosses the disguise, and the play ends with Viola still in men's clothes. Strong answers explore these as questions the play raises, not one fixed message.

Mission 6

The play is named for a party night. Who rules once the party starts?

Make a guess: The play is named for a party night. Who rules once the party starts?

Twelfth Night · festivity, misrule and the Elizabethan audienceScreen 1

What was Twelfth Night?

  • Twelfth Night was the last night of the twelve days of Christmas, before the feast of Epiphany on 6 January.
  • Christmas revels could be led by a Lord of Misrule: for a short time normal order was turned upside down.
  • The play's other title, What You Will, suits this: do as you please.
  • There's no evidence it was first staged on Twelfth Night; a law student saw it on 2 February 1602.

Twelfth Night · festivity, misrule and the Elizabethan audienceScreen 4

Puritans against festivity

  • Puritans attacked holiday excess: in 1583 Philip Stubbes's Anatomie of Abuses condemned Lords of Misrule, plays and drinking.
  • Their side later won: Parliament banned plays in 1642 and outlawed Christmas festivals in 1647.
  • So "cakes and ale" against "respect of place, persons, nor time" stages a live argument of the day.
  • But Malvolio has a point: it is past midnight in a house of mourning.
  • Link this context to the extract in part (b); don't just list dates.
Twelfth Night · festivity, misrule and the Elizabethan audienceScreen 5

The cost of misrule

  • Toby lives off Sir Andrew: "I have been dear to him, lad, some two thousand strong, or so." (3.2)
  • His tricks turn to violence: the false duel (3.4), then a brawl with Sebastian. Olivia: "Rudesby, be gone!" (4.1)
  • Toby himself tires: "I would we were well rid of this knavery." (4.2)
  • By Act 5 he is wounded and drunk, calls Andrew "a thin-faced knave, a gull", and has married Maria.

Twelfth Night · festivity, misrule and the Elizabethan audienceScreen 7

The party ends

  • Comedies end in marriage: three couples by Act 5, so festive disorder gives way to order.
  • Fabian jokes: "If this were played upon a stage now, I could condemn it as an improbable fiction." (3.4). The play reminds us it is a holiday from real life.
  • Feste's last song runs from childhood to old age with the refrain "For the rain it raineth every day."
  • Then he speaks to the audience: "our play is done".

Like a holiday Lord of Misrule, Sir Toby and the revellers rule for a while: they sing at midnight, defy the steward and trick him. But misrule has costs, and by Act 5 order returns through Olivia's authority and marriage, while Feste's last song reminds us the holiday is over.

Mission 7

The fool may be the wisest person in the play. How can that be?

Make a guess: The fool may be the wisest person in the play. How can that be?

Twelfth Night · Feste, songs and languageScreen 1

The allowed fool

  • Feste is Olivia's fool, a paid jester; he also sings at Orsino's court.
  • Olivia: "There is no slander in an allowed fool," (1.5): a licensed fool may mock anyone.
  • His motto: "Better a witty fool than a foolish wit." (1.5)
  • Viola: "This fellow is wise enough to play the fool," (3.1).
  • The part was probably written for Robert Armin, the company's clown from about 1599, a singer. This context helps in part (b).

Twelfth Night · Feste, songs and languageScreen 4

Verse, prose and song

  • Verse (mostly blank verse: unrhymed iambic pentameter) carries love and high feeling: Orsino, Viola, Olivia.
  • Prose carries comedy: Toby, Maria, Feste, Malvolio's letter scene.
  • Shifts matter. When Olivia unveils, Viola moves from prose into verse: "’Tis beauty truly blent". Malvolio, wronged, speaks verse in 5.1: "Madam, you have done me wrong, / Notorious wrong."
  • Rhyming couplets often close scenes: "O time, thou must untangle this, not I," (2.2).

Twelfth Night · Feste, songs and languageScreen 7

Feste's last word

  • In 5.1 Feste throws the letter, and Malvolio's Act 1 insult, back at him: "And thus the whirligig of time brings in his revenges." A whirligig is a spinning top.
  • Alone, he sings a life from boyhood to age, with the refrain "For the rain it raineth every day."
  • Then he turns to us: "And we’ll strive to please you every day."
  • Many readers hear a bittersweet end: the holiday is over.

Mission checkScreen 9

?The fool may be the wisest person in the play. How can that be?

An "allowed fool" may say what others can't. Feste uses that licence to show Olivia her grief makes no sense, to tease Orsino's changeable mind, and to sing that youth won't last. His wit sees through everyone, which is why Viola calls him "wise enough to play the fool".

Recap

Big ideas

  • Twelfth Night (probably 1601) has a love plot (Orsino, Olivia, Viola, Sebastian) and a gulling plot (Toby, Maria and the rest against Malvolio), both centred on Olivia's household.
  • Viola's "knot" couplet (Act 2 Scene 2) hands the tangle to time; Act 5 unties most of it on one stage.
  • The plots meet at key points: the ring, the duel, Sebastian's fight with Sir Andrew, and the Captain held "at Malvolio’s suit".
  • For part (b), knowing the five acts helps you find a theme from the extract elsewhere in the play quickly.
  • Viola disguises herself as Cesario after the shipwreck; the audience knows her secret from Act 1 Scene 2, which creates dramatic irony in every scene with Orsino.
  • Her asides and her "sister" story (Act 2 Scene 4) let her speak her love in code; "concealment, like a worm i’ th’ bud" shows its cost.
  • She trusts time, not action, to solve the tangle.
  • Disguise and concealed love are strong part (b) themes: link 1.4, 2.2, 2.4 and 3.4.
  • Malvolio is Olivia's steward: proud, ambitious and "sick of self-love"; the letter works on his dream "To be Count Malvolio".
  • The tone darkens from the comic letter scene (2.5) to the dark room (4.2) and his exit vowing revenge (5.1).
  • Readers have long disagreed: Lamb (1822) felt a tragic interest in him; others see a fitting comic punishment for pride.
  • For part (b), link Malvolio to context: Puritan attacks on festivity and the strict ranks of a great household.
  • Orsino presents love as an appetite to feed and kill ("If music be the food of love"), a fashionable melancholy, and contradicts himself about men's and women's constancy (2.4).
  • Olivia's love strikes "quickly" like "the plague" and lands on the wrong twin; Viola's love is silent and constant.
  • Not everyone is rewarded: Antonio and Malvolio end alone.
  • Love is a strong part (b) theme: link the extract to 1.1, 1.5, 2.4 and 5.1, with context on melancholy where it helps.
  • Boys played women's roles on Shakespeare's stage, so Viola is a boy playing a girl playing a boy; the play jokes about this ("a woman’s part").
  • Characters argue about gender (Orsino vs Viola in 2.4), and Olivia and Maria act with power; manhood is mocked in the duel.
  • Desire crosses the disguise (Olivia, Orsino, Antonio); readings of this differ.
  • For part (b), link the extract to context: boy actors and the expectations of women in Shakespeare's time.
  • Twelfth Night ended the twelve days of Christmas; misrule (order turned upside down for a short time) is the play's festive mood.
  • Act 2 Scene 3 sets "cakes and ale" against Malvolio's "respect of place, persons, nor time": a real argument of the day, as Puritans attacked revelry.
  • Misrule has costs (violence, debt, the dark room) and gives way to order and marriage; Feste's song ends the holiday.
  • For part (b), link festivity to its context: the holiday season, misrule and Puritan objections.
  • Feste is Olivia's "allowed fool", licensed to mock; he moves between both houses and sings for both.
  • Verse carries love and high feeling, prose the comedy; shifts (Viola's move to verse, Malvolio's verse in 5.1) are worth analysing.
  • His songs ("O mistress mine", "Come away, death", "the rain it raineth every day") comment on love, time and endings.
  • For part (a), spot shifts between prose and verse in your extract and say what they show.

  • There is no slander in an allowed fool, (Act 1 Scene 5 (Olivia)) The fool's licence.
  • This fellow is wise enough to play the fool, (Act 3 Scene 1 (Viola)) The wise fool.
  • Foolery, sir, does walk about the orb like the sun; it shines everywhere. (Act 3 Scene 1 (Feste)) Everyone is a fool somewhere.
  • Youth’s a stuff will not endure. (Act 2 Scene 3 (Feste's song)) Love now: time is short.
  • And thus the whirligig of time brings in his revenges. (Act 5 Scene 1 (Feste)) Time turns full circle.
  • For the rain it raineth every day. (Act 5 Scene 1 (Feste's song)) The bittersweet end.
