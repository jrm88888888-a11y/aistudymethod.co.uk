Mission 1

Why cells must communicate

  • In a multicellular organism, cells are specialised into tissues and organs.
  • Organs must coordinate their activities.
  • The organism must respond to changes in its internal and external environment.
  • Both animals and plants need this.
  • Animals use two systems: nervous (fast, electrical and chemical) and hormonal (chemicals carried in the blood).
Mission 1

Ligand meets receptor

  • Cell signalling: one cell releases a chemical messenger; another cell detects it and responds.
  • The messenger is a ligand.
  • It binds a receptor: a protein with a binding site complementary in shape and chemistry to that ligand.
  • Only target cells, which carry that receptor, respond.
  • Binding is reversible and leaves the ligand unchanged. It is the receptor that changes shape.
  • Say: "a complementary binding site" — not: "the same shape as the ligand".
Quick check
?Which statement correctly describes the relationship between a ligand and its receptor?
Mission 1

Near or far

  • Direct contact: cells touch; a molecule on one cell's surface binds a receptor on the other.
  • Short distance: the messenger diffuses to nearby cells. Neurotransmitters cross a synaptic gap of about 20 nm in under a millisecond.
  • Long distance: hormones travel in the blood, reach almost every cell, and act on target cells over minutes or longer.
Quick check
?Chemical X is released from one cell, diffuses about 20 nm and acts on a single adjacent cell within a millisecond before being broken down. Chemical Y is released into the blood and, over several minutes, acts on cells in the liver, heart and skeletal muscle. Which statement is correct?
Mission 1

Four kinds of signalling chemical

  • Hormones: secreted by endocrine glands into the blood; act on distant target cells. E.g. insulin.
  • Neurotransmitters: released by neurons into a synaptic gap; act on the adjacent cell. E.g. acetylcholine.
  • Cytokines: small proteins, mainly from immune cells; act mostly on nearby cells.
  • Calcium ions: signal inside cells. A rise in cytoplasmic Ca²⁺ triggers e.g. muscle contraction or neurotransmitter release.
  • The categories are by function, not chemistry: noradrenaline is a neurotransmitter at a synapse and a hormone in the blood.
Mission 1

Quorum sensing

  • 1: Each Vibrio fischeri cell secretes a signal, an autoinducer. At low density in open seawater it diffuses away, so its concentration stays low.
  • 2: At high density, inside a squid's light organ, autoinducer builds up past a threshold.
  • 3: Autoinducer binds a receptor protein in each cell → transcription of the lux genes → luciferase → light. The whole population switches on together.
  • A population-wide change in gene expression, set by cell density.
  • The squid uses the glow to mask its shadow from predators below.
Quick check
?Vibrio fischeri produces light when packed at high density inside the light organ of a squid, but not when living at low density in seawater. Which explanation is correct?
Mission 1

Receptor outside or inside?

  • Transmembrane receptors: for hydrophilic ligands (e.g. insulin, a protein; epinephrine (adrenaline), an amine) that cannot cross the phospholipid bilayer. The ligand stays outside.
  • Membrane-spanning parts of the receptor: hydrophobic amino acids. Parts in water on each side: hydrophilic amino acids.
  • Intracellular receptors: in the cytoplasm or nucleus, for hydrophobic ligands (steroid hormones, e.g. oestrogen, testosterone) that diffuse through the bilayer.
  • The steroid–receptor complex often binds DNA and changes gene expression.
Mission 1

Reception, transduction, response

  • Reception: the ligand binds; the receptor changes shape.
  • Transduction: the shape change sets off a sequence of molecular changes inside the cell: a signal transduction pathway.
  • Response: e.g. an enzyme activated, a gene switched on, a molecule secreted.
  • The same ligand can cause different responses in different cells: it depends on the receptor, relay and target proteins each cell contains.
  • Pathways can amplify the signal.
Quick check
?In humans, the hormone epinephrine causes liver cells to break down glycogen and release glucose, and it causes heart muscle cells to contract more strongly. Which statement best explains how the same hormone produces different responses in these two cell types?
Mission check

Mission 2

Receptors that are channels

  • Some receptors are themselves ion channels: ligand-gated channels.
  • Acetylcholine (ACh) receptor on a muscle fibre: ACh binds outside → the channel opens.
  • Na⁺ diffuses in down its concentration gradient → inside becomes less negative: depolarisation.
  • This may trigger an action potential and contraction.
  • Fast (milliseconds): no second messenger.
Quick check
?A muscle fibre has a resting membrane potential of −70 mV. When acetylcholine is applied, the potential changes to −50 mV within 1 ms. If sodium ions are first removed from the fluid outside the fibre, acetylcholine still binds but the potential stays at −70 mV. What do these results show?
Mission 2

G protein-coupled receptors

  • A GPCR is one protein chain that crosses the membrane seven times.
  • 1: The ligand binds outside → the receptor changes shape.
  • 2: The shape change activates a G protein on the inner face: it releases GDP and binds GTP.
  • 3: The active G protein switches on an effector enzyme, e.g. adenylyl cyclase.
  • The G protein switches itself off by hydrolysing GTP to GDP.
  • Humans have hundreds of different GPCRs: for smell, light, many hormones and neurotransmitters.
Mission 2

Epinephrine (adrenaline) and cAMP

  • 1: Epinephrine binds a GPCR on a liver cell. It stays outside.
  • 2: The G protein activates adenylyl cyclase, which converts ATP to cyclic AMP (cAMP): the second messenger.
  • 3: cAMP activates protein kinase A. Kinases add phosphate from ATP to the next protein, changing its shape and activity: a phosphorylation cascade.
  • 4: The last enzyme, glycogen phosphorylase, breaks down glycogen → glucose leaves the cell.
  • Phosphodiesterase breaks down cAMP, switching the signal off when epinephrine leaves.
  • Two names, one hormone: "adrenaline" (Latin ad renes, at the kidney) and "epinephrine" (Greek epi nephros, above the kidney).
  • Say: "cAMP relays the signal inside the cell" — not: "cAMP is a second hormone sent to other cells".
Mission 2Worked example

Amplification

Illustrative data: suppose each active molecule activates or makes this many of the next.

1. 1 receptor → 10 active adenylyl cyclase (via G proteins).

2. Each adenylyl cyclase → 100 cAMP. Total: 10 × 100 = 1 000 cAMP.

3. Each cAMP → 1 active kinase. Total: 1 000.

4. Each kinase → 10 active phosphorylase. Total: 10 000.

5. Each phosphorylase → 1 000 glucose. Total: 10 000 × 1 000 = 10 000 000.

  • Overall amplification = 10 × 100 × 1 × 10 × 1 000 = 10⁷.
  • Multiply the step factors; never add them. A ×1 step adds nothing.
Quick check
?Which sequence correctly describes the events after epinephrine (adrenaline) binds to its receptor on a liver cell?
Mission 2

Tyrosine kinase receptors: insulin

  • Insulin, a protein hormone, binds its receptor on the outside of the plasma membrane.
  • The receptor has tyrosine kinase activity: binding causes phosphorylation of tyrosine residues on its parts inside the cell.
  • This starts a sequence of reactions inside the cell...
  • ...ending with vesicles containing glucose transporters moving to and fusing with the plasma membrane.
  • More transporters in the membrane → more glucose enters. Fast: no new transporters are made.
Quick check
?Which sequence correctly describes how insulin increases glucose uptake by a muscle cell?
Mission 2

Feedback in signalling pathways

  • Negative feedback: the response reduces the stimulus. Keeps a variable near a set level.
  • Example: oestradiol inhibits GnRH secretion by the hypothalamus, keeping its own production in check.
  • Positive feedback: the response increases the stimulus. Drives a process to completion.
  • Example: in birth, cervix stretch → oxytocin → stronger contractions → more stretch, until delivery removes the stimulus.
  • Both are normal regulation.
  • Say: "negative feedback opposes change" — not: "negative feedback is the harmful kind".
Quick check
?During birth, stretching of the cervix stimulates secretion of oxytocin, which strengthens contractions of the uterus, which stretch the cervix further, until the baby is delivered. How should this regulation be described?
Mission check

?Drug Z inhibits phosphodiesterase, the enzyme that breaks down cAMP. Liver cells treated with Z are given a short pulse of epinephrine, which is then washed away. Compared with untreated cells, what is predicted?

Each step multiplies the signal, and each step can be switched on, off, or jammed. That is why so many drugs and toxins act on pathways.

Mission 3

Glucose in, glucose out

  • Cells need a steady supply of glucose as respiratory substrate. The brain depends on it most.
  • Raises blood glucose: digesting and absorbing carbohydrate; the liver releasing glucose from glycogen or making new glucose.
  • Lowers blood glucose: respiration (e.g. exercise); cells storing glucose as glycogen or fat.
  • Control: two pancreatic hormones, insulin and glucagon, by negative feedback.
Mission 3

The pancreas: two tissues

  • Most of the pancreas is exocrine: clusters of cells (acini) secrete digestive enzymes into a duct.
  • Scattered among them: the islets of Langerhans, the endocrine tissue.
  • β (beta) cells secrete insulin. α (alpha) cells secrete glucagon.
  • Islet cells secrete straight into capillaries: no duct.
  • The islet cells are both the receptors and the source of the hormones: no signal from the brain is needed.
Quick check
?Blood glucose concentration rises after a meal. What happens next?
Mission 3

Glucose too high: insulin

  • β cells detect the rise and secrete insulin into the blood.
  • Insulin binds receptors on target cells: liver, muscle and adipose (fat) cells.
  • In muscle and adipose cells, more glucose transporter channel proteins are put into the cell-surface membrane, so they take up more glucose.
  • Insulin activates enzymes that convert glucose to glycogen (glycogenesis) and to fat.
  • Cells respire more glucose.
  • Blood glucose falls → β cells secrete less insulin: negative feedback.
  • Say: "insulin makes cells take up glucose" — not: "insulin breaks down glucose".
Mission 3

The insulin receptor

  • The insulin receptor is a transmembrane protein with tyrosine kinase activity.
  • Insulin binds the receptor outside the cell.
  • Tyrosine residues on the receptor's cytoplasmic part are phosphorylated.
  • This starts a cascade of reactions inside the cell.
  • End point: vesicles containing glucose transporters move to and fuse with the plasma membrane.
  • More transporters → more glucose enters. No new protein is made, so it is fast.
Quick check
?Muscle cells increase their uptake of glucose within minutes of insulin binding to their receptors. Why is the response so fast?
Mission 3

Glucose too low: glucagon

  • α cells detect the fall and secrete glucagon.
  • Glucagon binds receptors on liver cells.
  • It activates enzymes that convert glycogen → glucose.
  • Glucose leaves the liver cells; blood glucose rises → α cells secrete less glucagon.
Quick check
?Which option correctly pairs a hormone with its effect on target cells?
Mission check

?In a rare inherited condition, liver cells cannot break down glycogen. Their glucagon receptors work normally. Which prediction for a person with this condition after an overnight fast is best supported?

Feedback needs every link: sensor, hormone, receptor and effector. Break the effector and the hormone shouts into silence.

Mission 4

Two types of diabetes

  • Diabetes mellitus: blood glucose stays too high because the insulin loop fails.
  • Type 1: the immune system destroys the β cells (autoimmune). Little or no insulin. Often starts in childhood; onset is usually quick.
  • Type 2: insulin is made, but target cells respond less to it: insulin resistance. Later, β cells may secrete less. Usually develops slowly in adults.
  • Type 2 risk factors: obesity (especially around the abdomen), physical inactivity, a high-sugar, energy-dense diet, increasing age, family history.
  • Type 1 is not caused by diet or lifestyle.
  • Say: "in type 2, target cells respond less to insulin" — not: "type 2 is a milder type 1".
Quick check
?A lean 12-year-old develops very high blood glucose within a few weeks; antibodies against pancreatic islet cells are found in the blood and blood insulin is very low. A 55-year-old with obesity has had slowly rising fasting blood glucose for years, with a high blood insulin concentration. Which interpretation is best supported?
Mission 4

Treatment and prevention

  • Type 1: insulin by injection or pump, matched to carbohydrate eaten and exercise. Regular blood glucose monitoring.
  • Type 2: first diet (less sugar and refined carbohydrate), exercise and weight loss: these improve insulin sensitivity.
  • Then drugs, e.g. metformin (the liver releases less glucose; cells respond better). Insulin is added later if needed.
  • Prevention of type 2: healthy body mass, regular activity, a balanced diet. Type 1 cannot be prevented this way.

Mission check

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • A ligand binds a receptor with a complementary binding site; only target cells with that receptor respond.
  • Signals act by direct contact, over short distances, or over long distances in the blood.
  • Hydrophilic ligands use transmembrane receptors; hydrophobic steroids use intracellular receptors. Binding starts a signal transduction pathway.

Mission 2

  • Ligand-gated channels change membrane potential in milliseconds; GPCRs act through G proteins, enzymes and second messengers such as cAMP.
  • Each step activates many molecules of the next, so a few ligand molecules cause a large response; multiply the step factors.
  • Insulin's receptor is a tyrosine kinase; negative feedback opposes change, positive feedback drives a process to completion.

Mission 3

  • High glucose → β cells → insulin → cells take up glucose and make glycogen; low glucose → α cells → glucagon → liver breaks down glycogen. Both are negative feedback.

Mission 4

  • Type 1: autoimmune loss of β cells, no insulin, treated with insulin. Type 2: target cells respond less to insulin, treated first by diet, exercise and weight loss.
  • World diabetes prevalence nearly doubled from 1980 to 2014 (WHO); a rise in a risk factor alongside is a correlation, not proof.
