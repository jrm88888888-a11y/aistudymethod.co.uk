Mission 1

Mission 1: Why can a popular new kind of café make big profits at first, then only just cover its costs later?

Make a guess: Why can a popular new kind of café make big profits at first, then only just cover its costs later?

Mission 1

Many firms, different products

  • Monopolistic competition: many firms, each selling a differentiated product.
  • Low barriers to entry and exit.
  • Each firm has a little price-setting power: its product is not identical to rivals'.
  • Examples: cafés, hairdressers, restaurants, takeaways, a new style of café such as bubble tea.
  • Firms are small, so each one acts independently: it does not plan around one rival's reaction.
Mission 1

Standing out: non-price competition

  • Product differentiation: real or perceived differences (quality, design, location, service, brand).
  • Non-price competition: competing on anything but price: branding, advertising, loyalty cards, opening hours, new menu items.
  • Aim 1: shift the firm's demand curve right (more customers at each price).
  • Aim 2: make demand less price elastic (customers less willing to switch).
  • Say: "the firm's demand curve slopes downward because its product is differentiated" — not: "because it has few rivals".
Quick check
?A hairdresser in a busy town centre raises its price slightly. It loses some customers but not all of them. Which best explains why?
Mission 1

The short run

  • Profit is maximised where MR = MC. Price is read up to the AR curve.
  • AR > AC: abnormal profit (also called supernormal profit). AR < AC: losses. AR = AC: normal profit.
  • The demand curve is more price elastic than a monopolist's: there are many close substitutes.
  • In the short run, this looks just like the monopoly diagram. The difference is what happens next.
Mission 1

Worked example: short-run profit

  • Illustrative data. A new bubble tea shop.
  • Demand: AR = 30 − 0.2Q, so MR = 30 − 0.4Q.
  • Costs: MC = 0.2Q. AC = 360/Q + 0.1Q.
  • MR = MC: 30 − 0.4Q = 0.2Q, so Q = 50.
  • Price from AR: 30 − 0.2 × 50 = 20.
  • AC at Q = 50: 360/50 + 0.1 × 50 = 7.2 + 5 = 12.2.
  • Abnormal profit = (AR − AC) × Q = (20 − 12.2) × 50 = 390.
Mission 1

From short run to long run

  • Profit above normal is a signal. Barriers are low, so new firms enter.
  • Each firm loses some customers to the newcomers: its demand curve shifts left.
  • More close substitutes: demand becomes more price elastic.
  • Entry stops when AR just touches (is tangent to) AC: normal profit.
  • Losses work the other way: firms leave, and each remaining firm's demand shifts right until AR touches AC again.
  • Say: "each firm's demand curve shifts left as firms enter" — not: "the market demand curve falls".
Mission 1Graph lab

Illustrative data. Start: 4 shops; each earns a profit of 390.

  • Takeaway: entry continues until profit is normal, at the point where AR touches AC to the left of the lowest point of AC.
Mission 1

Worked example: the long run

  • Illustrative data. After entry, each shop's demand: AR = 18 − 0.125Q, so MR = 18 − 0.25Q.
  • Costs are unchanged: MC = 0.2Q; AC = 360/Q + 0.1Q.
  • MR = MC: 18 − 0.25Q = 0.2Q, so Q = 40.
  • Price: 18 − 0.125 × 40 = 13.
  • AC at Q = 40: 360/40 + 0.1 × 40 = 9 + 4 = 13.
  • P = AC: profit per unit 0. Normal profit.
  • PED at the chosen price: SR −2; LR −2.6. Demand is more elastic after entry.
Mission 1

Excess capacity

  • The long-run tangency is on the falling part of AC.
  • So output is below the output at the lowest AC: excess capacity.
  • In the previous screen: output 40; lowest AC (12) at 60. Excess capacity = 60 − 40 = 20 units.
  • Not productively efficient: P = AC = 13 > lowest AC = 12.
  • Not allocatively efficient: P = 13 > MC = 8. Buyers value one more unit more than it costs to make.
  • IB: allocative inefficiency, because P > MC.
  • Say: "excess capacity: output is below the level that minimises AC" — not: "the firm has too many workers".

  • The key gain over perfect competition: variety. Buyers can choose the café, style or location they like best.
Mission 1

Advertising

  • Advertising aims to shift the firm's demand right and make it less price elastic (brand loyalty).
  • It also raises costs: AC shifts up.
  • Short run: if extra sales outweigh the extra cost, profit rises.
  • Long run: rivals copy the idea, and new firms enter. Profit returns to normal.
  • Informative advertising (prices, opening hours, what is new) helps buyers choose.
  • Persuasive advertising (image, brand) may add to costs and prices without making the product better.
Quick check
?A nail salon launches a successful advertising campaign that makes its customers more loyal. In the short run, what happens to the salon's demand curve?
Mission 1Evaluate

Is excess capacity a waste?

  • It depends on how much buyers value variety: a slightly higher price than perfect competition may be the cost of choice.
  • It depends on how elastic demand is: with many close substitutes, demand is very elastic, the tangency is near the lowest AC, and the excess capacity is small.
  • It depends on the type of advertising: informative or persuasive.
  • It depends on dynamic efficiency: normal profit leaves little to fund research, but rivalry pushes firms to try new products.
  • It depends on how realistic the model is: some firms build brands, or win locations, that rivals cannot easily copy.
Mission check

?Illustrative case: a town has a few gyms making large profits. Opening a gym is easy, and each gym differs a little in equipment and classes. New gyms open until the market settles. Which describes a typical gym in the new long-run equilibrium?

Low barriers mean profit above normal attracts entry until each firm's AR just touches AC, on the falling part of AC.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why can a giant firm make things more cheaply than a small one, and when does size start to hurt?

Make a guess: Why can a giant firm make things more cheaply than a small one, and when does size start to hurt?

Mission 2

Cost per unit as a firm grows

  • Economies of scale: long-run average cost falls as the scale of output rises.
  • Diseconomies of scale: long-run average cost rises as the scale of output rises.
  • They are long run: the firm grows by changing all its inputs, including plant size.
  • A firm's own growth moves it along its LRAC curve.
  • Say: "economies of scale lower average cost" — not: "lower costs". Total cost still rises with output.
Mission 2

Internal economies of scale

  • Internal economies come from the firm's own growth.
TypeWhy average cost falls
TechnicalBigger, specialised machines; mass production; larger containers cost less per unit of capacity
ManagerialSpecialist managers (finance, marketing, engineering) each run a bigger output
FinancialLenders see big firms as less risky: lower interest rates, more ways to raise money
PurchasingBulk buying earns discounts on inputs
MarketingOne advert or brand spread over many more sales
Risk-bearingMany products and markets: a fall in one is offset by others
  • Many are about indivisibilities: some inputs (a machine, an advert, a specialist) cannot be bought in small slices.
Mission 2

Worked example: why bigger ships carry more cheaply

  • Think of a cargo hold as a cube (simplified model).
  • Side 1 → 2: surface area (steel to build) 6 → 24, ×4. Volume (cargo it holds) 1 → 8, ×8.
  • Steel per unit of cargo: 6 ÷ 1 = 6 → 24 ÷ 8 = 3. It halves.
  • This is the law of increased dimensions, a technical economy of scale.
  • A bigger ship also needs only a slightly larger crew and engine per container carried.
  • Real data: MSC Irina, delivered in 2023, carries 24,346 twenty-foot containers.
Quick check
?Which is an example of a financial economy of scale?
Mission 2

Natural monopoly

  • Some industries have economies of scale over the whole range of market demand: average cost is still falling where it meets demand.
  • Then one firm can supply the whole market at a lower average cost than two or more firms could.
  • Examples: water pipes, electricity grids, rail track. Building two networks to every home would duplicate a huge fixed cost.
  • Illustrative data. A grid costs 90 million a year to run, plus 30 per household supplied. The market is 3 million households.
  • One firm: 90 million ÷ 3 million + 30 = 60 per household.
  • Three firms, 1 million households each: 90 million ÷ 1 million + 30 = 120 per household.
Quick check
?A water network costs 60 million a year to maintain, plus 40 per household supplied. There are 2 million households (illustrative data). What is the average cost per household with one network, and with two rival networks that each supply half the households?
Mission 2Evaluate

Is bigger better for consumers?

It depends on …

  • Whether cost savings are passed on: with strong competition, lower average cost means lower prices; a dominant firm may keep the savings as profit.
  • Diseconomies: past a point, more size raises average cost.
  • Barriers to entry: economies of scale protect established firms, which can weaken the pressure to keep costs and prices down.
  • Innovation: abnormal profits of large firms can fund research and development, bringing new products and lower costs later.
  • Natural monopoly: one firm has the lowest average cost, but it may then charge a high price. Governments often regulate or own such firms.
  • Judge: size can lower costs, but whether consumers gain depends on competition and how the firm is run.
Mission check

?A food firm grows over several years (illustrative data). At 10,000 meals a week its total cost is 200,000. At 50,000 meals it is 600,000. At 120,000 meals it is 1,800,000. What do these figures show?

Growing can lower cost per unit, but only up to a point; judge it by average cost, not total cost.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Can a new idea that wipes out whole industries still leave an economy better off?

Make a guess: A new technology wipes out an old industry. What happens to the economy as a whole?

Mission 3

Does market power help innovation?

  • New products can wipe out older products and the firms that made them. Those firms' workers and owners lose, but consumers gain better or cheaper goods, so the economy as a whole can still gain.
  • For (Schumpeter): firms with market power earn supernormal (abnormal) profit, which can finance R&D. Large firms can also bear the risk of failed projects.
  • Patents give an innovator a temporary monopoly (usually up to 20 years), so it can earn the profit that rewards its R&D.
  • Against: a monopolist that already earns high profit may feel little pressure to innovate. In competitive markets, firms must innovate or lose customers.
Quick check
?Which statement best supports the view that large firms with market power can benefit consumers through innovation?
Mission check

?A government proposes cutting patent protection on new medicines from 20 years to 10, so that cheaper copies can be sold sooner. Which is the strongest economic argument against the change?

The case for patents trades higher prices today for more innovation tomorrow; the debate is about the balance.

Mission 4

Mission 4: When should a government stop big firms from merging, fixing prices or growing too powerful?

Make a guess: When should a government stop big firms from merging, fixing prices or growing too powerful?

Mission 4

Why have competition policy?

  • Market power can mean higher prices, lower output, less choice and a deadweight loss.
  • Firms with little competition may also let costs drift up (X-inefficiency) and innovate less.
  • Competition policy: laws and authorities that promote competition and stop firms abusing market power.
Mission 4

Three targets

1. Anti-competitive agreements (restrictive practices): cartels, price fixing, sharing out markets, bid rigging.

2. Abuse of a dominant position: e.g. predatory pricing (pricing below cost to drive out a rival), refusing to supply rivals, forcing buyers to take one product to get another.

3. Mergers that would substantially lessen competition.

  • Being large is not illegal. Abusing that position is.
  • The key question: would buyers face higher prices, lower quality or less choice?
  • A firm facing cheap imports or easy entry by rivals has little power to raise prices, even with a large share.
Mission 4

Legislation, regulation and fines

  • Legislation: most countries ban cartels and abuse of a dominant position, and review large mergers.
  • A competition authority (e.g. the European Commission in the EU) investigates and enforces.
  • Regulation: regulators can set price caps and quality standards in industries with few firms.
  • Fines: in the EU, up to 10% of a firm's worldwide turnover.
    • In 2016 the European Commission fined truck makers €2.93 billion for a price-fixing cartel.
    • In 2017 it fined Google €2.42 billion for abusing its dominance in online search.
  • Fines punish, and aim to deter other firms.
Quick check
?Which of these is most likely to break competition law?
Mission 4

Worked example: a merger

  • Illustrative data. Market shares (%): A 30, B 20, C 15, D 10, others 25 (each under 5).
  • Three-firm concentration ratio (CR3) before: 30 + 20 + 15 = 65%.
  • B and C propose to merge: share 35%.
  • CR3 after: 35 + 30 + 10 = 75%.
  • Merger types: horizontal (same stage, rivals: B + C), vertical (supplier and buyer), conglomerate (unrelated markets).
  • Horizontal mergers get the closest scrutiny: they remove a rival directly.
  • The authority can clear the merger, clear it with conditions (e.g. sell some stores), or block it.
  • Against the loss of a rival, it weighs any cost savings passed on to buyers.
Mission 4Graph lab

What does a cartel cost buyers?

Illustrative data. Market demand: P = 50 − Q. MC = AC = 10. A cartel acts like a monopoly: P = 30, Q = 20. Competition would give P = 10, Q = 40.

  • Takeaway: ending a cartel returns its profit to buyers and removes the deadweight loss.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. Market demand is P = 80 − 2Q and MC = AC = 20. A cartel sets Q = 15 and P = 50, earning a profit of 450. Under competition, P = 20 and Q = 30. If the cartel is broken up, by how much does consumer surplus rise?
Mission 4Evaluate

How well does competition policy work?

  • It depends on information: proving a cartel, or telling predation from fierce competition, is hard and slow.
  • It depends on the size of the market: blocking a merger may stop a firm reaching the scale it needs to compete with global rivals.
  • It depends on dynamic efficiency: large firms' supernormal profit may fund R&D.
  • It depends on fines: too small to deter, or passed on to buyers?
  • It depends on the authority: regulatory capture (it comes to side with the firms it oversees) and the cost of investigations.
Mission check

?Illustrative case: four firms supply cement in a country, with market shares of 35%, 25%, 20% and 20%. The two smallest firms propose to merge, saying it will cut their costs. Which finding gives the strongest economic case for the competition authority to allow the merger?

The test is not size or profit but whether buyers would lose: higher prices, less choice or lower quality.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Should a country's trains, water pipes and power lines be run by the state or by private companies?

Make a guess: Should a country's trains, water pipes and power lines be run by the state or by private companies?

Mission 5

Four tools, four meanings

  • Nationalisation: the state takes a firm or industry into public ownership.
  • Privatisation: transfer of a firm or activity from the public sector to the private sector.
  • Regulation: rules set by government or an appointed regulator, e.g. on prices, quality, safety or entry.
  • Deregulation: removing rules that restrict entry, prices or how firms operate.
  • Ownership (who owns it) and market structure (how many rivals) are separate questions.
  • Say: "privatisation changes the owner" — not: "privatisation creates competition".
Mission 5

Forms of privatisation

  • Sale of a state-owned firm: shares sold to the public or a buyer (denationalisation). E.g. British Telecom (1984).
  • Contracting out (competitive tendering): the state still pays for a service but invites private firms to bid to provide it, e.g. bin collection.
  • Liberalisation: opening a state monopoly to private rivals (a form of deregulation).
  • Privatisation and deregulation are market-based supply-side policies: they aim to raise competition and efficiency.
Mission 5

The case for privatisation

  • Profit motive: private owners gain from cutting costs, so less X-inefficiency (waste when a firm faces little pressure to keep costs down).
  • Shareholder pressure: a poorly run firm's share price falls, and it risks a takeover.
  • Competition, where the industry allows rivals: lower prices, more choice.
  • Access to private capital for investment, without adding to government borrowing.
  • One-off revenue for the government from the sale.
  • Less political interference in pricing and investment decisions.
  • Example: the UK sold telecoms (1984), gas (1986), water in England and Wales (1989), electricity (1990–91) and rail (1994–97).
Mission 5

The case against, and renationalisation

  • A natural monopoly (one network serves the whole market most cheaply, e.g. a power grid) sold whole stays a monopoly: a private monopoly still needs regulation.
  • Short-termism: dividends may come before long-term investment.
  • Social objectives may be dropped, e.g. loss-making rural routes.
  • Sale price: if shares are sold too cheaply, taxpayers lose; the one-off revenue replaces a flow of future profits.
  • Fragmentation: splitting a network among many firms can raise coordination costs.
  • Renationalisation arguments: run for the public interest, cheaper public borrowing, no dividends to pay out, one integrated network.
  • Example: Railtrack (privatised rail infrastructure) was replaced in 2002 by Network Rail, now a public body. A 2024 law lets passenger train services in Great Britain return to public ownership as contracts end.
Quick check
?A city council stops using its own staff to clean public parks. It invites private firms to bid, and pays the winning firm to do the work for five years. The parks stay free to use. Which policy is this?
Mission 5

Nationalisation: evaluate

  • For:
    • Can price nearer MC and serve social aims (e.g. universal service), not just profit.
    • Long-term investment in networks; account for externalities.
    • Rescue of a failing firm that the economy relies on, e.g. the bank Northern Rock (UK, 2008).
    • Government ownership is one response to the abuse of market power.
  • Against:
    • Weak profit incentive: X-inefficiency; losses may be covered by taxpayers (a "soft budget").
    • Political interference, e.g. prices held down before elections.
    • Investment competes with schools and hospitals for public money.
    • Cost of compensating the previous owners.
Mission 5

Deregulation, licences and prohibitions

  • A licence limits who may operate (e.g. taxis, mobile spectrum). A prohibition bans an activity outright.
  • Deregulation removes barriers, so new firms can enter: supply rises, prices fall, choice widens.
  • Example: the US Airline Deregulation Act (1978) ended government control of fares and routes.
  • Example: UK local buses outside London were deregulated in 1986: any licensed operator could run a route.
  • Risks: firms "cherry-pick" profitable routes; safety and quality may suffer; financial deregulation can raise risk-taking.
  • Say: "deregulation lowers barriers to entry" — not: "deregulation means no rules at all".

  • Takeaway: removing an entry limit shifts the market to where price = MC. The gap was a reward for holding a licence.
Mission 5

What regulators do

  • Where competition is weak, a regulator acts for consumers in its place.
  • Role: set price caps, quality and service standards; grant licences; give rivals fair access to networks; fine firms that break the rules.
  • UK examples: Ofwat (water), Ofgem (energy), Ofcom (communications), the Office of Rail and Road.
  • Most countries have similar bodies, e.g. the US Federal Energy Regulatory Commission (energy networks).
  • Legislation and regulation are responses to the abuse of market power.
Mission 5

When regulators fail

  • Asymmetric information: the firm knows its costs better than the regulator. It may overstate costs to win a looser price cap.
  • Regulatory capture: a regulator comes to act in the interests of the firms it regulates, not consumers.
  • Why capture happens:
    • The regulator relies on the industry for information and expert staff.
    • A revolving door: staff move between regulator and industry.
    • A few firms gain a lot and lobby hard; millions of consumers each lose a little.
  • Example: after the 2010 Deepwater Horizon oil spill, the US Minerals Management Service, criticised as too close to the industry it oversaw, was broken up (2010–11).
  • Say: "regulatory capture" — not: "the regulator was bribed". Capture can happen without any law being broken.
Quick check
?Illustrative case: a country's rail regulator hires most of its senior staff from the train companies. Over ten years it approves every fare rise the companies ask for, using cost figures that only the companies collect. Which problem does this best show?
Mission 5

Supply side: shifting LRAS

  • Privatisation and deregulation aim to raise efficiency, competition and incentives to innovate.
  • If they work, the economy's productive capacity rises: LRAS shifts right (LRAS1 → LRAS2).
  • With AD unchanged: real GDP rises and the price level falls.
  • Limits: effects take time; a private monopoly may not become more efficient; gains may be small for the whole economy.
Mission 5Evaluate

Does it work?

  • It depends on competition: privatising a firm that faces rivals differs from selling a natural monopoly.
  • It depends on regulation: information, capture and the regulator's powers.
  • It depends on time: cost savings may come fast; under-investment may show only years later.
  • It depends on objectives: efficiency and low prices, or universal service and equity?
  • Evidence on ownership is mixed: many studies find that competition and regulation matter at least as much as who owns the firm.
Mission check

?Illustrative case: a government sells its state-owned national electricity grid as a single company. There is still only one grid, and the government sets up no regulator. What is the most likely result for the price of using the grid?

Privatising a monopoly without competition or regulation swaps a public monopoly for a private one.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Many firms, differentiated products, low barriers: each firm has a downward-sloping, fairly elastic demand curve.
  • Short run: MR = MC; profit above normal or a loss is possible. Long run: entry or exit leaves normal profit (AR tangent to AC).
  • Long run: excess capacity and P > MC, but buyers get variety.

Mission 2

  • Economies of scale: long-run average cost falls as output grows; diseconomies: it rises.
  • Internal economies (technical, managerial, financial, purchasing, marketing, risk-bearing) come from the firm's own growth.

Mission 3

  • Supernormal profit from market power can finance R&D; competition can also force innovation.

Mission 4

  • Competition policy targets anti-competitive agreements, abuse of dominance and mergers that would substantially lessen competition.
  • Responses to abuse of market power: legislation, regulation and fines.

Mission 5

  • Nationalisation, privatisation, regulation and deregulation answer two different questions: who owns the firm, and what rules it faces.
  • Privatisation and deregulation can cut costs and prices where rivals can enter; natural monopolies still need regulation.
  • Regulators face asymmetric information and the risk of regulatory capture.
  • As supply-side policies, they aim to shift LRAS to the right.
