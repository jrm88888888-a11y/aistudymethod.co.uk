Mission 1

Twelve years of wrongs, settled in one afternoon. How?

Make a guess: Twelve years of wrongs, settled in one afternoon. How?

The Tempest · structure and the unitiesScreen 1

The play in five acts

The Tempest was written c.1610–11; its first recorded performance was at court, at Whitehall, on 1 November 1611. It is the first play in the First Folio (1623), and probably the last Shakespeare wrote alone.

ActWhat happens
1Prospero's magic storm wrecks his enemies' ship near his island. He tells his daughter Miranda how his brother Antonio, helped by Alonso, King of Naples, took his dukedom of Milan twelve years ago. Alonso's son Ferdinand meets Miranda: love at first sight.
2Alonso thinks Ferdinand drowned. Antonio urges Sebastian to kill his brother Alonso for the crown; Ariel wakes Gonzalo in time. Caliban takes the drunken Stephano as his new god.
3Ferdinand and Miranda promise to marry. Caliban, Stephano and Trinculo plot to kill Prospero. Ariel, as a harpy, accuses the "three men of sin".
4Prospero blesses the engagement with a masque of spirits, then breaks it off to deal with Caliban's plot.
5Prospero gives up his magic, forgives his enemies, regains his dukedom and frees Ariel. In the Epilogue he asks the audience to set him free.

The Tempest · structure and the unitiesScreen 5

The three unities

  • Renaissance critics, building on Aristotle's Poetics, praised plays that kept three unities:
  • Time: the action fits within one day. Here it takes about four hours, the length of the story roughly matching a performance.
  • Place: one setting. After the shipwreck (Act 1 Scene 1), everything happens on the island.
  • Action: one main plot. Every strand serves what Prospero calls his "project" (Act 5 Scene 1).
  • Shakespeare rarely kept the unities; The Comedy of Errors is the other play that comes close.
The Tempest · structure and the unitiesScreen 6

Twelve years told, not shown

  • The past arrives through exposition: in Act 1 Scene 2 Prospero tells Miranda, and us, how they were cast out of Milan, "In the dark backward and abysm of time".
  • The new plots mirror the old crime: Antonio, who stole Milan, urges Sebastian to murder Alonso (Act 2 Scene 1); Caliban urges Stephano to kill Prospero (Act 3 Scene 2).
  • Three groups move round the island: the court, the lovers, the comic plotters. Prospero, through Ariel, watches and steers each one.

Mission 2

Prospero was robbed of a dukedom. Victim or tyrant?

Make a guess: Prospero was robbed of a dukedom. Victim or tyrant?

The Tempest · Prospero and powerScreen 1

How Prospero lost Milan

  • Twelve years ago Prospero, Duke of Milan, left ruling to his brother Antonio: "The government I cast upon my brother," (Act 1 Scene 2).
  • Antonio, helped by Alonso, King of Naples, seized Milan and set Prospero and little Miranda adrift. Gonzalo, "out of his charity", gave them food, water and books.
  • Usurpation (seizing a ruler's place) threatened all order. In 1611 King James I watched this story of a stolen dukedom.

Mission 3

'This island's mine,' says Caliban. Is he right?

Make a guess: 'This island's mine,' says Caliban. Is he right?

The Tempest · Caliban, Ariel and colonisationScreen 1

Two servants of Prospero

  • Ariel is a spirit. The witch Sycorax trapped him in a pine; Prospero's magic freed him. He serves in return for the "liberty" Prospero has promised.
  • Caliban is Sycorax's son. She was banished from "Argier" (Algiers) and left on the island, where Caliban was born. The cast list calls him "a savage and deformed slave".
  • Ariel obeys to win freedom. Caliban resists, curses, and is punished.

The Tempest · Caliban, Ariel and colonisationScreen 4

The other side of the story

  • Prospero replies that he treated Caliban "with human care" until Caliban tried to "violate / The honour of my child": he tried to rape Miranda. Caliban does not deny it.
  • Then comes the "Abhorred slave" speech, which says Caliban was taught to speak. In the First Folio and most school editions Miranda speaks it; some editors (and) give it to Prospero.
  • Caliban answers: "You taught me language, and my profit on ’t / Is, I know how to curse."
The Tempest · Caliban, Ariel and colonisationScreen 5

Voyages, colonies and "cannibals"

  • In 1609 the Sea Venture, sailing to the young English colony at Jamestown, Virginia, was wrecked on Bermuda; everyone survived, though England thought them lost. William Strachey's account (1610, printed 1625) probably reached Shakespeare in manuscript. Ariel mentions "the still-vex’d Bermoothes".
  • Montaigne's essay "Of Cannibals" (English, 1603) asks whether Europeans who call others barbarous are any less so. Gonzalo's ideal island echoes it (Act 2 Scene 1).
  • Trinculo jokes that the English "will lay out ten to see a dead Indian" (Act 2 Scene 2).
The Tempest · Caliban, Ariel and colonisationScreen 6

Readings that changed over time

  • Early critics mostly read the play as about art and magic.
  • From about 1950 (Octave Mannoni), critics read Prospero and Caliban as coloniser and colonised. George Lamming (The Pleasures of Exile, 1960) and Aimé Césaire (Une Tempête, 1969) took Caliban as a voice of the colonised.
  • Others warn: Prospero landed by chance, not as a settler, on an island between Tunis and Naples.
  • Test a colonial reading against the text.

Caliban's claim has real force: the island was his mother's, he shared it, and it was taken. But the play also gives Prospero a serious charge against him and sets the island in the Mediterranean. Colonial readings, strong since the 1950s, are powerful interpretations to test against the text.

Mission 4

Love at first sight, or a match Prospero arranged?

Make a guess: Love at first sight, or a match Prospero arranged?

The Tempest · Miranda and FerdinandScreen 1

First sight, and a father watching

  • Ariel's music leads Ferdinand, who thinks his father drowned, to Miranda (Act 1 Scene 2). She has seen only two men before: Prospero and Caliban.
  • Prospero's asides show he is pleased, and steering: "It goes on, I see, / As my soul prompts it."
  • So he makes it hard, "lest too light winning / Make the prize light": he calls Ferdinand a spy and sets him to work.
  • Later Ferdinand praises her: "Admir’d Miranda! / Indeed, the top of admiration;" (Act 3 Scene 1).

The Tempest · Miranda and FerdinandScreen 4

Tests, chastity and politics

  • Ferdinand must carry logs, the task Caliban does: "Am I this patient log-man." (Act 3 Scene 1).
  • Prospero warns him not to "break her virgin knot" before the wedding (Act 4 Scene 1): a princess's chastity was tied to her honour and her heirs.
  • The match unites Milan and Naples. Gonzalo: "Was Milan thrust from Milan, that his issue / Should become Kings of Naples?" (Act 5 Scene 1).
  • In 1613 the play was performed during the celebrations for Princess Elizabeth's royal wedding.

Mission 5

Storms, spirits, a vanishing feast: what is the magic for?

Make a guess: Storms, spirits, a vanishing feast: what is the magic for?

The Tempest · magic, illusion and theatreScreen 1

Prospero's "art"

  • Prospero calls his magic his art: it comes from his books, his staff and his robe ("Lie there my art," he says, laying the robe aside).
  • Ariel and lesser spirits do the work: the storm, the music, the banquet, the harpy, the masque, the hounds.
  • Sycorax, the "damn’d witch", used cruelty; Prospero's art is learned and controlled.
  • Yet James I wrote against witchcraft (Daemonologie, 1597), and the 1604 Witchcraft Act made conjuring evil spirits punishable by death. Prospero conjures spirits too.

The Tempest · magic, illusion and theatreScreen 4

Magic as theatre

  • Prospero praises Ariel like a director: "Bravely the figure of this Harpy hast thou / Perform’d, my Ariel;" (Act 3 Scene 3).
  • The stage direction says "with a quaint device, the banquet vanishes": a piece of stage machinery.
  • The play was probably written with the Blackfriars in mind, the King's Men's indoor theatre, where music and effects were easier to stage.
  • So Prospero's spells are also illusions: scenes staged to make people see, fear and feel.

The Tempest · magic, illusion and theatreScreen 7

Ways to read the magic

  • Moral tool: the illusions lead his enemies to "heart-sorrow" and guide the lovers. Then he gives the magic up: "this rough magic / I here abjure" (Act 5 Scene 1).
  • Dangerous power: he boasts that "graves at my command / Have wak’d their sleepers", raising the dead: close to the use of the dead that the 1604 Act made a capital crime.
  • Playwright figure: some readers see Prospero's farewell to magic as Shakespeare's farewell to the stage. That is a reading: he went on to co-write plays.

Prospero's magic works like theatre. He stages a storm, a vanishing banquet, a harpy and a masque to gather his enemies, make them face what they did, and bless his daughter's match. Once that work is done, he gives the magic up.

Mission 6

Prospero has his enemies trapped. Why does he forgive them?

Make a guess: Prospero has his enemies trapped. Why does he forgive them?

The Tempest · forgiveness and freedomScreen 1

A revenge plot that turns

  • By the end of Act 4, Prospero has his enemies where he wants them: "At this hour / Lies at my mercy all mine enemies."
  • In a revenge tragedy (such as Hamlet, c.1600) the wronged man usually takes bloody vengeance and dies.
  • The Tempest is grouped with Shakespeare's late plays (Pericles, The Winter's Tale, Cymbeline), often called romances, which end in reunion and reconciliation.
  • Ariel reports that the prisoners' grief would make Prospero's feelings "tender", if he saw them.

Mission 7

Prose, verse, song, rhyme: why does the play keep changing voice?

Make a guess: Prose, verse, song, rhyme: why does the play keep changing voice?

The Tempest · language, the masque and the EpilogueScreen 1

Who speaks how

  • Blank verse (unrhymed iambic pentameter: ten-syllable lines, five stresses) for the nobles and serious moments.
  • Prose for comedy and the everyday: Stephano and Trinculo, and the sailors in the opening storm.
  • Caliban, the "monster", mostly speaks verse, even when Stephano and Trinculo answer in prose (Act 2 Scene 2, Act 3 Scene 2).
  • Songs for Ariel ("Full fathom five"); rhyming couplets for the masque goddesses and the Epilogue.

The Tempest · language, the masque and the EpilogueScreen 5

The masque (Act 4)

  • A masque was a court entertainment of music, dance and costume, with gods and goddesses; Ben Jonson was its leading writer.
  • Prospero's spirits play Iris, Juno and Ceres, blessing the couple in rhyming couplets and song: "Honour, riches, marriage-blessing,".
  • Venus and Cupid are kept away: the masque celebrates chaste, fertile marriage.
  • It breaks off when Prospero remembers "that foul conspiracy" of Caliban: harmony interrupted by the threat it shut out.

The Epilogue

The Tempest · language, the masque and the EpilogueScreen 7

Stepping out of the play

  • The Epilogue is in short four-stress lines (tetrameter) in rhyming couplets, unlike the play's blank verse.
  • Prospero speaks as an actor without magic: "Now my charms are all o’erthrown,".
  • Applause ("your good hands") and kind words ("Gentle breath") will free him. Power passes to the audience.
  • The last couplet asks for pardon in return for pardon, like the Lord's Prayer: the play's forgiveness theme, turned on us.
  • Some readers hear Shakespeare's own farewell: a reading, not a fact.

Mission checkScreen 9

?Prose, verse, song, rhyme: why does the play keep changing voice?

The play's voices sort its worlds: blank verse for the court, prose for the clowns, songs for Ariel, couplets for the masque and the Epilogue. Shakespeare then plays against the pattern: Caliban, called a monster, speaks lovely verse, and Prospero ends in a humble rhyming plea.

Recap

Big ideas

  • The Tempest (c.1610–11; First Folio 1623) keeps close to the unities: one island, about four hours, one controlling plan.
  • Exposition in Act 1 Scene 2 supplies twelve years of backstory; later plots mirror the original usurpation.
  • Three strands (court, lovers, comic plotters) run in parallel, each steered by Prospero through Ariel.
  • Prospero lost Milan partly through his own neglect: he "cast" the government on Antonio while he studied (Act 1 Scene 2).
  • On the island he controls everyone: Ariel through promises and threats, Caliban through pain, Ferdinand through charms and labour, Miranda through sleep and command.
  • Readers divide: a wise father-figure guiding events, or a controlling ruler who echoes the usurpers. The strongest answers weigh both.
  • Caliban claims the island "by Sycorax my mother"; he shared its secrets and was then confined. Ariel serves for promised freedom.
  • Prospero charges Caliban with trying to rape Miranda; the "Abhorred slave" speech (Miranda's in the Folio) says he was taught language.
  • The Sea Venture wreck (1609), Strachey's account and Montaigne's "Of Cannibals" link the play to early colonial voyages; colonial readings grew from about 1950.
  • Prospero brings the couple together and tests Ferdinand with labour; his asides show he approves.
  • Miranda is both steered and bold: she disobeys her father and proposes herself (Act 3 Scene 1).
  • The marriage is political as well as romantic: it unites Milan and Naples. The 1613 court performance fell during a royal wedding's celebrations.
  • Prospero's "art" (books, staff, robe) works through Ariel and the spirits; Sycorax's witchcraft is its dark mirror.
  • His spells are illusions, staged like theatre: the harpy and the masque are performances with a purpose.
  • "Our revels now are ended" links the vanished masque to the brevity of life, and many readers hear the Globe in "the great globe itself".
  • Some readers find his power troubling (spirits, the dead raised); he gives it up in Act 5.
  • The play sets up a revenge plot ("Lies at my mercy all mine enemies"), then turns to forgiveness, like Shakespeare's other late plays.
  • Prospero's choice, "the rarer action is / In virtue than in vengeance", is prompted by Ariel and depends on repentance.
  • Alonso repents; Antonio is forgiven but silent; Caliban's future is open. Ariel is freed; Prospero asks to be freed.
  • Blank verse for nobles and serious moments, prose for comedy, songs for Ariel, couplets for the masque and the Epilogue.
  • Caliban's "isle is full of noises" speech gives the "monster" delicate verse; it complicates how others label him.
  • The masque blesses a chaste marriage and is broken off by Caliban's plot; the Epilogue hands power to the audience and asks for pardon.

  • As you from crimes would pardon’d be, / Let your indulgence set me free. (Epilogue (Prospero)) Pardon turned on the audience.
