Mission 1

Mission 1: Even the richest person on Earth cannot have everything. Why not?

Make a guess: Even the richest person on Earth cannot have everything. Why not?

Mission 1

The basic economic problem

  • Scarcity: resources are limited, but wants are unlimited.
  • Needs: things required to survive (food, water, shelter). Wants: things we desire beyond them.
  • Even the richest person has only 24 hours a day, and every resource they use is lost to other uses.
  • So every person, firm and government must choose.
  • Choosing one thing means giving up another: a trade-off.
  • Say: "scarcity" (permanent, everywhere) — not: "shortage" (a temporary lack of one good at its current price).
Quick check
?Which statement best describes scarcity as economists use the term?
Mission 1

Is anything not scarce?

  • Economic good: uses scarce resources, so it has an opportunity cost. Almost everything.
  • Free good: needs no scarce resources; more is available than people want, even at a zero price. Zero opportunity cost.
  • Examples: air on a remote mountain, seawater, sunlight.
  • Free of charge is not the same as a free good. A state school uses teachers, buildings and tax revenue: an economic good given at a zero price.
  • Free goods can become scarce: clean air in a polluted city.
  • Say: "a free good has zero opportunity cost" — not: "a free good is anything with a zero price".
Quick check
?Which of the following is most likely to be a free good?
Mission 1

Scarcity and sustainability

  • Renewable resources can regenerate: fish, forests, wind, solar energy.
  • Non-renewable resources are a fixed stock that is used up: oil, coal, natural gas, metal ores.
  • Renewable is not unlimited. Used faster than it regenerates, a renewable resource runs down.
  • Sustainability: meeting the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs.
  • Using more today can raise the opportunity cost for the future.
  • Case: Canada banned fishing for northern cod off Newfoundland on 2 July 1992, after the stock collapsed.
  • Nearly 30,000 people in Newfoundland and Labrador lost their jobs. A commercial cod fishery returned only in 2024.
Mission 1

Opportunity cost

  • Opportunity cost: the value of the next best alternative forgone when a choice is made.
  • Only the next best alternative counts, not every alternative added up.
  • Individual: an evening revising means giving up a paid shift.
  • Firm: money spent on a new machine cannot also pay for an advert campaign.
  • Government: a budget spent on a new hospital cannot also build a new school.
  • Society: resources used for one good cannot make another.
  • Free to you does not mean no opportunity cost: a free concert ticket still costs your evening; free school meals still use food, cooks and tax revenue.
  • Say: "the next best alternative forgone" — not: "the price paid" or "all the alternatives".
Mission 1

Worked example: the real cost of a summer course

  • Illustrative data. Amounts in any one currency.
  • Maya can take a summer course (fee 1,500) or work a summer job (pay 4,000). The job is her next best alternative.
  • Choosing the course, she gives up the job's pay and whatever the 1,500 fee could have bought.
  • Money measure of the opportunity cost: 1,500 + 4,000 = 5,500.
  • The fee alone (1,500) understates the cost: it ignores the pay she gives up.

Mission 1

Three questions every economy must answer

  • What to produce, and how much of each? (hospitals or roads, food or computers)
  • How to produce? (which mix of workers, machines and land)
  • For whom to produce? (who gets the output: by income, need or another rule)
  • Answering them is resource allocation.
  • In markets, prices act as signals and incentives: a rising price tells firms to make more and buyers to use less.
  • Governments also allocate: by planning, taxes and laws. Mission 4 compares market, planned and mixed economies.
Quick check
?A government decides that hospital treatment will be given according to medical need, not ability to pay. Which basic economic question is this decision mainly answering?
Mission 1Evaluate

How useful is opportunity cost in practice?

It depends on:

  • Information: the next best alternative may be unknown or uncertain when the choice is made.
  • Valuation: many alternatives have no price (time, health, a clean environment), so they are hard to compare.
  • Time: some costs fall on future generations, as when a resource is over-used.
  • Whose cost: a firm's choice may impose costs on others (pollution) that it does not count.
  • Judgement: the idea always applies, but measuring it can be hard.
Mission check

Scarce resources have other uses, so even a "free" service costs society the next best thing those resources could have made.

Mission 2

Mission 2: When economists disagree, is it about the evidence or about values?

Make a guess: Two economists study the same data and still disagree about what the government should do. Why?

Mission 2

A science of people

  • Economics is a social science: it studies how people choose to use scarce resources.
  • Like a natural science, it builds theories and tests them against evidence.
  • Unlike chemistry or physics:
  • Controlled experiments are hard: you can't rerun a recession with one thing changed.
  • Many things change at once, so causes are hard to separate.
  • People react to forecasts and policies, so the thing studied can change.
  • Microeconomics studies individual markets, households and firms; macroeconomics the whole economy.
  • Nine key concepts run through the course: scarcity, choice, efficiency, equity, economic well-being, sustainability, change, interdependence, intervention.
Mission 2

Models, hypotheses and evidence

  • A model is a simplified picture of how part of the economy works (e.g. the PPC, supply and demand).
  • It rests on simplifying assumptions: e.g. two goods, people act rationally, nothing else changes.
  • A good model leaves out detail so the key link is clear, as a map does.
  • The method: use logic to form a hypothesis → test it against empirical evidence → keep it or refute it. Hypotheses that survive many tests become theories.
  • Evidence can refute a hypothesis, but never prove it true for good.
  • Say: "the model assumes…" — not: "the model is wrong because real life is messier". Judge a model by whether it explains and predicts.
Mission 2

Ceteris paribus

  • Ceteris paribus = "other things being equal".
  • Economists change one variable and hold all others constant, to isolate its effect.
  • Example: if bus fares rise, ceteris paribus, fewer bus journeys are demanded. Incomes, fuel prices and the weather are held fixed.
  • In the real world other things rarely stay equal, so data must be read with care.
Quick check
?A student notices that one summer the price of ice cream rose and so did the quantity sold. The student concludes that higher prices make people buy more ice cream. What is the main flaw?
Mission 2

Positive and normative

  • A positive statement is about what is, was or will be. It can be tested against evidence.
  • A normative statement is about what ought to be. It rests on a value judgement, so evidence alone cannot settle it.
  • A positive statement can be false and still be positive: "Inflation was 90% last year" (illustrative) can be checked and refuted.
  • A prediction is positive: it can be tested once the future arrives.
  • Clue words for normative: should, ought, fair, too high, better. But check the meaning, not just the word.
  • Say: "positive = testable" — not: "positive = true" or "positive = optimistic".
Quick check
?Which of these statements is normative?
Mission 2

Value judgements in policy

  • A value judgement is a view based on what someone believes is right, fair or important.
  • Policy needs both: positive analysis (what will happen?) and normative choices (is that outcome good?).
  • Example: economists may agree a tax will cut pollution and raise prices for poorer households. Whether that trade-off is worth it is a value judgement.
  • Governments make value judgments in policy making whenever they rank goals.
  • So economists can agree on the evidence and still disagree on the policy.
Mission 2

Equity and equality

  • Equality: outcomes are the same, or similar, for different people or groups.
  • Equity: fairness. It is a normative idea: people disagree on what is fair.
  • Equal shares are not always seen as fair: e.g. people with greater needs may get more.
  • Measuring inequality (e.g. of income) is positive. Deciding how much inequality is fair is normative.
Mission 2Evaluate

How scientific is economics?

  • Against: forecasts can fail badly. In November 2008, visiting the London School of Economics, Queen Elizabeth II asked why nobody had seen the financial crisis coming.
  • In 2009 a group of economists replied that it was "a failure of the collective imagination of many bright people".
  • For: economists test ideas against evidence, increasingly with experiments. The 2019 Nobel prize in economics went to Banerjee, Duflo and Kremer for field experiments on reducing global poverty.
  • It depends on the question: the direction of an effect (a tax cuts sales) is often clearer than its exact size or timing.
  • It depends on the data: natural experiments and large datasets make causes easier to separate.
Mission check

?Two economists discuss fuel duty (illustrative figures).
Economist 1: "A 10% rise in fuel duty would cut fuel use by 3% in the long run."
Economist 2: "Fuel duty should not rise, because poorer households spend a larger share of their income on fuel."

A fact inside a statement doesn't make its conclusion positive; the word "should" carries a value.

Mission 3

The four factors of production

  • Factors of production: the resources used to produce goods and services.
  • Land: all natural resources: soil, water, minerals, fish, forests.
  • Labour: human effort, physical and mental, used in production.
  • Capital: man-made goods used to produce other goods and services: machines, tools, buildings, roads.
  • Entrepreneurship: organising the other factors and taking the risk of production.
  • Capital depends on use: a car is capital for a taxi driver, a consumer good for a family.
  • Say: "capital = goods used to make other goods" — not: "capital = money". Money buys capital; it does not produce anything itself.
Quick check
?Which is the best example of enterprise (entrepreneurship) as a factor of production?
Mission 3

Economic agents and what they want

  • Households: own the factors of production and sell their services; buy goods and services. Usual aim: maximise utility (satisfaction).
  • Firms: hire factors to produce goods and services. Usual aim: maximise profit; others include growth, market share, survival.
  • Government: taxes, spends, provides public services, makes laws. Aims include growth, jobs, stable prices and a fairer distribution of income.
  • Banks and the financial sector: take in savings and lend to households and firms.
  • Foreign sector: foreign firms and households that buy exports and sell imports.
Mission 3

The circular flow: everyone depends on everyone

  • Households sell factor services to firms. Firms pay factor incomes (wages, rent, interest, profit).
  • Households spend that income on goods and services. Their spending is firms' revenue.
  • Real flows (factors, goods) go one way; money flows go the other.
  • One agent's spending is another agent's income: interdependence.
  • Leakages take money out of the flow: saving (to banks), taxes (to government), imports (to the foreign sector).
  • Injections put money in: investment (often financed by banks), government spending, exports.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Who decides what gets made: prices, planners or a mix of the two?

Make a guess: In a country of millions, who decides how many loaves of bread are baked tomorrow?

Mission 4

Three questions every economy must answer

  • Resources are scarce, so every society must decide:
  • What to produce (and how much)?
  • How to produce it (which resources, which methods)?
  • For whom to produce it (who gets the output)?
  • An economic system is the way a society answers these questions.
  • It has two parts: who owns the resources, and the coordinating mechanism that links millions of decisions.
Mission 4

Free market economy

  • Resources are privately owned.
  • The price mechanism coordinates: prices rise where goods are wanted and scarce, fall where they are not.
  • Firms seek profit; consumers choose what to buy. Spending acts like votes (consumer sovereignty).
  • The state's role is small: protect property rights, enforce contracts, keep law and order.
  • Strengths: incentives to work, invest and innovate; prices carry information quickly; wide choice.
  • Weaknesses: some goods under-provided (e.g. street lighting); pollution ignored; large inequality of income and wealth; booms and slumps.
Mission 4

Planned (command) economy

  • Most resources are owned by the state.
  • A central planning agency answers what, how and for whom: it sets output targets and fixes prices.
  • Strengths: resources can be directed to national priorities (e.g. heavy industry, defence, health); income can be kept more equal; full employment can be a target.
  • Weaknesses: planners lack the information to plan millions of goods; fixed prices bring shortages and queues or surpluses; weak incentives to cut costs or innovate; little consumer choice.
Quick check
?In a planned economy, a central agency sets each factory's output and fixes all prices. A shortage of winter coats appears. What is most likely to happen next under this system?
Mission 4

Mixed economy

  • Both the market (private sector) and the state (public sector) allocate resources.
  • Most goods are bought and sold in markets.
  • The state provides some goods (e.g. defence, roads, often schools and healthcare), taxes, regulates and redistributes income.
  • Every real economy is mixed. They differ in how much the state does.
  • Think of a spectrum: free market at one end, planned at the other. Countries sit in between, and move along it over time.
Mission 4

Real case: planning and reform

  • The Soviet Union ran its economy through central plans, with five-year plans from 1928 until the state broke up in 1991.
  • Planning built heavy industry fast, but consumer goods were often short and queues were common.
  • China began market reforms in 1978. Over the next few years farm households were allowed to keep or sell output above their state quota; private firms were later allowed.
  • The state still owns many large firms in China, alongside a large private sector: a mixed economy.
  • Both show economies moving along the spectrum, not fixed in one box.
Mission 4

The role of the state

  • Even market supporters give the state a role. Common roles:
  • Legal framework: property rights, contracts, competition law.
  • Public goods and services the market under-provides (e.g. defence, street lighting).
  • Correcting market failure: pollution, information gaps, monopoly.
  • Redistribution: taxes and benefits to reduce inequality.
  • Stabilising the economy in booms and slumps.
  • But governments can fail too: poor information, slow decisions, unintended effects (government failure).
  • IB frames this as market versus government intervention in answering the basic questions.
Mission 4Evaluate

Which system allocates resources best?

  • It depends on information: prices pass on dispersed knowledge well; planners struggle with millions of goods.
  • It depends on goals: markets tend to score well on efficiency and choice; equality and national priorities may need the state.
  • It depends on market failures: where externalities or public goods matter, markets alone under- or over-provide.
  • It depends on the state's capacity: good intervention needs good information, honest institutions and incentives.
  • Economists disagree about where on the spectrum an economy should sit. Much of that is a value judgement.
Quick check
?In Country Y, most firms are privately owned and most prices are set in markets. The state funds schools and hospitals from taxes and sets a minimum wage. How is Country Y's economy best described?
Mission check

?A sudden frost destroys half of a country's coffee harvest. How would the response most likely differ between a free market economy and a planned economy with fixed prices?

Markets let a price change do the coordinating; plans need someone to notice and decide.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Scarcity: limited resources, unlimited wants. It forces choices and trade-offs.
  • Opportunity cost is the value of the next best alternative forgone, even when the price is zero.
  • Every economy must decide what, how and for whom to produce.

Mission 2

  • Positive statements can be tested (even if false); normative statements rest on value judgements.
  • Ceteris paribus: change one thing, hold the rest equal.
  • Economics is a social science: it uses models and evidence, but controlled experiments are hard.

Mission 3

  • Four factors: land, labour, capital, enterprise (in your course: entrepreneurship). Capital is man-made goods used in production, not money.
  • Households, firms and government (in your course: also banks and the foreign sector) depend on each other.

Mission 4

  • Every economy must answer what, how and for whom; systems differ in ownership and in their coordinating mechanism.
  • Free market: prices and private ownership. Planned: the state owns and plans. Mixed: both; every real economy is mixed.
