Mission 1

Four key words

  • Peace: not just the absence of war or conflict, but harmony, security and justice. Hebrew shalom and Arabic salaam mean wholeness and well-being.
  • Justice: fairness; giving each person what is due to them; putting right what is wrong.
  • Forgiveness: letting go of resentment and the wish for revenge towards someone who has wronged you.
  • Reconciliation: restoring a broken relationship, so that former enemies can live or work together again.
  • They link: justice is often needed for lasting peace; forgiveness opens the way to reconciliation.

Mission 1

Christian teaching on forgiveness

  • In the Lord's Prayer: "Forgive us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors." (Matthew 6:12, WEBBE)
  • "If you forgive men their trespasses, your heavenly Father will also forgive you." (Matthew 6:14, WEBBE)
  • Peter asks how often to forgive: "until seventy times seven" (Matthew 18:22, WEBBE): without limit.
  • Jesus on the cross: "Father, forgive them, for they don't know what they are doing." (Luke 23:34, WEBBE)
  • God has given Christians "the ministry of reconciliation" (2 Corinthians 5:18, WEBBE). Catholics receive forgiveness in the sacrament of Reconciliation.
  • Forgiveness does not cancel justice: most Christians say a forgiven offender may still rightly face punishment.

Mission 1

Justice and human dignity

  • "Let justice roll on like rivers, and righteousness like a mighty stream." (Amos 5:24, WEBBE)
  • "What does the LORD require of you, but to act justly, to love mercy, and to walk humbly with your God?" (Micah 6:8, WEBBE)
  • Imago Dei: "God created man in his own image" (Genesis 1:27, WEBBE): every person has equal dignity.
  • Catholic Social Teaching: Gaudium et Spes 29 (paraphrase): all people share equal dignity, so discrimination is against God's will. GS 78: peace is not merely the absence of war; it is the fruit of justice.
  • Victims: justice matters for victims; it recognises the wrong done to them.
  • Non-religious: Humanists value justice and human rights on the basis of shared humanity and reason (e.g. the Universal Declaration of Human Rights, 1948).
Mission 1

Peace and peacemaking for Christians

  • "Blessed are the peacemakers, for they shall be called children of God." (Matthew 5:9, WEBBE)
  • Christians differ: Quakers and some others are pacifist; most churches accept force as a last resort (see Mission 4).
Mission 1

The Truth and Reconciliation Commission

  • South Africa: under apartheid (1948–1994) the white minority government denied rights to the Black majority; violence came from the state and from some opponents.
  • After the first free elections (1994), the TRC was set up (1995), chaired by Archbishop Desmond Tutu (1931–2021).
  • Victims told their stories in public hearings. Those who fully confessed politically motivated crimes could receive amnesty.
  • Aim: restorative justice: truth and healing, rather than revenge. Tutu, a Christian, argued there is no future without forgiveness.
  • Criticisms: some victims felt justice was denied; reparations were slow; some offenders never came forward.
Mission 1Evaluate

"Forgiveness should always be offered, whatever the wrong."

  • Reasons for: Jesus teaches forgiveness without limit (Matthew 18:22) and forgives from the cross; forgiveness frees the victim from bitterness; it can break cycles of revenge.
  • Also for: the TRC shows it can help a nation.
  • Reasons against: some wrongs, such as genocide, seem unforgivable; many Jews teach that only the victim can forgive a wrong done to them, so no one can forgive on behalf of the murdered; forgiveness offered too easily can excuse wrong or deny victims justice.
  • Another view: forgiveness may be a goal, but it cannot be forced; it is different from excusing, forgetting or letting off punishment.
  • Humanists: forgiveness is often good for everyone, but it is a free choice, not a duty.
  • Judge it by: Is forgiveness possible for every wrong? Who has the right to forgive? Can forgiveness and justice go together?

Mission 2

Violence and the human condition

  • Violence: using force to hurt, damage or control. It can be physical, verbal, emotional, or online.
  • The Bible shows violence early in the human story: after the Fall, "Cain rose up against Abel, his brother, and killed him" (Genesis 4:8, WEBBE).
  • By Noah's time, "the earth was filled with violence" (Genesis 6:11, WEBBE).
  • Christians see this as a result of sin: human nature is damaged, so violence is part of the human condition.
  • Jesus traces violence to the heart: being angry with a brother "without a cause" puts a person in danger of judgement (Matthew 5:22, WEBBE).
Mission 2

Christian teaching on violence

  • "Love your enemies, bless those who curse you, do good to those who hate you, and pray for those who mistreat you and persecute you" (Matthew 5:44, WEBBE).
  • "Whoever strikes you on your right cheek, turn to him the other also." (Matthew 5:39, WEBBE)
  • At his arrest: "all those who take the sword will die by the sword" (Matthew 26:52, WEBBE).
  • "Repay no one evil for evil." (Romans 12:17, WEBBE)
  • Almost all Christians reject violence for personal revenge. They differ on force by the state: most accept it as a last resort in self-defence or war (see Mission 4); Quakers and some others refuse all violence.

Mission 2

Righteous anger

  • Righteous anger: anger at injustice or wrong done to others, not at personal insult.
  • Jesus "made a whip of cords and drove all out of the temple" and "overthrew their tables" (John 2:15, WEBBE): anger at the Temple being used for trade.
  • "Be angry, and don't sin." (Ephesians 4:26, WEBBE). Christians read this as: anger itself is not sin; what we do with it matters.
  • Catholic teaching (CCC 2302, paraphrase): anger that seeks revenge is sinful; anger that seeks to correct wrong and uphold justice can be right.
  • Righteous anger aims at the wrong, not at destroying the person; many Christians say it should lead to action for justice, not violence.
  • Humanists also see anger at injustice as a healthy motive for change, if channelled peacefully.
Mission 2

Violent protest

  • Protest: a public show of disagreement. In the UK peaceful protest is legal; violent protest (riots, damage, attacks) is not.
  • Examples: some suffragettes smashed windows (1912); the ANC turned to armed struggle against apartheid (1961); Martin Luther King (1929–1968) led non-violent protest for civil rights.
  • Most Christians support non-violent protest, following King and the example of Jesus.
  • Catholic teaching: Pope Paul VI (Populorum Progressio 31, 1967, paraphrase) warned that revolution brings new injustice, except in the case of long-standing tyranny that gravely harms human rights.
  • Some Christians joined resistance to tyranny: Dietrich Bonhoeffer (1906–1945), a Lutheran pastor, was executed for links to the plot against Hitler.
  • Non-religious views vary too: most Humanists favour peaceful change; some argue violence may be justified against tyranny.
Mission 2

Bullying and cyberbullying

  • Bullying: repeated behaviour meant to hurt someone, where there is an imbalance of power.
  • Types: physical, verbal, social (exclusion, rumours), and cyberbullying (messages, images, posts online). Cyberbullying can follow a person everywhere, 24 hours a day, often anonymously.
  • Why it happens: wanting power or status, prejudice, peer pressure, having been bullied oneself.
  • Christian teaching: every person is made in God's image (Genesis 1:27); the Golden Rule (Matthew 7:12); "no corrupt speech" but words "for building others up" (Ephesians 4:29, WEBBE).
  • Inclusion: "you are all one in Christ Jesus" (Galatians 3:28, WEBBE).
  • Islam: "Let not a folk deride a folk who may be better than they" (Surah 49:11, Pickthall).
  • Solutions: tell a trusted adult; report and block online; school anti-bullying policies; bystanders speaking up; restorative meetings.

Mission 2Evaluate

"Violence is never justified."

  • Reasons for: Jesus' teaching (Matthew 5:39, 5:44; Luke 6:27–31) and example; violence breeds more violence (Matthew 26:52); non-violent movements (Gandhi, King) achieved change.
  • Reasons against: self-defence and protecting the innocent; the Temple shows anger at injustice can lead to action.
  • Also against: resistance to tyranny (Bonhoeffer).
  • Another view: force by police or a lawful state differs from personal violence; "never" may be too absolute.
  • Non-religious: Humanists generally oppose violence, but most accept self-defence and some just wars.
  • Judge it by: Who is using force, and why? Is it the last resort? Does it protect others or seek revenge?

Mission 3

Forgiveness, repentance, reconciliation

  • Forgiveness: choosing to let go of anger and the wish for revenge against someone who has wronged you.
  • It is not pretending the wrong did not happen, and not saying it was acceptable.
  • Repentance: being truly sorry for wrongdoing and turning away from it (metanoia in Christianity; teshuvah in Judaism; tawbah in Islam).
  • Reconciliation: restoring a broken relationship, so both sides can live in peace again.
Mission 3Read the source

Jesus on forgiveness

"Lord, how often shall my brother sin against me, and I forgive him? Until seven times?" Jesus said to him, "I don't tell you until seven times, but, until seventy times seven." (Matthew 18:21–22)

"For if you forgive men their trespasses, your heavenly Father will also forgive you. But if you don't forgive men their trespasses, neither will your Father forgive your trespasses." (Matthew 6:14–15)

  • "Peace I leave with you. My peace I give to you; not as the world gives, I give to you." (John 14:27) Christ's peace makes reconciliation possible.
  • On the cross: "Father, forgive them, for they don't know what they are doing." (Luke 23:34)

Show answer

Mission 3

Forgiveness and punishment

  • The tension: if Christians must forgive without limit, should offenders be punished at all?
  • Most Christians answer: forgiveness is personal; justice is the state's duty (Romans 13:4). A victim can forgive and still want a fair sentence.
  • Punishment can itself serve the offender's good: it helps them face the wrong and change.
  • Example: Pope John Paul II was shot in 1981 by Mehmet Ali Ağca. In 1983 he visited Ağca in prison and forgave him, yet Ağca stayed in prison.
  • Some argue the other way: punishing someone you have forgiven still looks like holding on to the wrong.

Mission 3Diversity

Different Christian and non-religious views

  • Unconditional forgiveness: many Christians forgive whether or not the offender repents, following Jesus on the cross (Luke 23:34).
  • Conditional forgiveness: others point to "If he repents, forgive him" (Luke 17:3); full reconciliation needs repentance.
  • Catholic teaching: God's forgiveness is offered to all and received through repentance, especially in the Sacrament of Reconciliation; Catholics are called to forgive as they are forgiven.
  • Humanists: forgiveness can heal both victim and offender and help society; but it is a choice, not a duty, and victims should never be pressured to forgive.
  • Some non-religious people argue some acts should not be forgiven, out of respect for victims.
Mission 3

Examples of forgiveness

  • Gordon Wilson (Methodist): his daughter Marie was killed in the Enniskillen bombing in 1987. He said he bore the bombers no ill will and prayed for them; he later worked for peace.
  • Gee Walker (Christian): her son Anthony was murdered in a racist attack in Liverpool in 2005. She said she forgave his killers, because of her faith.
  • Azim Khamisa (Muslim): his son Tariq was shot by a 14-year-old in 1995. He forgave the boy and set up a foundation with the boy's grandfather to stop youth violence.
  • Each acted from personal belief; none said the crime did not matter.
Mission 3Evaluate

"Some actions are unforgivable."

  • Reasons for: some crimes (murder, genocide) cause harm that can never be repaired. Forgiving may seem to disrespect victims. Some offenders show no repentance. Humanists: forgiveness is a choice, not a duty.
  • Reasons against: Jesus taught "seventy times seven" and forgave on the cross. Examples like Gordon Wilson and Gee Walker show it is possible.
  • Another view: forgiving the person is possible, while the act stays wrong and justice is still done.
  • Judge it by: Who has the right to forgive? Must the offender repent? Does forgiveness cancel punishment?

Mission 4: Can a war ever be fought justly, and who decides the rules?

Make a guess: Can a war ever be fought justly, and who decides the rules?

Mission 4

Where the idea came from

  • Just war theory: a set of conditions that, Christians in this tradition hold, must all be met for a war to be morally right, and limits on how it is fought.
  • St Augustine of Hippo (354–430) asked how Christians, taught to love enemies, could serve as soldiers.
  • His answer: war may be a sad necessity to restore peace and stop wrongdoing, never for revenge or gain.
  • In City of God (XIX, 12) he writes that "peace is the end sought for by war".
  • St Thomas Aquinas (c. 1225–1274) set out three conditions in the Summa Theologiae (II-II, q. 40, a. 1).
  • Later thinkers (e.g. Francisco de Vitoria, c. 1483–1546; Hugo Grotius, 1583–1645) added more, including rules for conduct in war.
Mission 4Read the source

Aquinas' three conditions

Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologiae II-II, q. 40, a. 1 (English Dominican translation):

> "In order for a war to be just, three things are necessary. First, the authority of the sovereign by whose command the war is to be waged."

  • Second: "a just cause": those attacked deserve it because of some wrong they have done.
  • Third: "a rightful intention": to advance good or avoid evil, not for cruelty or power.
  • Aquinas quotes Augustine: "we go to war that we may have peace."

Q1 Why can a private person not start a just war?

Show answer

Only the ruler responsible for the common good has authority.

Q2 Could a war with authority and a just cause still be unjust?

Show answer

Yes: a wicked intention, such as revenge or greed, makes it unlawful.

Mission 4

The criteria today

Going to war (jus ad bellum):

  • Just cause: e.g. self-defence against attack, or protecting the innocent.
  • Legitimate authority: declared by a lawful government, not a private group.
  • Right intention: to restore peace and justice, not to gain land, wealth or revenge.
  • Last resort: every peaceful means (talks, sanctions) has been tried.
  • Reasonable chance of success: no pointless loss of life.
  • Proportionality: the good achieved must outweigh the harm caused.

Fighting the war (jus in bello):

  • Discrimination: civilians must never be deliberately targeted.
  • Proportional force: no more force than needed; prisoners and the wounded treated humanely.

Mission 4Read the source

Catechism 2309

The Catechism of the Catholic Church (CCC 2309) lists "strict conditions for legitimate defense by military force". All must be met at the same time (paraphrase):

  • The damage done by the aggressor is lasting, grave and certain.
  • All other means of stopping it have proved impractical or ineffective.
  • There are serious prospects of success.
  • Using arms must not produce evils graver than the evil to be removed; modern weapons weigh heavily in this.
  • The Catechism calls these the traditional elements of the "just war" doctrine.
  • Judging them belongs to those responsible for the common good (lawful leaders), not to individuals.

Q1 Which condition do modern weapons make hardest to meet?

Show answer

The last: not causing greater evils.

Q2 Why does the Catechism speak of "defense"?

Show answer

Mission 4

Christian sources and views

  • "Let every soul be in subjection to the higher authorities, for there is no authority except from God" (Romans 13:1). The ruler "doesn't bear the sword in vain" (Romans 13:4).
  • Catholic and Anglican teaching, and many other Protestants, accept just war as a last resort.
  • Quakers and some Mennonites reject it: Jesus taught non-violence (pacifism in a later lesson).

Mission 4Evaluate

"A just war is impossible today."

  • Reasons for: modern weapons (bombs on cities, nuclear weapons) cannot spare civilians, so discrimination and proportionality fail. Pope Francis (Fratelli Tutti, 2020, §258) argues it is now very hard to apply the old just war criteria (paraphrase). Pacifists say war is never the most loving way.
  • Reasons against: a country that is invaded still has a just cause to defend itself; the UN Charter (1945, Article 51) recognises this. Intervention may stop genocide. Precision weapons can reduce civilian deaths.
  • Another view: the theory still matters because it is used to judge and limit wars, even when no war fully meets it.
  • Judge it by: Can any modern war meet every condition? Is doing nothing ever worse? Who decides whether the conditions are met?
Mission 4Worked answer

Belief, source, development

Question: Explain two reasons why many Christians accept the Just War theory.

  • Reason 1: Lawful governments have God-given authority to protect people. Source: the ruler "doesn't bear the sword in vain" (Romans 13:4). Development: so a government may use force to defend its citizens, as long as the war is a last resort.
  • Reason 2: War may be needed to restore peace and protect the innocent. Development: Augustine taught that peace is the aim of a just war; the strict conditions stop war becoming revenge or greed.

Mission 5

Weapons of mass destruction

  • WMD: weapons that can kill huge numbers of people and cause great damage, without distinguishing soldiers from civilians.
  • Nuclear: blast, heat and radiation. Hiroshima (6 August 1945) and Nagasaki (9 August 1945) are the only wartime uses.
  • Chemical: poison gases (e.g. nerve agents). Biological: deadly diseases spread deliberately.
  • Effects last for generations: radiation sickness, cancers, poisoned land and water.
  • International law: Biological Weapons Convention (1972); Chemical Weapons Convention (1993); Nuclear Non-Proliferation Treaty (1968); Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons (2017), which nuclear-armed states, including the UK, have not joined.
Mission 5

Nuclear deterrence

  • Deterrence: owning nuclear weapons so that no enemy dares attack, for fear of devastating retaliation.
  • "Mutually assured destruction": if both sides would be destroyed, neither strikes first.
  • The UK keeps a nuclear deterrent (Trident submarines).
  • For: no nuclear war between major powers since 1945; protects a nation against blackmail.
  • Against: accidents or misjudgement could cause catastrophe; enormous cost; encourages other states to seek weapons; terrorists cannot be deterred; it rests on a threat to kill civilians.
  • Unilateral disarmament: giving weapons up alone. Multilateral: all sides disarming together by agreement.

?Which statement correctly describes nuclear deterrence?
Mission 5

Catholic teaching

  • Second Vatican Council, Gaudium et Spes 80 (1965): any act of war aimed at destroying whole cities and their people is "a crime against God and man himself" (also CCC 2314).
  • The arms race is not a safe way to keep peace (Gaudium et Spes 81).
  • John Paul II (1982): deterrence "may still be judged morally acceptable", but only as a step towards progressive disarmament.
  • Pope Francis (2017): the threat to use nuclear weapons, and even possessing them, is to be firmly condemned. At Hiroshima (2019) he called their use and possession immoral.
  • The Holy See was among the first states to ratify the Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons (2017).
  • Different Catholic views: some Catholics still accept deterrence for now, until all sides disarm together; others (e.g. Pax Christi) campaign for disarmament now.
Mission 5

Other Christian views

  • Many Anglicans and other Protestants have accepted deterrence as a temporary necessity while working for multilateral disarmament.
  • Quakers, Methodists and Christian CND oppose nuclear weapons outright: they cannot meet the just war rule of discrimination.
  • "all those who take the sword will die by the sword" (Matthew 26:52).
  • Genesis 1–2: humans are stewards of creation; WMD can poison the earth for generations.
Mission 5

Utilitarian and non-religious arguments

  • Utilitarianism (Jeremy Bentham, 1748–1832; John Stuart Mill, 1806–1873): actions are right "in proportion as they tend to promote happiness" (Mill, Utilitarianism, 1863).
  • Argument for acquiring WMD: if deterrence prevents a world war, owning them produces the greatest good for the greatest number.
  • Some argued the Hiroshima bomb saved more lives than it took by ending the war sooner; historians dispute this.
  • Utilitarian argument against: even a small risk of nuclear war risks suffering on an unimaginable scale; the money could relieve poverty.
  • Humanists and atheists judge by consequences and human rights; most support multilateral disarmament; some accept deterrence as the lesser evil for now.
  • Religious responses: some believers accept the deterrence argument; others say deliberately threatening civilians is wrong whatever the outcome.

Mission 5Evaluate

"Nuclear deterrence is morally justified."

  • Reasons for: no nuclear war between great powers since 1945; a nation has a duty to protect its people; utilitarian benefit if war is prevented; John Paul II (1982) accepted it as a temporary step.
  • Reasons against: it rests on a willingness to kill millions of civilians; one accident could be catastrophic; Francis (2017) condemned even possession; Qur'an 5:32 and the First Precept treat each life as precious; vast cost.
  • Another view: acceptable only temporarily, with real steps towards multilateral disarmament.
  • Judge it by: Is threatening an act as wrong as doing it? Has deterrence actually kept the peace? What happens if one side disarms alone?

Mission 6

The human cost

  • Civilians (people not fighting) are killed, injured and bereaved; homes, schools and hospitals are destroyed.
  • Hidden wounds: trauma, grief and post-traumatic stress, for civilians and soldiers.
  • After the war: landmines and unexploded bombs kill and maim for decades.
  • Families are separated; disease and hunger follow when water, power and farms are destroyed.
  • Soldiers too: death, disability and mental injury; many return to families who must care for them.
Mission 6

Refugees and child soldiers

  • Refugee: someone forced to flee their country because of war or persecution (1951 Refugee Convention). Internally displaced people flee but stay inside their own country.
  • Refugees lose home, work, school and community, and often wait years in camps.
  • The UN refugee agency (UNHCR, 1950) protects and supports them.
  • Child soldiers: children under 18 used by armed forces or groups as fighters, porters, spies or cooks.
  • An international agreement (Optional Protocol to the Convention on the Rights of the Child, 2000) sets 18 as the minimum age for taking direct part in fighting.
  • Harm: lost childhood and education, trauma, and difficulty returning home, where they may be feared or rejected.
Mission 6

Economic and environmental cost

  • Economic: money spent on weapons is not spent on schools, hospitals or aid; destroyed roads, factories and farms take decades to rebuild.
  • US President Eisenhower (1953): "Every gun that is made, every warship launched, every rocket fired signifies, in the final sense, a theft from those who hunger and are not fed, those who are cold and are not clothed."
  • Environmental: poisoned land and water (e.g. the defoliant Agent Orange in Vietnam), burning oil wells (Kuwait, 1991), destroyed forests and farmland, landmines that make fields unusable.
  • Pope Francis (Laudato Si', 2015, §57) teaches that war always does grave harm to the environment and to peoples' cultural riches, and nuclear and biological weapons magnify the risk (paraphrase).
  • Even ancient rules limited this: do not cut down an enemy's fruit trees (Deuteronomy 20:19).

Mission 6

Christian teaching on victims of war

  • The Catechism (CCC 2313) teaches that non-combatants, wounded soldiers and prisoners must be respected and treated humanely (paraphrase).
  • Pope Francis's four verbs for migrants and refugees (2018): welcome, protect, promote and integrate.
  • Christians believe every person, including the enemy, the refugee and the former child soldier, has dignity as made in God's image (Genesis 1:27).
  • Christian responses: prayer, emergency aid, hosting refugees, campaigning for peace and fair treatment, rebuilding communities.
  • Diversity: Christians agree that refugees must be helped; they differ on how many a country should take and how quickly.
  • Non-religious views: Humanists support refugees on the basis of human rights and shared humanity; many work through secular agencies such as the Red Cross.
Mission 6

Religious organisations helping victims

  • CAFOD (Catholic Agency for Overseas Development, 1962), part of the worldwide Caritas network: emergency food, water and shelter in war zones; long-term development; local peacebuilding and trauma support.
  • Why: Matthew 25:35–40; Catholic social teaching on dignity and the common good.
  • Christian Aid (1945): founded by British and Irish churches to help refugees after the Second World War; still works in conflict zones.
  • Jesuit Refugee Service (1980): accompanies, serves and speaks up for refugees, including education in camps.
Quick check
?A Catholic charity gives emergency shelter and counselling to families fleeing a war, without asking their religion. Which teaching most directly explains why it helps everyone equally?
Mission 6

Religion in 21st-century conflicts

  • In some conflicts religion has marked who is targeted: e.g. the so-called Islamic State's persecution of Yazidis, Christians and Shi'a Muslims in Iraq and Syria (2014–17).
  • Often religion is bound up with ethnic and political identity: whether the conflict is "about" religion is debated.
  • Religious leaders as peacemakers: in the Central African Republic (from 2013), the Catholic archbishop of Bangui, Dieudonné Nzapalainga, worked with Imam Omar Kobine Layama and Protestant leaders to calm violence between Christian and Muslim militias.
  • Churches, mosques and temples often shelter the displaced and distribute aid.
  • Catholic responses: the Pope and bishops speak for persecuted minorities of every faith; Caritas agencies support victims on all sides.
Mission 6Evaluate

"Religious groups should focus on helping the victims of war, not on trying to stop wars."

  • Reasons for: helping victims is concrete and urgent (Matthew 25); religious groups have little power over governments; taking sides in politics may risk their neutrality and access.
  • Reasons against: "Blessed are the peacemakers" (Matthew 5:9); preventing war prevents victims; Romero, King and Sant'Egidio show religious groups can change events; aid without justice treats symptoms, not causes.
  • Another view: the two belong together: CAFOD and Caritas give aid and also work for peacebuilding.
  • Judge it by: Which saves more lives in the long run? Does campaigning against war cost an agency its access to victims? What do the religion's sources command?

The costs of war last long after the fighting; religious responses aim to heal people as well as rebuild places.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Peace (harmony, shalom, salaam), justice (fairness), forgiveness (letting go of revenge) and reconciliation (restoring relationships) are linked but different.
  • Christians are taught to forgive without limit (Matthew 6:12–14, 18:21–35) and to act justly (Micah 6:8, Amos 5:24); human dignity rests on the image of God; forgiveness does not cancel justice.
  • South Africa's Truth and Reconciliation Commission chose truth and amnesty over revenge, and was criticised for denying some victims justice.

Mission 2

  • Violence is part of the human condition in the Bible (Cain, Genesis 4:8); Jesus teaches love of enemies, turning the other cheek and the Golden Rule (Matthew 5:39, 5:44; Luke 6:27–31).
  • Righteous anger targets injustice, not people (John 2:15; Ephesians 4:26).
  • Most believers support non-violent protest; views differ on resisting tyranny.
  • Bullying, including cyberbullying, attacks human dignity; religions teach inclusion and respect.

Mission 3

  • Forgiveness: letting go of anger and revenge; repentance: turning from wrong; reconciliation: restoring the relationship.
  • Jesus: forgive "seventy times seven" (Matthew 18:22).
  • Forgiving the person can go with justice for the act.

Mission 4

  • Just war theory (Augustine, Aquinas) sets conditions for going to war (just cause, authority, intention, last resort, success, proportionality) and rules for fighting (discrimination, proportional force).
  • CCC 2309: legitimate defence only, with grave and certain damage, no other means, serious prospects of success and no graver evils.

Mission 5

  • WMD (nuclear, chemical, biological) kill indiscriminately; deterrence keeps them so they are never used.
  • Catholic teaching condemns indiscriminate destruction (Gaudium et Spes 80); popes have moved from tolerating deterrence (1982) to condemning possession (2017).
  • Utilitarians weigh consequences; most religious traditions reject using WMD, but believers differ about deterrence.

Mission 6

  • War's costs are human (civilians, refugees, child soldiers, trauma), economic and environmental, and they outlast the fighting.
  • Christians respond from Matthew 25 and human dignity, through organisations such as CAFOD, Christian Aid and the Jesuit Refugee Service.
  • Catholic teaching: non-combatants and prisoners must be treated humanely; refugees welcomed, protected, promoted and integrated; war harms creation.
