Mission 1

Three orders

  • Threefold ministry: three orders of ordained ministers.
  • Bishop (Greek episkopos, "overseer"): leads a local church; ordains; guards teaching.
  • Presbyter (presbyteros, "elder"): from which the English word "priest" comes; leads worship and teaches under the bishop.
  • Deacon (diakonos, "servant"): practical and pastoral service, care of the poor, assisting at worship.
  • The question for this mission: when, how and why did these three become a fixed pattern, with one bishop over each city?
Mission 1

Ministry in the New Testament

  • Apostles: the Twelve, Paul and others with authority from the risen Christ.
  • Charismatic gifts: "apostles… prophets… teachers…" (1 Corinthians 12:28); leadership by gifts of the Spirit, as at Corinth.
  • Elders: appointed "in every assembly" by Paul and Barnabas (Acts 14:23); at Jerusalem "the apostles and the elders" decide together (Acts 15:6).
  • The Seven (Acts 6:1–6): chosen to serve at tables, with the laying on of hands; later tradition saw them as the first deacons (the word is not used of them).
  • Overseers and servants (deacons): Paul greets "the overseers and servants" at Philippi (Philippians 1:1, WEBBE); qualifications for both in 1 Timothy 3.

Mission 1

The Apostolic Fathers I: Didache and Clement

  • Didache (late 1st or early 2nd century): "appoint for yourselves bishops and deacons" (15.1, paraphrase); travelling prophets and apostles still honoured. A twofold pattern.
  • 1 Clement (c. 96), from the church of Rome to Corinth, where young members had removed their leaders:
  • The apostles appointed their first converts as "bishops and deacons" (42).
  • They foresaw strife over the office and arranged that tested men should succeed them (44): an early idea of succession.
  • Clement still uses "bishop" and "presbyter" for the same people.
  • The letter is sent by the church of Rome, not in the name of a single bishop.
Mission 1

The Apostolic Fathers II: Ignatius

  • Ignatius of Antioch, writing on his way to martyrdom in Rome (c. 110; date debated): the first clear threefold ministry with one bishop in each church (monepiscopacy).
  • "See that you all follow the bishop, even as Jesus Christ does the Father, and the presbytery as you would the apostles" (Smyrnaeans 8, ANF).
  • "Wherever the bishop shall appear, there let the multitude of the people also be; even as, wherever Jesus Christ is, there is the Catholic Church" (Smyrnaeans 8, ANF). First known use of "Catholic Church".
  • No valid Eucharist without the bishop or someone he appoints (Smyrnaeans 8).
  • Why so strong? Unity against false teachers (docetists, Judaisers).
  • But: his urgency may show the pattern was new or not yet universal. His letter to Rome mentions no bishop there.
Mission 1

Irenaeus and Tertullian: succession

  • Irenaeus of Lyons (c. 130–c. 202), Against Heresies (c. 180): true teaching is guaranteed by apostolic succession, the public, unbroken line of bishops from the apostles (3.3.1).
  • He lists Rome's bishops from Linus to Eleutherius, the twelfth (3.3.3), and names Polycarp of Smyrna, appointed by apostles (3.3.4).
  • Tertullian (c. 155–c. 220), Prescription against Heretics 32 (c. 200): let heretics show their bishops' line back to an apostle, as Smyrna (Polycarp, placed by John) and Rome (Clement, ordained by Peter) can.
  • Tertullian, On Baptism 17: the bishop ("chief priest") has the right to baptise; then presbyters and deacons, but only with his authority.
  • Succession here is about teaching handed down openly: the guarantee of truth against Gnostic "secret tradition" (see Mission 2).
Mission 1

Cyprian: the bishop is the church

  • Cyprian, bishop of Carthage 248/249–258 (see Mission 4).
  • "the bishop is in the Church, and the Church in the bishop" (Letter 66.8, to Florentius Pupianus; ANF): no church without its bishop.
  • Bishops now called sacerdotes (priests), and the Eucharist a sacrifice: priestly language for ministry.
  • One episcopate: "The episcopate is one, each part of which is held by each one for the whole" (On the Unity of the Catholic Church 5, ANF). Every bishop shares one office; unity through councils of bishops.
  • Each bishop answers to God for his own church; Cyprian resisted Rome's bishop Stephen over baptism (see Mission 4).

Mission 1

The Bishop of Rome

  • Peter and Paul: both died in Rome under Nero (tradition); "you are Peter, and on this rock I will build my assembly" (Matthew 16:18, WEBBE).
  • Clement (c. 96): Rome's church corrects Corinth: concern, or authority?
  • Ignatius greets Rome's church as "presiding in love" (Romans, opening; paraphrase).
  • Irenaeus: "it is a matter of necessity that every Church should agree with this Church, on account of its preeminent authority" (Against Heresies 3.3.2, ANF). The Latin is disputed: some read "agree with", others "resort to".
  • Victor (c. 190) tried to cut off the churches of Asia over the date of Easter; Irenaeus rebuked him (Eusebius, Church History 5.24).
  • Stephen (254–257) appealed to Peter's succession against Cyprian; Cyprian called Rome "the chair of Peter" (Letter 59.14) but denied it could overrule other bishops.

Mission 1

Why the threefold ministry developed

  • Death of the apostles: someone had to guard their teaching.
  • Heresy: one recognised teacher per city made truth checkable (see Mission 2).
  • Persecution: a single leader could hold a church together and decide quickly.
  • Growth and money: larger churches needed administration, care of widows and the poor.
  • Unity: one bishop, one Eucharist, one church in each place.
  • Scholars disagree on how: J. B. Lightfoot (1828–1889), "The Christian Ministry" (in St Paul's Epistle to the Philippians, 1868), argued the bishop rose out of the presbyters, not directly from the apostles, by the early 2nd century in Asia.
Mission 1

Continuing significance

  • Catholic: bishops succeed the apostles by divine institution; the Pope, as Bishop of Rome, is Peter's successor with universal authority (Vatican I, 1870; Vatican II, Lumen Gentium, 1964).
  • Orthodox: threefold ministry and apostolic succession; the Bishop of Rome was historically first among equals, not ruler of the whole church.
  • Anglican: keeps bishops, priests and deacons as "from the Apostles' time" (Preface to the 1662 Ordinal), without the papacy.
  • Presbyterian (e.g. the Presbyterian Church in Ireland): no bishops; ministers and elders govern through Kirk session, presbytery and General Assembly, appealing to the New Testament overseer = elder pattern.
  • Congregational / Baptist: each local church governs itself; pastors and deacons.
  • Ecumenical talks: the World Council of Churches' Baptism, Eucharist and Ministry (Lima, 1982) asked all churches to consider the threefold ministry as a sign of unity.
Mission 1Evaluate

Is the threefold ministry essential to the church?

  • Claim: The threefold ministry is essential to the true church.
  • For: Very early (Ignatius by c. 110), almost universal by c. 200, and unbroken in Catholic, Orthodox and Anglican churches.
  • For: It guarded teaching against heresy and held churches together through persecution (Irenaeus, Cyprian).
  • Against: Not clearly in the New Testament, where overseer and elder overlap (Acts 20, Titus 1); Lightfoot sees a development, not a command.
  • Against: Churches without bishops (Presbyterian, Baptist, Quaker) show the marks of Christian life.
  • Judgement criteria: Is scripture alone the test, or scripture and early tradition? Is a development guided by the Spirit as binding as a command? Essential to the being of the church, or to its well-being?
Mission 1Evaluate

How important was the Bishop of Rome?

  • Claim: By AD 260 the Bishop of Rome already had primacy over the whole church.
  • For: Peter and Paul's tradition; Matthew 16:18; Clement's letter; Irenaeus' "preeminent authority"; Victor's and Stephen's claims.
  • Against: Early Rome itself was led by a group of presbyters (Clement; no bishop in Ignatius' Romans).
  • Against: Irenaeus rebuked Victor; Cyprian and Firmilian resisted Stephen. Respect was not obedience.
  • Note: Catholic, Orthodox and Protestant scholars read this evidence differently; the same texts support honour, leadership or jurisdiction.
  • Judgement criteria: Prestige or legal power? Rome's claims or other churches' acceptance? Read back from later doctrine, or in its own time?

Mission 2

What "heresy" meant

  • Greek hairesis: a choice, then a school or party. In the church, a teaching that departs from the apostolic faith.
  • Orthodoxy: "right opinion"; the teaching the main body of churches recognised as handed down from the apostles.
  • The New Testament already warns of false teachers: those who deny "that Jesus Christ came in the flesh" (2 John 1:7, WEBBE).
  • 2nd century: no creeds agreed by councils, no closed New Testament, no emperor on the church's side. How do you tell true from false?
  • Three challenges this mission covers: Gnosticism, Marcion, Montanism.
Mission 2

Gnosticism: origins

  • From gnōsis, "knowledge": salvation through secret, saving knowledge of one's true divine origin.
  • Not one church but many schools, mainly 2nd century: Basilides (Alexandria), Valentinus (Alexandria, then Rome c. 135–160), the "Sethian" groups.
  • Roots debated: Platonist philosophy, Jewish speculation, Christian themes, possibly Persian dualism.
  • Sources: until the 20th century, mostly their opponents (Irenaeus, Hippolytus, Tertullian).
  • Nag Hammadi (Egypt, 1945): a buried library of 13 codices in Coptic, including the Gospel of Truth and the Apocryphon of John. Now Gnostic voices can be read directly.
Mission 2

Gnosticism: teachings

  • Dualism: spirit is good; matter is a prison or a mistake.
  • The high God is utterly remote, surrounded by the Pleroma ("fullness") of divine beings (aeons).
  • An aeon (often Sophia, Wisdom) falls; a lesser god, the Demiurge (craftsman), makes the material world. Often identified with the God of Genesis.
  • Divine spark: some humans carry a spark of the true God, trapped in a body.
  • Christ comes as a revealer of knowledge, not to die for sin; often docetic (Greek dokein, to seem): he only seemed to have a body or suffer.
  • Salvation is escape of the spirit, not resurrection of the body. Valentinians divided people into spiritual, psychic (soul-people) and material.
  • Ethics: some ascetic; opponents claimed some were libertine.
Mission 2

Marcion

  • Marcion of Sinope (Pontus), a wealthy shipowner; in Rome c. 140; expelled by the Roman church in 144. He founded a rival church with its own bishops that lasted centuries.
  • Antitheses (lost): set Old Testament against Gospel.
  • Two gods: the Creator of the Hebrew Bible is just, harsh and warlike; the good, unknown God revealed by Jesus is pure love.
  • Rejected the Hebrew Bible entirely.
  • First known canon: a shortened Luke and ten letters of Paul (no Pastorals), edited to remove what he saw as Jewish additions.
  • Docetic Christ: not born, appeared suddenly as an adult.
  • Strict asceticism: no marriage for baptised members.
  • Not strictly Gnostic (no secret knowledge or aeons), but shares the low view of the Creator and of matter.
Mission 2

Montanism

  • Montanus, in Phrygia (Asia Minor), c. 156–172 (sources differ), with two prophetesses, Priscilla (Prisca) and Maximilla. They called it the New Prophecy.
  • Ecstatic prophecy: the Paraclete (the Spirit promised in John 14:26) was said to speak directly through them, in the first person.
  • Imminent end: the New Jerusalem would descend at Pepuza in Phrygia.
  • Stricter discipline: more fasting; no second marriage; no flight in persecution; some serious sins could not be forgiven by the church.
  • Doctrine largely orthodox: the problem was authority. Could new revelation add to the apostles' teaching? Who judges a prophet?
  • Opposed by the bishops of Asia in some of the first church synods (Eusebius, Church History 5.16). In Africa it won Tertullian (see Mission 3).

Mission 2

The threefold defence

  • 1. The Rule of Faith (regula fidei): a short summary of the apostles' teaching, taught at baptism: one God, Creator of heaven and earth; one Christ Jesus, the Son, made flesh, born of a virgin, suffered, rose, ascended, coming again as judge; the Holy Spirit who spoke through the prophets (Irenaeus, Against Heresies 1.10.1; Tertullian, Prescription 13).
  • Answers: Gnostic and Marcionite two gods, and docetism.
  • 2. The Canon of Scripture: which books are apostolic and read in all churches? Marcion's short list forced the question.
  • Irenaeus: four Gospels, no more, no fewer (3.11.8), and the Hebrew scriptures as the same God's word.
  • 3. Apostolic succession: churches founded by apostles can name their bishops in a public line (see Mission 1). Their teaching is open, not secret.
  • Each defence supports the others: canon read by the Rule, taught by bishops in succession.

Mission 2

Irenaeus: life and work

  • Irenaeus (c. 130–c. 202), born in Asia Minor, probably Smyrna. As a boy he heard Polycarp, who had known the apostle John: a living link to the apostles.
  • Presbyter at Lyons (Gaul); took a letter to Rome during the persecution of 177; then bishop of Lyons after Bishop Pothinus' martyrdom.
  • Against Heresies (c. 180), 5 books: Book 1 describes Gnostic systems (especially Valentinus); Books 2–5 refute them from reason, scripture and the words of Jesus.
  • Demonstration of the Apostolic Preaching: a short summary of the faith for believers.
  • A peacemaker too: he urged Bishop Victor of Rome not to cut off the churches of Asia over Easter (Eusebius, Church History 5.24).
Mission 2

Irenaeus' answers

  • One God: the Creator is the Father of Jesus. Against two gods: the Old and New Testaments are one story.
  • Creation is good: "God saw everything that he had made, and, behold, it was very good" (Genesis 1:31, WEBBE).
  • Recapitulation (anakephalaiōsis, from Ephesians 1:10, "to sum up all things in Christ"): Christ relives Adam's story and gets it right, undoing the Fall stage by stage.
  • Real flesh: Christ was truly born and suffered; the body will be raised; the Eucharist's bread and wine (made from creation) nourish the body for resurrection.
  • Public tradition: the apostles would have told secrets to the bishops they appointed, not to Gnostic teachers (3.3.1).
  • Scripture read whole: Gnostics take passages out of context, like rearranging the stones of a king's mosaic into a dog or fox (1.8.1, paraphrase).

Mission 2Evaluate

How effective was the threefold defence?

  • Claim: The threefold defence was the main reason orthodoxy survived.
  • For: It answered each threat: the Rule against two gods and docetism; the canon against Marcion's cut-down Bible and Gnostic gospels; succession against secret tradition and new prophecy.
  • For: It gave ordinary believers clear tests, without needing philosophy.
  • Against: Marcionite churches lasted for centuries; Montanism survived into the 6th century. The defence did not end them.
  • Against: The canon was not fixed for another two centuries (Athanasius' list of 27 books, 367).
  • Against: Other factors: Gnostic groups' elitism, Christian worship and community life, later imperial support.
  • Risk: stressing bishops' authority could silence real prophecy or reform (the Montanist complaint).
  • Judgement criteria: Survival or truth? Short-term or long-term? Which strand did the most work?
Mission 2Evaluate

Irenaeus and the "winners"

  • Claim: Our picture of early heresy is distorted, because it comes from the side that won.
  • For: Irenaeus was a hostile witness; he called Marcion's teaching blasphemy and reports Polycarp calling Marcion "the first-born of Satan" (3.3.4, paraphrase).
  • For: Walter Bauer (1877–1960), Orthodoxy and Heresy in Earliest Christianity (1934): in some regions (e.g. Edessa, Egypt) what was later called heresy may have come first.
  • For: Some scholars now question whether "Gnosticism" is one thing at all (Michael Williams, Rethinking "Gnosticism", 1996; Karen King, What Is Gnosticism?, 2003).
  • Against: Nag Hammadi texts largely confirm Irenaeus' account of Valentinian and Sethian ideas.
  • Against: Critics of Bauer (e.g. H. E. W. Turner, The Pattern of Christian Truth, 1954) argue a recognisable core faith was there from the start, as in 1 Corinthians 15:3.
  • Judgement criteria: How accurate are hostile sources? Did orthodoxy develop or was it handed on? Can we judge without later definitions?
Mission 2

Why it still matters

  • Most churches still read both Testaments as one revelation of one God: Marcion's challenge set that shape.
  • Creeds (Apostles', Nicene) grew from the Rule of Faith.
  • Debates on spiritual gifts and authority (charismatic renewal) echo Montanism.
  • Popular interest in "lost gospels" and secret knowledge keeps Gnostic ideas in view.
  • Christians differ on the weight of tradition: Catholic and Orthodox churches stress succession; many Protestants stress scripture as the final test.

Mission 3

Tertullian's life

  • Quintus Septimius Florens Tertullianus, c. 155/160–after c. 220, of Carthage in Roman North Africa.
  • Well educated in Latin and Greek rhetoric and probably law; his writing argues like a court advocate.
  • Jerome (On Illustrious Men 53, 392) says he was the son of a centurion and a presbyter; many scholars doubt both details.
  • Converted c. 193–197, perhaps moved by the courage of martyrs. Married (To His Wife).
  • Writings c. 196–c. 220: about 31 survive.
  • The first major Christian writer in Latin: often called the "father of Latin theology". Cyprian read him daily and called him "the master" (Jerome, Illustrious Men 53).
Mission 3

Three kinds of writing

  • Apologetic: defending the church against popular slanders and unjust law (Apology, To the Nations, To Scapula).
  • Polemical and doctrinal: defending the church from heresy and false teaching (Prescription against Heretics, Against Marcion, Against Praxeas, On Baptism, On the Flesh of Christ).
  • Moral (disciplinary): the proper conduct of a Christian (On the Shows, On Idolatry, On the Military Crown, On the Apparel of Women, To His Wife, On Repentance).
  • Many works are hard to date; later ones show Montanist views.
Mission 3

The Apology (197)

  • Addressed to the governors of the Roman provinces.
  • Unjust procedure: Trajan's rule (do not hunt Christians; punish them if accused) is absurd: if guilty, why not search them out; if innocent, why punish? (Apology 2, paraphrase; see Pliny's letter.)
  • Christians are condemned for the name alone, and tortured to deny, not to confess.
  • Slanders answered: no child murder or incest; Christian meals are modest and shared (7–9, 39).
  • Loyal: Christians pray for the emperor and the empire (30), though they will not call him god.
  • Scapegoats: if the Tiber floods or the Nile does not, the cry goes up: "The Christians to the lion!" (40, paraphrase).

Mission 3

Against heresy: Prescription and Marcion

  • Prescription against Heretics (c. 200): a legal argument. A praescriptio blocked a case before trial. Heretics have no right to argue from the scriptures: the scriptures belong to the apostolic churches (37).
  • Test of truth: does a church agree with the churches founded by apostles, with bishops in succession (32;)?
  • Rule of Faith (13): the summary of belief, not open to endless debate.
  • "What indeed has Athens to do with Jerusalem?" (7, ANF): philosophy breeds heresy. A wall between faith and Greek thought (contrast Justin).
  • Against Marcion (5 books, c. 207–212): one God is both just and good; the Old Testament prepares for Christ; Christ had real flesh.
Mission 3

Doctrine: Trinity, flesh, baptism

  • Against Praxeas (c. 213): against Monarchianism (the Father himself became the Son). God is one substance (substantia) in three persons (personae). First to use Trinitas in Latin.
  • His complaint (ch. 1, paraphrase): Praxeas drove out prophecy and brought in heresy; he put the Paraclete to flight and crucified the Father.
  • On the Flesh of Christ (5): the Son of God died; it is to be believed because it is absurd; he rose; it is certain because it is impossible (paraphrase). Not "I believe because it is absurd" as a general rule: a rhetorical paradox.
  • On Baptism (c. 200): the first treatise on a sacrament. Water and the Spirit; the bishop's right to baptise (17); advises delaying baptism of young children and the unmarried (18).

Mission 3

Moral writings

  • Separation from idolatry: On Idolatry: no making idols, no taking oaths to gods, great caution about public office and teaching pagan literature.
  • On the Shows: Christians must avoid the games, theatre and gladiator fights: cruelty, lust and idolatry.
  • On the Military Crown (211): defends a soldier who refused the laurel crown; doubts whether a Christian can serve as a soldier at all.
  • Dress and marriage: On the Apparel of Women (modest dress; contains harsh language about women that many now criticise); To His Wife: widows should not remarry.
  • Repentance: On Repentance: one second chance after baptism for serious sin, through public penance (exomologesis).
  • On Patience: he admits he lacks it.
Mission 3

Tertullian and Montanism

  • From c. 207 his works welcome the New Prophecy (see Mission 2): the Paraclete gives fuller discipline, as Christ promised (John 16:13).
  • He calls mainstream Christians "psychics" (soul-people, unspiritual) and Montanists "spiritual".
  • Montanist-period works: On Monogamy (no second marriage), On Fasting, On Flight in Persecution (do not flee), On Modesty (attacks a bishop who claimed to forgive adultery and fornication; Tertullian calls him, mockingly, "bishop of bishops").
  • Why? His rigorism, dislike of laxity, belief in the Spirit's continuing work, and an end-time urgency.
  • Jerome says he left because of the envy and insults of the Roman clergy.
  • Augustine (On Heresies 86) knew of "Tertullianists" in Carthage, reconciled in his own day.
Mission 3

Did he leave the church?

  • Traditional view: he broke with the Catholic church and led a separate sect (Jerome; Augustine's Tertullianists).
  • Revisionist view: in Carthage the Montanists were a party within the church; he never formally left. David Rankin, Tertullian and the Church (1995); Timothy Barnes, Tertullian (1971) re-dated his life and doubted Jerome's details.
  • Evidence: no record of his excommunication; his doctrine of God and Christ stays orthodox; his quarrel is about discipline and authority.
  • He was never recognised as a saint; but his theology was used everywhere in the West.

Mission 3Evaluate

Tertullian's contribution

  • Claim: Tertullian's contribution to the church outweighs the damage of his rigorism.
  • For: Vocabulary: Trinitas, persona, substantia shaped Western doctrine through to Chalcedon (451).
  • For: A powerful apology: legal, witty, memorable; "the blood of Christians is seed".
  • For: Strong defence against Marcion and the Gnostics: one God, real flesh, apostolic tradition.
  • Against: Harsh, sometimes intolerant rhetoric; a wall against philosophy; language about women many find demeaning.
  • Against: His rigorism (one repentance, no remarriage, no flight) left little room for weak believers; Cyprian and others later took a more pastoral line (see Mission 4).
  • Against: Ended outside, or on the edge of, the mainstream: not a model of unity.
  • Judgement criteria: Ideas or character? His time or later centuries? Can a writer be both a teacher of the church and a critic of it?

Mission 4

Cyprian's life

  • Thascius Caecilius Cyprianus, born c. 200–210, a wealthy, admired teacher of rhetoric in Carthage.
  • Converted c. 246 through a presbyter, Caecilian; gave much of his wealth to the poor; described his new birth in To Donatus.
  • Made bishop of Carthage c. 248/249, by popular acclaim, within about two years of baptism. Some presbyters resented it.
  • Bishop for about ten years of crisis: persecution (250, 257–258), plague (from 252), schism and controversy.
  • Martyred 14 September 258 under Valerian: beheaded near Carthage. His last words to the sentence: "Thanks be to God" (Acts of Cyprian).
  • Sources: 81 letters (to and from him), treatises, and a Life by his deacon Pontius.
Mission 4

The Decian persecution (250)

  • Emperor Decius ordered everyone in the empire to sacrifice to the gods and get a certificate (libellus) to prove it.
  • Cyprian went into hiding (January 250 to spring 251), leading by letter. Critics, including the Roman clergy at first, called it cowardice; he said it served his people.
  • Many Christians gave way: the lapsed (lapsi).
  • Sacrificati: offered sacrifice.
  • Thurificati: burned incense.
  • Libellatici: obtained a certificate, often by bribery, without sacrificing.
  • Confessors: those who stood firm, often imprisoned or tortured, held in great honour.
Mission 4

The crisis of the lapsed

  • Letters of peace: some confessors issued notes promising forgiveness to the lapsed; some presbyters readmitted them at once (the laxist party: in Carthage, Felicissimus, supported by the presbyter Novatus).
  • Rigorists: at Rome, Novatian held that the church could never readmit those who had sacrificed; their case was for God alone. He was set up as a rival bishop of Rome against Cornelius (251): a schism.
  • Cyprian's middle way (Council of Carthage, 251):
  • Libellatici: readmitted after a period of penance, case by case.
  • Sacrificati: long penance; reconciled at least at the point of death.
  • 252/253: with a new persecution feared, all who were doing penance were readmitted, to strengthen them.
  • The bishop, with his clergy and people, decides; not individual confessors.
Mission 4

On the Lapsed (251)

  • Opens with joy for the confessors; then grief that so many fell, some running to sacrifice before they were even asked.
  • The persecution was God's testing of a church grown worldly.
  • Penance must be real and long: prayer, tears, fasting, almsgiving. Quick reconciliation is "false peace".
  • Martyrs' letters cannot override the gospel or the bishop: only God forgives, through the church's discipline.
  • Warning stories: a little girl, given food offered to idols as a baby, could not take the Eucharistic cup (25, paraphrase).
  • So Cyprian is neither laxist nor rigorist: forgiveness is possible, but through the church's process.

Mission 4

On the Unity of the Catholic Church (251)

  • Written against the schisms of Felicissimus in Carthage and Novatian in Rome.
  • The church is founded on Peter (Matthew 16:18–19), to show that unity starts from one; all the apostles share the same authority (ch. 4).
  • Images of unity (ch. 5): many rays, one sun; many branches, one tree; many streams, one spring. Cut a ray from the sun and it has no light.
  • The seamless robe of Christ (John 19:23–24), not torn by the soldiers: the church cannot be divided (ch. 7).
  • Noah's ark (ch. 6): no one outside it was saved.
  • Schismatics are like Korah, Dathan and Abiram who rebelled against Moses (ch. 18).

Mission 4

Two versions of chapter 4

  • Chapter 4 survives in two forms.
  • "Primacy text": speaks of a primacy given to Peter and of the "chair of Peter".
  • "Received text": stresses that the other apostles had equal honour and power with Peter.
  • Long debated in Catholic–Protestant argument about the papacy.
  • Maurice Bévenot, Jesuit scholar (The Tradition of Manuscripts, 1961): Cyprian wrote both: the primacy text first (251, backing Cornelius against Novatian); he revised it during his dispute with Rome (c. 256).
  • Either way, Peter is the symbol of unity; whether Rome's bishop rules other bishops is the question.
Mission 4

The baptism controversy (255–256)

  • Question: when someone baptised by a heretic or schismatic (e.g. a Novatianist) joins the Catholic church, what then?
  • Cyprian (Letters 69–75; councils of Carthage 255–256, the last with 87 bishops): baptism outside the one church is invalid: heretics have no Holy Spirit to give. Such converts must be baptised; it is not "rebaptism".
  • "There is no salvation outside the church" (salus extra ecclesiam non est, Letter 73.21).
  • Stephen of Rome (254–257): follow tradition: do not repeat baptism given in the name of the Trinity; receive converts by laying on of hands for penance.
  • Stephen threatened to break communion; Firmilian of Caesarea backed Cyprian (Letter 75).
  • Ended without a settlement when Valerian's persecution began (257).
  • Later: the Council of Arles (314) and Augustine (against the Donatists, who appealed to Cyprian) took the Roman view: Christ, not the minister, is the true baptiser. Most churches today follow that view.

Mission 4

Penance and unity: one concern

  • For Cyprian, unity and penance belong together: both are about the church as the only place of salvation.
  • The lapsed must come back into the church, through its bishop, by penance.
  • The schismatic must come back into the church, through its bishop, by baptism.
  • The bishop is the centre: "the bishop is in the Church, and the Church in the bishop" (Letter 66.8, ANF;).
  • Collegial: bishops decide together in councils; each answers to God for his church.
  • A pastoral realism: discipline strict enough to honour the martyrs, merciful enough to save the weak (contrast Tertullian's rigorism).
Mission 4Evaluate

Cyprian's achievement

  • Claim: Cyprian did more to strengthen the church than any writer of his century.
  • For: He held the church in Carthage together through persecution, schism and plague, and died a martyr.
  • For: His middle way on the lapsed became the church's practice: repentance is possible; discipline matters.
  • For: His doctrine of the church and the episcopate shaped Catholic, Orthodox and Anglican thought; On the Unity is still read in ecumenical debate.
  • Against: His flight in 250 weakened his authority at first.
  • Against: On baptism the church later rejected his view; the Donatists used his writings to justify division.
  • Against: "No salvation outside the church" can sound harsh today: many churches now speak of grace beyond visible boundaries (e.g. Vatican II on other Christians).
  • Judgement criteria: Practical leadership or lasting doctrine? Judged by his time, or by later church teaching? Did his stress on unity help or harm unity in the long run?
Mission 4Evaluate

Strict or merciful?

  • Claim: A church should readmit those who fail under pressure.
  • For: The gospel of forgiveness: Peter denied Christ three times and was restored ("Feed my sheep", John 21:17, WEBBE).
  • For: Most of the lapsed were frightened, not malicious; a church of only heroes would be tiny.
  • Against (rigorist): Easy readmission dishonours the martyrs and makes denial cheap (Novatian; Tertullian before him).
  • Middle (Cyprian): forgiveness through real penance, decided by the community's leaders.
  • Today: churches still debate discipline: restoring leaders after serious wrongdoing; Catholic practice of the sacrament of reconciliation; Protestant views of forgiveness without a set penance.
  • Judgement criteria: What protects the vulnerable? What honours costly faithfulness? Who has authority to forgive in the church's name?

Mission 5

What gives a leader authority?

  • Authority: power that people accept as rightful.
  • Max Weber (1864–1920), Economy and Society (1922): three types.
  • Traditional: "it has always been so" (inherited office, ancient custom; e.g. apostolic succession).
  • Charismatic: personal gifts that followers recognise as from God (prophets; founders; Montanus).
  • Legal-rational: rules, elections, constitutions (church synods, assemblies, written codes).
  • Most churches mix all three. A challenge to authority arises when people stop accepting one of these grounds.
Mission 5

Models of church leadership

  • Episcopal: bishops in succession. Catholic (Pope and bishops; the Magisterium, the teaching office); Orthodox (bishops in synods, patriarchs); Anglican (bishops with synods of clergy and laity; e.g. the Church of Ireland).
  • Presbyterian: no bishops; ministers and elected elders govern through Kirk session, presbytery and General Assembly (e.g. the Presbyterian Church in Ireland). A yearly Moderator.
  • Connexional: Methodist churches linked by an annual Conference.
  • Congregational: each local church governs itself (Baptist, many independent churches).
  • Charismatic / network: leaders recognised by spiritual gifts, sometimes as modern "apostles" (many Pentecostal and new churches).
  • No ordained leadership: Quakers (the Religious Society of Friends) seek the Spirit together in meetings.
Mission 5

Authority and relevance today

  • Episcopal strengths: unity across nations; a clear voice; continuity with the early church. Critiques: slow to change; distance from ordinary members.
  • Presbyterian strengths: shared leadership; accountability; lay elders. Critiques: committees and division over decisions.
  • Congregational strengths: local ownership; flexibility. Critiques: isolation; strong pastors with little oversight.
  • Charismatic strengths: energy, growth, fits a culture of experience. Critiques: depends on one personality; risk of abuse of power.
  • Believers today often ask less "Who is in charge?" and more "Is this leader trustworthy, and does this community help me live my faith?"
Mission 5

Challenges to religious authority

  • Individualism: people choose beliefs for themselves ("consumer" religion); institutions are trusted less in general.
  • Reason and science: since the Enlightenment, claims must be justified, not just handed down.
  • Scandals: abuse in churches and the way leaders handled it badly damaged trust, notably in Ireland.
  • Democratic expectations: demands for lay voices, and for women in leadership (ordained in many Protestant and Anglican churches; not ordained as priests in Catholic and Orthodox churches).
  • Internal disagreement: e.g. on sexuality and marriage, leading to divisions within some denominations.
  • Information: online access to teaching and debate bypasses local leaders.
Mission 5

Secularisation: what it is

  • Secularisation: the process by which religious thinking, practice and institutions lose social significance (Bryan Wilson, 1926–2004, Religion in Secular Society, 1966; paraphrase).
  • Three levels: society (religion loses control of law, education, welfare); institutions (churches decline, change); individuals (fewer believe or practise).
  • Causes often given: industrialisation and city life; science; pluralism (many faiths make each seem one choice among others); welfare state; rising wealth and education.
  • Steve Bruce (b. 1954), God is Dead (2002): secularisation in the West is long-term and hard to reverse.
  • Peter Berger (1929–2017) argued for it in The Sacred Canopy (1967), then changed his mind: much of the world is "as furiously religious as it ever was" (The Desecularization of the World, 1999).
Mission 5

Secularisation: impact and alternatives

  • Impact: fewer baptisms, church weddings and regular attenders in much of Europe; less church influence on law and public life; religion more private.
  • Grace Davie (b. 1946): "believing without belonging" (Religion in Britain since 1945, 1994): belief often outlasts membership.
  • "Spiritual but not religious": interest in meaning, meditation and wellbeing without institutions (Paul Heelas and Linda Woodhead, The Spiritual Revolution, 2005).
  • Growth in some places: Pentecostal and charismatic churches; migrant churches in Irish and British cities; Christianity in Africa, Asia and Latin America.
  • Church responses: courses for enquirers (e.g. Alpha); "fresh expressions" of church in cafés and homes; social action (food banks).
  • Northern Ireland has had higher churchgoing than Britain; whether it is now following the same path is debated.

Mission 5Evaluate

Is secularisation inevitable?

  • Claim: Secularisation is the inevitable result of modern society.
  • For: Steady long-term decline in practice across Western Europe (Wilson, Bruce).
  • For: Pluralism and choice make faith a private option, weakening shared religious authority.
  • Against: The USA, a modern society, remains far more religious than Europe; global religion is growing (the later Berger).
  • Against: Belief and spirituality persist without institutions (Davie; Heelas and Woodhead): religion changes form.
  • Against: New movements, migration and renewal show religion adapting, not simply declining.
  • Judgement criteria: Measured by attendance, belief or influence? Europe or the world? Decline or transformation?
Mission 5

C. S. Lewis

  • C. S. Lewis (1898–1963), born in Belfast; Oxford and Cambridge scholar of English literature; Anglican.
  • Atheist in youth; theist in 1929; Christian in 1931 (after talks with J. R. R. Tolkien and Hugo Dyson).
  • Mere Christianity (1952, from BBC radio talks 1941–44): the core belief shared by most Christians, not one denomination.
  • Moral argument: a Moral Law everyone appeals to points to a moral lawgiver behind the universe (Book 1).
  • Trilemma: Jesus' claims leave three options: a lunatic, something worse, or the Son of God; "great moral teacher" is not one of them (Book 2, ch. 3, paraphrase).
  • The Problem of Pain (1940): suffering as the cost of free will and a means of shaping souls; A Grief Observed (1961), after his wife's death, rawer and less certain.
  • Miracles (1947); The Screwtape Letters (1942); The Chronicles of Narnia (1950–56): faith through imagination.
Mission 5

Alister McGrath

  • Alister McGrath (b. 1953), also born in Belfast; doctorate in molecular biophysics, then theology; atheist as a teenager, Christian at Oxford; Anglican priest.
  • Andreas Idreos Professor of Science and Religion, Oxford (2014–2022).
  • Science and faith: not enemies; both seek truth, on critical realist lines: reality is there to be known, though our maps are partial (A Scientific Theology, 2001–03).
  • Answering the "new atheists": Dawkins' God (2004) and The Dawkins Delusion? (2007, with Joanna Collicutt McGrath): faith is not "blind trust"; Dawkins misreads theology and overreaches science.
  • The Twilight of Atheism (2004): atheism's cultural appeal is fading.
  • Natural theology renewed: seeing nature through a Christian lens, rather than proving God from nature.
  • Writes for students (Christian Theology: An Introduction, 1994) and wrote a biography of Lewis (2013).
Mission 5

Karl Rahner

  • Karl Rahner (1904–1984), German Jesuit; perhaps the most influential Catholic theologian of the 20th century.
  • Spirit in the World (1939), Hearer of the Word (1941), Theological Investigations (from 1954), Foundations of Christian Faith (1976).
  • Every person is open to God: in every act of knowing and loving we reach beyond ourselves towards mystery; God's offer of grace is part of human existence (the supernatural existential).
  • Anonymous Christians: people of other faiths or none who sincerely follow conscience may be saved by Christ's grace without knowing it.
  • Vatican II (1962–65): an expert adviser (peritus); his ideas are echoed in its openness to other religions and to the modern world.
  • The Christian of the future will need personal experience of God, a "mystic", or will not be a Christian at all (paraphrase).

Mission 5Evaluate

Their impact on religious thinking

  • Claim: These three thinkers have had a lasting impact on religious thinking.
  • For (Lewis): still among the most-read Christian writers; Mere Christianity is often cited in adult conversion stories; he made theology accessible.
  • Against (Lewis): philosophers fault the trilemma (it ignores the option that the Gospel claims are legend); his reply on naturalism in Miracles was challenged by Elizabeth Anscombe (1948) and he revised it (1960).
  • For (McGrath): a leading voice in public debate on science and religion; trains students worldwide through his textbooks.
  • Against (McGrath): critics say he answers popular atheism more than the strongest philosophical objections.
  • For (Rahner): reshaped Catholic theology and Vatican II's openness to the world and other faiths.
  • Against (Rahner): "anonymous Christian" called patronising (Hans Küng) or a weakening of mission (Hans Urs von Balthasar); hard to read.
  • Judgement criteria: Impact on scholars or ordinary believers? On one church or many? Lasting, or tied to their time?
Mission 5Evaluate

Do these thinkers answer secularisation?

  • Claim: Apologists and theologians can halt secularisation.
  • For: Lewis and McGrath give reasons that help some people believe or stay; Rahner shows faith as experience, which fits a "spiritual" age.
  • Against: Sociologists explain secularisation by social change (cities, pluralism, wealth), which arguments cannot reverse.
  • Against: Most people leave churches through drift, not argument; most come in through relationships.
  • Link: compare the early apologists: they won respect more than they ended persecution.
  • Judgement criteria: Is belief chiefly a matter of reasons, experience or belonging? Which of the three thinkers addresses which?

Mission 6

Secularisation and secularism

  • Secularisation: a process in which religious thinking, practice and institutions lose social significance (Bryan Wilson, Religion in Secular Society, 1966).
  • Secularism: a view about how society should be: religion should have no special place in public life.
Mission 6

Is Britain secularising?

  • Evidence for decline: falling church attendance, baptisms and church weddings since the 1950s. In the 2021 Census (England and Wales), under half the population (46%) described themselves as Christian for the first time; 37% said "no religion".
  • Secularisation thesis (Steve Bruce, God is Dead, 2002): modernisation steadily erodes religion.
  • Challenges to the thesis: Grace Davie: many people are "believing without belonging" (1994); religion is growing worldwide (Peter Berger changed his mind, 1999); migration brings new, vigorous churches.
  • Northern Ireland has higher religious practice than Britain, but it is also declining.
  • Decline in belonging is clear; decline in belief and spirituality is debated.
Mission 6

Dawkins: the God delusion

  • Richard Dawkins (b. 1941), The God Delusion (2006).
  • Before him, Sigmund Freud (1856–1939, The Future of an Illusion, 1927) called religion an illusion: a belief driven by wishes, such as an infantile longing for a protecting father, though not necessarily false. He also called it a "universal obsessional neurosis".
  • A delusion (stronger than Freud's illusion): a persistent false belief held against strong evidence.
  • God is a scientific hypothesis, and very improbable.
  • Religion causes conflict: faith that cannot be questioned breeds absolutism and violence.
  • Morality does not need God: moral sense has evolutionary roots and changes over time.
Mission 6

Hitchens: "religion poisons everything"

  • Christopher Hitchens (1949–2011), journalist; God Is Not Great: How Religion Poisons Everything (2007).
  • Religion is man-made: it began in humanity's ignorant, fearful childhood.
  • It is harmful: it sanctions violence, divides people and opposes science and medicine.
  • It is totalitarian: a God who watches every thought is a heavenly dictatorship.
  • "What can be asserted without evidence can also be dismissed without evidence."
  • With Dawkins, Sam Harris and Daniel Dennett, he became one of the New Atheists: confrontational, popular, media-savvy (AQA: militant atheism).

Mission 6

Disillusionment or hard-line atheism?

  • Hard-line atheism: active rejection: religion is irrational and harmful and should be challenged (Dawkins, Hitchens).
  • Disillusionment: drifting away from institutions, not necessarily from belief:
  • Scandals, especially abuse and its cover-up (e.g. inquiry reports in Ireland, 2009, and in England and Wales, 2020–22).
  • Teaching that some find outdated (on gender and sexuality); worship that feels irrelevant; perceived hypocrisy.
  • Many of the "nones" are not hostile to religion: some still pray or believe in God; some describe themselves as spiritual but not religious.
Mission 6Evaluate

Would society be happier without Christianity?

  • Claim: Society would be happier without Christianity.
  • For (Freud): religion keeps people infantile and represses natural instincts through guilt.
  • For (Dawkins, Hitchens): religion causes conflict, harms children and blocks rational inquiry.
  • Against: Christianity has inspired hospitals, schools, abolition of the slave trade, civil rights, and much charity today.
  • Against: 20th-century atheist regimes also caused great harm; violence has many causes, not religion alone.
  • Judgement criteria: What evidence counts (history, psychology, surveys)? Is harm caused by religion as such, or by its misuse? Who decides what "happier" means?
Mission check

Secularisation has more than one face: some people argue against God; many more simply drift from the Church.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • New Testament: apostles, gifts, elders = overseers, deacons; by Ignatius (c. 110): one bishop, presbyters, deacons.
  • Irenaeus and Tertullian: succession of bishops guarantees apostolic teaching; Cyprian: the bishop is the church, the episcopate is one.
  • Rome's bishop gained great prestige (Peter and Paul, Irenaeus 3.3.2) but early primacy was contested; churches today keep, adapt or reject the threefold ministry.

Mission 2

  • Gnosticism (secret knowledge, Demiurge, docetism), Marcion (two gods, no Hebrew Bible, first canon) and Montanism (new prophecy, strict discipline, Pepuza).
  • The threefold defence: Rule of Faith, canon of scripture, apostolic succession.
  • Irenaeus (c. 130–c. 202), Against Heresies: one God, good creation, recapitulation, real flesh, public tradition; how reliable he is as a hostile witness is debated (Bauer).

Mission 3

  • Tertullian (c. 155/160–after 220), Carthage: first great Latin theologian.
  • Apologetic (Apology: unjust procedure, slanders, "the blood of Christians is seed"); polemical and doctrinal (Prescription, Against Marcion, Against Praxeas: one substance, three persons; On Baptism); moral (shows, idolatry, military crown, dress, marriage, repentance).
  • From c. 207 a Montanist: stricter discipline from the Paraclete; whether he left the church is debated (Jerome vs Rankin).

Mission 4

  • Cyprian (c. 200/210–258), bishop of Carthage: hid in the Decian persecution, faced the crisis of the lapsed, martyred under Valerian.
  • On the Lapsed: real penance, decided by the bishop; middle way between laxists (Felicissimus) and rigorists (Novatian).
  • On the Unity: one church, one episcopate, "God for his Father … the Church for his mother"; baptism outside the church invalid, against Stephen of Rome.

Mission 5

  • Authority: traditional, charismatic, legal-rational (Weber); church models: episcopal, presbyterian, connexional, congregational, charismatic, Quaker. Challenges: individualism, reason, scandals, demands for participation, disagreement.
  • Secularisation (Wilson, Bruce) vs persistence or change (later Berger, Davie: believing without belonging).
  • Lewis (moral argument, trilemma, imagination), McGrath (science and faith, answer to new atheism), Rahner (openness to God, anonymous Christians, Vatican II).

Mission 6

  • Secularisation is a process (loss of social significance); secularism is a view about public life.
  • Popular atheists (Dawkins, Hitchens) call religion irrational and harmful; many who leave churches are disillusioned rather than atheist.
