Mission 1

Mission 1: Cut every nerve to a heart and it keeps on beating. So what are those nerves for?

Make a guess: Cut every nerve to a heart and it keeps on beating. So what are those nerves for?

Mission 1

The heart starts its own beat

  • Cardiac muscle is myogenic: it contracts without any nerve impulse.
  • The sinoatrial node (SAN), in the wall of the right atrium, is the pacemaker.
  • It sends out a wave of electrical excitation at regular intervals. Each wave starts one heartbeat.
  • Nerves and hormones only change the rate of the SAN. They do not start the beats.
  • Say: "the SAN initiates each beat" — not: "the brain sends each heartbeat".
Mission 1

Spreading the wave

  • (1) The wave from the SAN spreads across both atria → atrial systole.
  • (2) A layer of non-conducting tissue between atria and ventricles blocks it. The only way through is the atrioventricular node (AVN).
  • (3) The AVN delays the wave (about 0.1 s): the atria finish emptying first.
  • (4) The wave passes down the bundle of His in the septum to the apex, then up through the ventricle walls.
  • Ventricles contract from the apex upwards, squeezing blood up into the arteries.
  • OCR names: SAN, AVN, Purkyne tissue.
Quick check
?Suppose the AVN passed the wave of excitation on with no delay. What would be the main effect?
Mission 1

The ECG

  • An electrocardiogram (ECG): electrodes on the skin record the heart's electrical activity.
  • P wave: excitation spreading over the atria → atrial systole.
  • QRS complex: excitation spreading through the ventricles → ventricular systole.
  • T wave: ventricles recovering (repolarising) → diastole.
  • P to the start of QRS includes the AVN delay.
  • Heart rate (beats min⁻¹) = 60 ÷ time between two R peaks (s).
Mission 1

Worked example: heart rate from an ECG

  • ECG paper runs at 25 mm s⁻¹. One large square = 5 mm = 0.20 s.
  • R peak to R peak = 4 large squares = 4 × 0.20 = 0.80 s.
  • Heart rate = 60 ÷ 0.80 = 75 beats min⁻¹.
Mission 1

Abnormal traces

  • Tachycardia: resting rate above 100 beats min⁻¹. R peaks close together.
  • Bradycardia: resting rate below 60 beats min⁻¹. R peaks far apart. Normal in some trained athletes.
  • Fibrillation: uncoordinated, irregular excitation. Atrial: no clear P waves, irregular QRS. Ventricular: no QRS at all; the heart cannot pump.
  • Ectopic heartbeat: an extra beat that comes early, followed by a longer gap.
Quick check
?On an ECG recorded at 25 mm s⁻¹, the distance between two R peaks is 30 mm. What is the heart rate in beats per minute?
Mission 1

Nerves and hormones adjust the rate

  • A centre in the medulla oblongata controls heart rate.
  • Sympathetic nerve to the SAN → SAN fires faster → heart rate up; contractions stronger, so stroke volume up.
  • Parasympathetic nerve (the vagus) to the SAN → SAN fires more slowly → heart rate down.
  • Hormone: adrenaline from the adrenal glands also makes the SAN fire faster.
  • Say: "the medulla changes the rate of the SAN" — not: "the medulla makes the heart beat".
Mission 1

What the medulla senses

  • Baroreceptors (pressure receptors): stretch receptors in the walls of the aorta and carotid arteries. They monitor blood pressure.
  • Chemoreceptors: in the aorta and carotid arteries, and in the medulla. They monitor blood pH, CO2 and O2.
  • Blood pressure high → more baroreceptor impulses → medulla → more vagus impulses → rate falls.
  • Blood pressure low → fewer baroreceptor impulses → medulla → more sympathetic impulses → rate rises.
  • CO2 up, pH down (e.g. exercise) → chemoreceptors → medulla → sympathetic → rate rises → CO2 removed faster.
  • Each response reverses the change: negative feedback.
Quick check
?A person stands up quickly and their blood pressure falls for a few seconds. Which sequence describes the feedback response that restores blood pressure?
Mission 1Practical

Caffeine and Daphnia heart rate

  • Safe and ethical use of organisms to measure a physiological function.
  • Place one Daphnia in a drop of pond water on a cavity slide; low power; let it settle for a few minutes.
  • Count heartbeats for 15 s with a tally counter; repeat 3 times; × 4 for beats min⁻¹.
  • Replace the water with a caffeine solution; settle; count again. Repeat for a range of concentrations.
  • Control: temperature (a lamp heats the slide), settling time, size of Daphnia.
  • Ethics, arguments for: invertebrates, a simple nervous system, brief use, returned to pond water. Arguments against: whether they feel pain is uncertain; caffeine may harm them; a human pulse study could replace them.
  • With human volunteers: informed consent, right to withdraw, no one put at risk.
Mission check

?In a heart transplant, the nerves linking the donor heart to the medulla are cut, and they do not regrow for many months. Which statement about the transplanted heart is correct?

The heart makes its own beat; the nerves are the volume control, turning the rate up or down to match what the body needs.

Mission 2

Why sample, and why at random

  • Counting every individual is usually impossible: too slow, organisms hidden or moving, the habitat trampled.
  • So count a sample and scale up.
  • Random sampling: every point has an equal chance of being chosen. This avoids bias.
  • The estimate still differs from the true size. That difference is sampling error. More samples reduce it; only a full count removes it.
  • Non-random sampling: systematic (regular intervals, e.g. along a transect), stratified (sample each zone in proportion to its area), opportunistic (where organisms are easy to find; quick, but biased).
  • Say: "random placement avoids bias" — not: "choose typical spots".
Mission 2

Random quadrats

  • For sessile or slow-moving organisms: plants, limpets, barnacles.
  • Lay two tape measures at right angles. Use random numbers as coordinates for each quadrat.
  • Measures of abundance:
    • Density: number of individuals per m².
    • Frequency: % of quadrats in which the species occurs.
    • Percentage cover: % of the quadrat area it covers. Use where individuals can't be counted, e.g. grass.
Mission 2

Worked example: from quadrats to a population

  • Plantain plants in a 1200 m² field. Ten random 0.5 m × 0.5 m quadrats.
  • Counts: 3, 0, 5, 2, 4, 1, 6, 3, 2, 4. Total 30.
  • Mean per quadrat = 30 ÷ 10 = 3.0.
  • Quadrat area = 0.5 × 0.5 = 0.25 m². Density = 3.0 ÷ 0.25 = 12 per m².
  • Estimate = 12 × 1200 = 14 400 plants (14 000 to 2 significant figures).
Quick check
?A student places ten 0.5 m × 0.5 m quadrats at random in a 250 m² meadow and counts 20 orchids in total. What is the estimated population of orchids in the meadow?
Mission 2

Transects: sampling along a change

  • Use a transect where conditions change across the site: shore, woodland edge, sand dune, path.
  • Line transect: record every species touching a line at regular intervals.
  • Belt transect: place quadrats along the line, continuously or at regular intervals (interrupted).
  • At each quadrat, measure the abiotic factor: light intensity, soil moisture, soil pH, salinity, temperature. Sensors and data loggers help.
  • Pair each abundance value with its abiotic value. Then look for a correlation.
  • A correlation is not proof of cause. Up a shore, several factors change together.
Mission 2Practical

Distribution and an abiotic factor

  • PAG3: sampling techniques, random and non-random, in the field.
  • Method: lay a transect across the gradient; quadrat at fixed intervals; record % cover or density; measure the factor at each quadrat.
  • Control: same quadrat size, same observer, same time of day; repeat transects.
  • Analysis: plot abundance against the factor; test the correlation (e.g. Spearman's rank; choosing tests in another lesson).

Mission 2

Catching small animals

  • Pitfall trap: a cup sunk level with the soil. Ground-running invertebrates fall in.
  • Sweep net: swept through long grass to catch insects on the vegetation.
  • Pooter: suck small insects into a tube without harming them.
  • Tullgren funnel: a lamp dries soil or leaf litter; invertebrates move down and fall into a container.
  • Kick-sampling: disturb a stream bed upstream of a net; the current carries invertebrates in.
  • Trap sizes, times and numbers must be the same at each site to compare.
Mission check

?A student estimates the number of dandelions on a school field. She places ten quadrats in the spots where she can see dandelions growing, counts carefully, and scales up by the area of the field. Which statement about her estimate is correct?

Count a random sample and scale up: the snails in a wood, or dandelions on a field. Choose the spots yourself and the estimate is biased.

Mission 3

Three levels of biodiversity

  • Biodiversity: the variety of life, at three levels.
  • Ecosystem (habitat) diversity: the range of habitats or ecosystems, e.g. sand dune, woodland, meadow, stream.
  • Species diversity: the number of species and how evenly the individuals are spread among them.
  • Genetic diversity: the variety of alleles within a species.
Quick check
?A survey of an island records three findings: the island contains six distinct ecosystems; 420 species have been identified; and its population of one lizard species carries 18 alleles at a studied gene, compared with 4 alleles in the same species on a neighbouring island. Which of these findings describe the island's biodiversity?
Mission 3

Richness and evenness

Illustrative data: two meadows, same four species, 40 plants each.

SpeciesMeadow AMeadow B
Buttercup1031
Clover103
Daisy103
Plantain103
  • Species richness: the number of species. Both meadows: 4.
  • Species evenness: how evenly individuals are spread among the species. A is even; B is dominated by buttercup.
  • Species diversity needs both. Meadow A is more diverse.
  • Say: "diversity depends on richness and evenness" — not: "diversity is the number of species".
Mission 3

Simpson's Index of Diversity

  • Simpson's Index of Diversity: D = 1 − Σ(n/N)². The formula is given in the exam.
  • N = total number of organisms of all species; n = number of organisms of each species.
  • Values from 0 to just under 1. Closer to 1 = more diverse; near 0 = one species dominates.
  • Meadow A: 0.75. Meadow B: 0.38.
  • High D: many species, evenly spread; such a habitat is usually more stable. Low D: dominated by a few species; a change hits it harder.
Mission 3

Worked example

Illustrative data: pitfall traps in a wood. Species P 12, Q 6, R 2.

  • N = 12 + 6 + 2 = 20.
  • n/N: 0.60, 0.30, 0.10.
  • Square each: 0.36, 0.09, 0.01. Sum: Σ(n/N)² = 0.46.
  • D = 1 − 0.46 = 0.54.
  • Square before you add, and do not stop at 0.46.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A rock pool holds limpets 8, barnacles 6, periwinkles 4 and sea anemones 2. Using D = 1 − Σ(n/N)², what is Simpson's Index of Diversity?
Mission 3

Sampling for an index

  • You cannot count every organism. Take samples that represent the habitat.
  • Random sampling: random-number coordinates on a grid; quadrats of equal size. Avoids bias.
  • Take many samples. One quadrat misses species and shows nothing about variation.
  • Non-random sampling: systematic (at regular intervals, e.g. along a transect across a gradient); stratified (sample each zone in proportion to its area); opportunistic (where organisms happen to be: quick, but biased).
  • Compare two sites only with the same method and effort.
Mission 3

Practical: collecting samples

  • PAG3: collect random and non-random samples in the field.
  • Sweep net: swept through long grass; catches flying and plant-dwelling insects.
  • Pitfall trap: a cup sunk level with the soil; catches ground-dwelling invertebrates.
  • Pooter: suck small insects from a net or tray into a tube.
  • Tullgren funnel: lamp heat and light drive soil and leaf-litter invertebrates down into a collecting tube.
  • Kick-sampling: disturb a stream bed for a set time; the current carries invertebrates into a net held downstream.
  • Calculation: the index of diversity for each site, then compare.
Quick check
?Ecologists want to compare the species diversity of the plant communities in two meadows by calculating Simpson's Diversity Index for each. Which procedure is most appropriate?
Mission check

?Illustrative data. Two hedgerows were sampled for insects with the same method and effort. Each had the same 6 species and 120 insects in total. In hedgerow X, one species made up 100 of the 120 insects. In hedgerow Y, each species had 20 insects. Which statement is correct?

Counting species is only half the story. How evenly the individuals are shared out decides which habitat is more diverse.

Mission 4

Genetic diversity

  • Genetic diversity: the variety of alleles within a species or population.
  • Every member of a species has the same genes. They differ in which alleles they carry.
  • Example: different breeds within one species, such as dog breeds.
  • Small, isolated or captive populations often have low genetic diversity.
  • Say: "different alleles of the same genes" — not: "different genes".
Quick check
?Cheetahs (Acinonyx jubatus) show extremely little genetic variation: unrelated cheetahs are so alike that skin grafts between them are not rejected. In a reserve, cheetah numbers have been stable for 20 years, and the species present and their relative abundances have not changed. Which statement about biodiversity in the reserve is correct?
Mission 4

Proportion of polymorphic loci

  • A locus is the position of a gene on a chromosome.
  • Polymorphic locus: two or more alleles are present in the population. Monomorphic: only one.
  • Proportion of polymorphic gene loci = number of polymorphic loci ÷ total number of loci
  • Illustrative example: a captive population; 30 loci tested; 9 polymorphic.
  • 9 ÷ 30 = 0.30 (30%).
  • Higher proportion = more genetic diversity. Useful for zoos, rare breeds and pedigree animals.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. In a rare breed of sheep, 40 gene loci were tested. 6 were polymorphic and 34 were monomorphic. What is the proportion of polymorphic gene loci?
Mission 4

Why genetic diversity matters

  • Environments change: new diseases, new climates, new chemicals.
  • High genetic diversity: some individuals are more likely to carry alleles that let them survive. The population persists.
  • Low genetic diversity: if one is susceptible, nearly all are. The population can crash.
  • Example: California condors fell to 22 birds in 1982. All living condors descend from the 27 taken into captivity by 1987.
  • Say: "the population is more likely to contain resistant individuals" — not: "each individual is more resistant".
Quick checkRead the data
?Real data (Zhu et al. 2000). In Yunnan, China, farmers planted rows of disease-susceptible rice varieties among disease-resistant varieties in every rice field of 5 townships (1998) and 10 townships (1999). Plots of the susceptible varieties grown alone (monoculture) were the comparison.
Which conclusion is best supported?
  • Rice blast (a fungal disease) on the susceptible varieties: 94% less severe in mixtures.
  • Yield of the susceptible varieties: 89% greater in mixtures.
Mission check

?Almost all bananas in international trade are one variety, Cavendish. Every plant is grown from a piece of another plant, so all are genetically identical. A fungal wilt disease that kills Cavendish plants is spreading between plantations. Why are biologists concerned for the whole crop?

Numbers do not protect a population; variety does. Genetic diversity is a population's insurance against change.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Mendel's peas came out at almost three to one, never exactly. How close is close enough?

Make a guess: Mendel's peas came out at almost three to one, never exactly. How close is close enough?

Mission 5

Chance or a real difference?

  • Which gametes fuse is random. So observed counts scatter around the expected ones.
  • The χ² test asks: is the difference small enough to be due to chance?
  • Null hypothesis: there is no significant difference between observed and expected results.
  • The test uses counts in categories (phenotype classes). Never percentages or ratios.
  • The counted offspring are a sample of all the offspring the cross could produce.
Mission 5

The formula

  • χ² = Σ (O − E)² ÷ E. O = observed, E = expected, Σ = sum over all classes.
  • The formula is provided in the exam.
  • E = total counted × expected fraction for that class.
  • Degrees of freedom = number of classes − 1.
  • Big differences → big χ². A perfect fit → χ² = 0.
Mission 5

Worked example: Mendel's seed shape

F2 of round × wrinkled peas (Mendel, 1866). Null hypothesis: the data fit 3 round : 1 wrinkled.

Critical value and decision

  • Find the critical value for your degrees of freedom at p = 0.05.
  • χ² below it: the difference is not significant. A difference this large would arise by chance more than 5% of the time.
  • So accept the null hypothesis: the difference is due to chance.
  • χ² above it: a significant difference. Reject the null hypothesis.
  • Mendel: 0.26 < 3.84. His data fit 3 : 1.
  • Say: "the data are consistent with 3 : 1" — not: "the data prove 3 : 1".

Quick check
?A cross between two double heterozygotes gives 160 F2 offspring: 95 show both dominant traits, 28 show the first dominant and the second recessive, 30 show the first recessive and the second dominant, and 7 show both recessive traits (illustrative data). The critical value of chi-squared at p = 0.05 is 7.815 for 3 degrees of freedom and 9.488 for 4 degrees of freedom. What is the outcome of a chi-squared test against a 9:3:3:1 ratio?
Mission 5

When the null hypothesis is rejected

  • A significant difference means the expected ratio is probably wrong for this cross.
  • Then look for a biological reason:
    • two genes linked (too many parental types);
    • genes interacting (epistasis);
    • a sex-linked gene;
    • unequal survival of one phenotype.
  • χ² tells you whether the difference is significant, not why.
Mission 5

Common slips

  • Use raw counts, not percentages.
  • Scale E to your total. Never use 9, 3, 3, 1 as E.
  • Degrees of freedom = classes − 1.
  • Big χ² = big difference. Above the critical value → significant.
  • Not significant ≠ proved.
Mission check

?In tomatoes, purple stems (A) are dominant to green stems (a). An Aa plant is crossed with an aa plant. Of 200 seedlings, 115 are purple and 85 are green (illustrative data). Critical values at p = 0.05: 3.84 for 1 degree of freedom, 5.99 for 2. Which conclusion is correct?

Unlike Mendel's seed counts, this gap is too large to be chance. Next step: look for a reason, such as fewer green seedlings surviving.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Cardiac muscle is myogenic: the SAN starts each beat; the AVN delays the wave; the bundle of His and Purkyne tissue carry it to the apex.
  • Baroreceptors and chemoreceptors → medulla → sympathetic (faster) or vagus (slower) nerves to the SAN.
  • ECG: P (atria), QRS (ventricles), T (recovery); rate = 60 ÷ R–R time.

Mission 2

  • Sample at random and scale up: quadrats for sessile organisms, transects where conditions change.

Mission 3

  • Species diversity = richness (number of species) + evenness (how individuals are spread).
  • 1 − Σ(n/N)²: closer to 1 = more diverse.

Mission 4

  • Genetic diversity is the variety of alleles in a population; high diversity makes it more likely that some individuals survive a change.
  • Proportion of polymorphic loci = polymorphic loci ÷ total loci.

Mission 5

  • χ² = Σ (O − E)²/E with degrees of freedom = classes − 1; compare with the critical value at p = 0.05.
  • Below it: accept the null hypothesis (difference due to chance). Above it: reject it (significant difference).
