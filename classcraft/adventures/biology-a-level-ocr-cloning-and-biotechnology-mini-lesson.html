Mission 1

Mission 1: Why would a factory lock its enzymes in place when free enzymes work faster?

Make a guess: Why would a factory lock its enzymes in place when free enzymes work faster?

Mission 1

Enzymes held in place

  • An immobilised enzyme is held on, or inside, an insoluble support.
  • The substrate solution flows past. Product flows out.
  • The enzyme stays behind, ready for the next batch.
  • It still works: substrate molecules move at random and collide with the fixed active site.
  • Say: "substrate molecules collide with the immobilised enzyme" — not: "a fixed enzyme cannot meet its substrate".
Mission 1

Four ways to immobilise

  • Adsorption: enzyme sticks to the surface of a support (e.g. clay, glass beads, porous carbon). Simple, but the enzyme can leak off.
  • Covalent bonding: enzyme bonded to a support (e.g. cellulose fibres). Little leakage; some active sites may be distorted.
  • Entrapment: enzyme trapped inside a gel bead (e.g. calcium alginate) or a fibre mesh. Substrate must diffuse in.
  • Membrane separation: enzyme kept on one side of a partially permeable membrane. Small substrate and product molecules pass through.
Quick check
?Lactase is mixed with sodium alginate solution. Drops of the mixture fall into calcium chloride solution and set as small beads with the enzyme inside. Which method of immobilisation is this?
Mission 1

Why industry immobilises

  • Reuse: the same enzyme works for many batches, or for weeks in a column. Enzymes are costly, so this cuts costs.
  • Enzyme-free product: no step is needed to remove the enzyme from the product.
  • Improved stability: held in shape by the support, the enzyme is less easily denatured. It works over a wider range of temperature and pH.
  • Continuous flow: substrate is pumped through a column of enzyme without stopping.
  • Higher temperatures become usable, so reactions run faster.
Mission 1

The trade-off

  • Reduced activity: some active sites face the support or sit deep inside a bead. Substrate cannot reach them.
  • Substrate must diffuse into the gel, so each run is slower.
  • Set-up cost is higher; adsorbed enzyme may leak off.
  • Still cost-effective when the enzyme is used many times.
  • Evaluate by weighing these: one long-lived column against a fresh dose of free enzyme for every batch.

Mission 1Practical

Lactase in alginate beads

  • PAG4 (rates of enzyme-controlled reactions), applied to an immobilised enzyme.
  • Mix lactase with sodium alginate; drip into calcium chloride solution to set beads. Rinse.
  • Pack the beads into a syringe barrel with a tap. Run lactose solution (or milk) through.
  • Test the liquid that drips out with a glucose test strip.
  • Control: beads made without lactase. No glucose should appear.
  • Variable to test: flow rate (tap setting). Keep temperature and bead volume the same.
  • Calculation: % lactose hydrolysed = (glucose out ÷ lactose in) × 100. Example: 35 ÷ 50 mmol dm⁻³ → 70 %.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Can one cell from an adult animal's body be used to make a whole new animal?

Make a guess: Can one cell from an adult animal's body be used to make a whole new animal?

Mission 2

Clones

  • A clone: genetically identical to its parent, or to its siblings.
  • Made by mitosis alone. No meiosis, no fertilisation.
  • Natural in many plants and some animals.
  • Differences between clones come from the environment (and rare mutations).
Mission 2

Natural plant clones

  • Vegetative propagation: a new plant grows from part of the parent.
  • Runners: strawberry. Rhizomes: couch grass. Tubers: potato.
  • Bulbs: daffodil, onion. Suckers: new shoots from roots, as in elm.
  • Growers exploit this: split bulbs, pot up runners, separate suckers.
  • Result: a uniform crop, true to the parent variety.
Mission 2Practical

Taking cuttings

  • PAG2: dissecting plant material to produce cuttings.
  • Cut a healthy, non-flowering shoot about 10 cm long. Cut just below a node, at a slant.
  • Remove the lower leaves. This cuts water loss by transpiration.
  • Dip the cut end in rooting powder (contains auxin). Plant in moist compost.
  • Cover with a clear plastic bag to keep the air humid.
  • Variable to test: with and without rooting powder. Count rooted cuttings after 3–4 weeks; give % rooted.
Quick check
?A gardener wants new plants that are clones of a prize plant. Which method gives clones?
Mission 2

Micropropagation

  • Tissue culture on a large scale.
  • (1) Cut a small explant, often from a shoot tip (meristem). Sterilise its surface.
  • (2) Place it on sterile agar with nutrients and plant hormones (auxins and cytokinins). Cells divide to form a callus: a mass of undifferentiated cells.
  • (3) Divide the callus. Move pieces to new media: more cytokinin → shoots; more auxin → roots.
  • (4) Plantlets are hardened off in a greenhouse, then planted out.
  • Aseptic technique throughout: one fungal spore can ruin a batch.

Mission 2

Natural animal clones

  • Identical (monozygotic) twins: one zygote divides; the early embryo splits in two.
  • Each half develops into a full individual. Same genotype.
  • Possible because early embryo cells are totipotent: not yet specialised.
  • Nine-banded armadillos usually give birth to identical quadruplets from one embryo.
Mission 2

Artificial embryo twinning

  • (1) An egg from a high-value cow is fertilised (in the cow, or by IVF).
  • (2) The early embryo, cells still totipotent, is split into several parts.
  • (3) Each part grows into an embryo in the lab.
  • (4) Each embryo is implanted into a surrogate mother.
  • The offspring are clones of each other, not of either parent.
Mission 2

Somatic cell nuclear transfer (SCNT)

  • (1) Take a somatic (body) cell from adult A, the animal to be cloned.
  • (2) Take an egg cell from female B. Enucleate it: remove its nucleus.
  • (3) Fuse A's cell with the empty egg using a small electric pulse. The pulse also starts division.
  • (4) The embryo is implanted into surrogate C.
  • The offspring's nuclear DNA is A's. Its mitochondrial DNA comes from B's egg.
  • Dolly the sheep (1996): the first mammal cloned from an adult cell, a mammary gland cell.
Quick check
?A clone is made by SCNT. The nucleus comes from a skin cell of ram A. The egg comes from ewe B. The embryo grows in surrogate ewe C. Where do the clone's nuclear genes come from?

Mission check

?In 2020 a Przewalski's horse foal, Kurt, was born by SCNT. The nucleus came from skin cells of a Przewalski's stallion, frozen in 1980. The egg and the surrogate were domestic horses. Which statement is correct?

Yes. An adult body cell keeps every gene, so its nucleus, put into an egg, can build a whole new animal.

Mission 3: How can a tank of tiny living cells make medicines, cheese and even clean up oil spills?

Make a guess: How can a tank of tiny living cells make medicines, cheese and even clean up oil spills?

Mission 3

Why microorganisms?

  • Short life cycle: bacteria can divide every 20–30 minutes in good conditions.
  • Simple needs: many grow on cheap nutrients, even waste.
  • Grow at lower temperatures than many chemical processes: less energy.
  • Grown all year, whatever the climate; little land needed.
  • Easy to genetically modify to make new products.
  • Products are often purer than chemical routes, with fewer ethical issues than using animals.

Mission 3

GM bacteria make human proteins

  • A human gene is inserted into bacteria. (How.)
  • The genetic code is universal: bacterial ribosomes read the human gene and make the human protein.
  • The gene goes in without introns: bacteria cannot remove them.
  • Products from GM microorganisms include:
  • Human insulin for diabetes (first approved 1982).
  • Human growth hormone.
  • Chymosin (rennet) for cheese, so no calf stomachs are needed.
Quick check
?GM bacteria carrying the human insulin gene make insulin identical to human insulin. Why?
Mission 3

Growth in a closed culture

  • Closed culture: nothing added, nothing removed after the start.
  • Lag: cells adapt, make enzymes; little division.
  • Log (exponential): no limiting factors; numbers double each generation.
  • Stationary: nutrients run low, toxic waste builds up; births = deaths.
  • Death (decline): deaths exceed births.
  • Plot numbers on a log scale: the log phase becomes a straight line.
  • Say: "numbers double each generation" — not: "the same number of cells is added each time".
Mission 3

Worked example: counting doublings

  • N = N0 × 2ⁿ. N0 = starting number; n = number of generations (doublings).
  • A culture starts with 500 bacteria. They divide every 20 minutes. How many after 3 hours of log phase?
  • Step 1: n = 180 min ÷ 20 min = 9 generations.
  • Step 2: 2⁹ = 512.
  • Step 3: N = 500 × 512 = 256 000 (2.56 × 10⁵).
  • Time must be in the same units as the generation time.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Immobilised enzymes are held on or inside an insoluble support: reused, stable, and leave the product enzyme-free.
  • The price: lower activity, as some active sites are hidden and substrate must diffuse in; worth it when the enzyme is used many times.

Mission 2

  • Clones form by mitosis alone: natural (runners, bulbs, identical twins) or artificial (cuttings, micropropagation, embryo twinning, SCNT).
  • SCNT puts an adult cell's nucleus into an enucleated egg: the clone shares the donor's nuclear DNA. Evaluate cloning by weighing uniformity and speed against lost variation, cost and low success.

Mission 3

  • Microorganisms grow fast on cheap nutrients: food, drugs and bioremediation. Closed cultures follow lag, log, stationary and death phases; N = N0 × 2ⁿ. Batch suits secondary metabolites; continuous holds log phase.
  • GM bacteria make human proteins (insulin, growth hormone, chymosin) because the genetic code is universal.
