Mission 1

Lipids are more than fat

  • Lipids dissolve in non-polar solvents but only sparingly in water: they are hydrophobic.
  • Lipids include fats, oils, waxes (waterproof coatings, such as a leaf's cuticle), phospholipids and steroids.
  • Not every lipid is an energy store.
Mission 1

Building a phospholipid

  • Start with a triglyceride: glycerol + three fatty acids.
  • Swap one fatty acid for a phosphate-containing group.
  • So: glycerol + two fatty acids + phosphate group.
  • Each link forms by condensation, releasing one water molecule: three in all.
  • Result: a head (phosphate) and two tails (fatty acids).
Mission 1

Amphipathic: two personalities

  • Head: phosphate group, charged. It is hydrophilic: it interacts with water.
  • Tails: hydrocarbon chains, non-polar. They are hydrophobic.
  • One molecule with a hydrophilic and a hydrophobic region is amphipathic.
  • Say: "the tails are excluded from water" — not: "the tails repel water".
Quick check
?Which components are linked together by condensation reactions to form one phospholipid molecule?
Mission 1

The bilayer builds itself

  • (1) On water, phospholipids form a single layer: heads in the water, tails in the air.
  • (2) Under water, tails cannot stay in the air. They hide by pairing up tail to tail.
  • (3) Result: a phospholipid bilayer. Heads face the water on both sides; tails form a non-polar core.
  • No enzymes, no energy from the cell: water molecules hydrogen-bond to each other and exclude the tails.
  • A sheet's edges still expose tails, so it closes into a sphere.
  • The molecules are not covalently bonded to each other, so the bilayer is fluid.
Quick check
?Phospholipids are shaken with water in a test tube, with no cells or enzymes present. What happens, and why?
Mission 1

Steroids and cholesterol

  • Steroids: four fused rings of carbon atoms, three with six atoms and one with five. Spot this shape in any diagram.
  • Mostly C and H, so non-polar: steroids dissolve in the bilayer's core and diffuse straight through.
  • Examples: the hormones oestradiol and testosterone. Both enter cells and bind receptors inside.
  • Cholesterol is a steroid lipid.
Quick check
?Testosterone binds to receptors inside its target cells. How does it get through the plasma membrane to reach them?
Mission 1

The first compartments

  • A fatty acid is also amphipathic: a carboxyl head, one hydrocarbon tail.
  • In water at a suitable pH, fatty acids coalesce into bilayers that close into spheres: vesicles.
  • This is self-assembly. No enzymes, no cell.
  • A vesicle encloses water: a compartment.
  • Inside, molecules can stay concentrated, so the chemistry inside can become different from outside.
  • That was a necessary step towards the first cells.
Quick check

Mission check

One rule builds membranes, vesicles and lipoproteins: heads to the water, tails away from it.

Mission 2

Cell theory

  • Cell theory: all living organisms are made of one or more cells.
  • The cell is the smallest unit that carries out all the processes of life.
  • New cells arise only from existing cells, by division.
  • Viruses are not cells and are not classed as living, so they are not exceptions.
  • The theory grew from many observations (induction). Using it to predict that a new organism will be made of cells is deduction.
Quick check

Mission 2

Units for tiny things

  • 1 mm = 1000 µm (micrometres).
  • 1 µm = 1000 nm (nanometres).
  • mm → µm: × 1000. µm → nm: × 1000.
  • Whole cells: µm. Organelle detail, viruses, molecules: nm.
  • A red blood cell is about 7.5 µm across; a ribosome about 25 nm.
  • Say: "1 mm = 1000 µm" — not: "1 mm = 100 µm".
  • A micrometre is one-millionth of a metre, not of a millimetre.
Mission 2

The magnification formula

  • magnification = image size ÷ actual size
  • Rearrange: actual size = image size ÷ magnification.
  • Rearrange: image size = actual size × magnification.
  • Put both sizes in the same unit first.
  • Magnification has no unit: write ×2000.
  • Check: the actual size must be smaller than the image. If yours is bigger, you multiplied.
Mission 2

Worked example: size and magnification

Actual size of a chloroplast in a micrograph at ×4000. The image is 24 mm long.

  • Convert: 24 mm = 24 000 µm.
  • Actual size = 24 000 ÷ 4000 = 6 µm.

Magnification of a mitochondrion drawn 45 mm long. Its actual length is 1.5 µm.

  • Convert: 45 mm = 45 000 µm.
  • Magnification = 45 000 ÷ 1.5 = ×30 000.
Quick check
?In a photomicrograph taken at a magnification of ×2000, a red blood cell measures 15 mm across. What is the actual width of the cell?
Mission 2

Scale bars

  • A scale bar is a line on an image labelled with the actual length it represents.
  • Magnification = measured bar length ÷ bar label (same units).
  • Example: a bar 20 mm long labelled 5 µm. 20 000 µm ÷ 5 µm = ×4000.
  • Then use the formula for anything else in that image.
  • To draw a bar: drawn length = label × magnification.
  • Say: "measure the bar with a ruler" — not: "the label is the bar's length".
  • Add a scale bar to your own drawings and photographs.
Mission 2

Practical: calibrating an eyepiece graticule

  • Eyepiece graticule: a scale in the eyepiece. Its divisions have no fixed size.
  • Stage micrometer: a slide with a real scale, often 1 mm in 100 divisions of 10 µm.
  • Line up the two scales. Example at the ×10 objective: 25 graticule divisions = 250 µm. So 1 division = 10 µm.
  • Change the objective, recalibrate. At ×40: 10 divisions = 25 µm. So 1 division = 2.5 µm.
  • Measure: a cell spans 18 divisions at ×40. 18 × 2.5 µm = 45 µm.
  • Skill: measure sizes with an eyepiece graticule.
Quick check
?With the ×40 objective lens in place, 40 divisions of an eyepiece graticule line up exactly with 100 µm on a stage micrometer. Using the same objective, a cell spans 44 graticule divisions. What is the actual length of the cell in millimetres?
Mission 2

Making and viewing a slide

  • Temporary mount: a thin layer of cells (e.g. onion epidermis) in a drop of water or stain; lower a coverslip at an angle to avoid air bubbles.
  • Stains add contrast, not detail. Iodine stains starch blue-black; methylene blue stains cells blue, nuclei most strongly.
  • Focus at low power first, with the coarse then the fine adjustment.
  • Switch to high power. Refocus with the fine adjustment only.
  • Say: "the stain increases contrast" — not: "the stain makes the image sharper".
  • Photograph the image through the eyepiece or a camera; record the magnification or add a scale bar.
Mission check

?A micrograph shows a pollen grain 60 mm across. A scale bar on the micrograph is 20 mm long and is labelled 10 µm. What is the actual diameter of the pollen grain?

You never measure the cell directly. You measure the image, then use a calibrated scale (graticule or scale bar) to convert it.

Mission 3

Magnification is not resolution

  • Magnification: how many times larger the image is than the object.
  • Resolution: the smallest distance between two points that can still be seen as separate.
  • Resolution is limited by the wavelength used. Visible light (about 400–700 nm) gives a limit of about 200 nm.
  • Electrons have a far shorter wavelength, so electron microscopes resolve much finer detail.
  • Enlarging past the resolution limit gives a bigger blur, not more detail.
  • Say: "higher resolution shows more detail" — not: "higher magnification shows more detail".
Quick check
?Ribosomes are about 25 nm across. They cannot be seen with a light microscope but are clearly visible in electron micrographs. What is the reason?
Mission 3

Three microscopes, three ways to make an image

  • Light (optical) microscope: light passes through a thin specimen; glass lenses focus it.
  • Transmission electron microscope (TEM): a beam of electrons passes through a very thin section. Electromagnets focus it.
  • In a TEM, parts stained with heavy metals scatter electrons and look dark. The image is a flat slice of internal structure.
  • Scanning electron microscope (SEM): an electron beam is scanned across the surface of a specimen coated in metal.
  • In an SEM, electrons given off or scattered back from the surface build a 3D image of the surface only.

Mission 3

Reading micrographs

  • Light micrograph (photomicrograph): coloured by stains; whole cells and tissues. Only structures larger than about 0.2 µm, such as nuclei and chloroplasts.
  • TEM micrograph: a flat slice. Shows membranes inside organelles, e.g. the folds inside a mitochondrion.
  • SEM micrograph: a 3D-looking surface with shadows; no internal detail.
  • First find the scale bar or the magnification.
  • Next missions: naming the organelles you see.
Mission 3

Developments in microscopy

  • Electron microscopy: far higher resolution than light, revealing cell ultrastructure.
  • Freeze fracture: a cell is frozen and split. The fracture runs between the two layers of a membrane; a metal replica shows proteins as bumps within the bilayer.
  • Cryogenic electron microscopy (cryo-EM): samples frozen so fast that no ice crystals form; no fixing or staining. Combining images of many molecules gives 3D protein structures in near-atomic detail.
  • Fluorescent stains: bind particular substances (e.g. DNA) and glow when lit with light of a set wavelength.
  • Immunofluorescence: antibodies that bind one specific protein carry a fluorescent marker, showing where that protein is.
Quick check
?A researcher wants to see where one particular protein is located inside cultured cells. Which approach will show this?
Mission check

Enlarging an image never adds detail. Detail is set by resolution, and resolution is set by the wavelength used.

Mission 4

What is an organelle?

  • An organelle is a distinct part of a cell with a specific function.
  • Eukaryotic cells have a nucleus and other membrane-bound organelles.
  • The cell surface (plasma) membrane controls what enters and leaves the cell.
  • Internal membranes divide the cell into compartments, each with its own enzymes and reactions.
  • Organelles include the nucleus, vesicles, ribosomes and the plasma membrane. The cell wall, cytoskeleton and cytoplasm are not organelles.
  • Every cell has DNA, a cytoplasm that is mainly water (the solvent for metabolism) and a lipid membrane (a barrier to water-soluble substances).
Quick check
?Which statement correctly applies the term organelle?
Mission 4

The nucleus

  • Nuclear envelope: a double membrane with nuclear pores. mRNA leaves through the pores; proteins enter.
  • Chromatin: DNA wound around histone proteins. It condenses into visible chromosomes when the cell divides.
  • Nucleolus: a dense region that makes ribosomal RNA and assembles ribosomes.
  • The nucleus holds the cell's genes and controls which proteins are made.
Mission 4

Ribosomes

  • Made of ribosomal RNA (rRNA) and protein; no membrane.
  • Two subunits. They read mRNA and join amino acids: protein synthesis.
  • Free in the cytoplasm, or bound to the RER.
  • Eukaryotic cytoplasm: 80S ribosomes. Prokaryotes, mitochondria and chloroplasts: smaller 70S.
  • Say: "ribosomes translate mRNA into protein" — not: "ribosomes copy DNA".
Mission 4

The endoplasmic reticulum

  • Rough ER (RER): flattened membrane sacs studded with ribosomes. Makes and transports proteins, e.g. proteins for secretion.
  • Smooth ER (SER): a network of tubes with no ribosomes. Makes lipids.
Mission 4

Golgi apparatus, vesicles and lysosomes

  • Golgi apparatus: a stack of curved, flattened membrane sacs; no ribosomes.
  • It modifies proteins (e.g. adds carbohydrate) and packages them into vesicles.
  • Vesicles: small membrane sacs that carry substances around the cell.
  • Lysosome: a vesicle with a single membrane, full of hydrolytic enzymes. It digests engulfed material and worn-out organelles.
  • The lysosome membrane keeps the enzymes apart from the cytoplasm they would digest.
  • Compartments keep enzymes and their substrates concentrated together, and keep incompatible processes apart.
  • Lysosomes fuse with phagocytic vacuoles: engulfed bacteria are digested without harming the cell.
Mission 4

Mitochondria

  • Double membrane. The inner membrane folds into cristae; the fluid inside is the matrix.
  • Site of aerobic respiration, which makes ATP.
  • Cristae give a large surface area for the membrane-bound steps that make ATP.
  • Mitochondria contain their own DNA and 70S ribosomes.
  • Say: "mitochondria transfer energy from glucose to ATP" — not: "mitochondria make energy".
Quick check
?Which statement correctly describes a membrane-bound organelle of a eukaryotic cell?
Mission 4

Centrioles, cilia and flagella

  • Centrioles: a pair of short, hollow cylinders of microtubules, at right angles, near the nucleus of animal cells.
  • They help organise the spindle when an animal cell divides.
  • Microtubules belong to the cytoskeleton, a network of protein fibres through the cytoplasm (roles in a later lesson).
  • The cytoskeleton is made of microtubules and microfilaments.

Mission 5

The cell wall

  • A cell wall lies outside the plasma membrane.
  • Plant walls are made of cellulose. Fungal walls are made of chitin.
  • The wall gives strength and shape, and stops the cell bursting when water enters by osmosis.
  • Water and most small solutes pass through the wall. The plasma membrane controls what enters the cell.
  • Say: "the membrane controls entry" — not: "the wall controls entry".
Mission 5

Chloroplasts

  • Found in plants and algae: the site of photosynthesis.
  • Double membrane (envelope).
  • Thylakoids: flattened membrane sacs holding chlorophyll, stacked into grana.
  • Stroma: the fluid around the thylakoids, with the enzymes that make sugars.
  • Chloroplasts contain their own DNA and 70S ribosomes.
  • Not every plant cell has them: root cells, for example, do not.
  • Say: "plant cells have chloroplasts and mitochondria" — not: "chloroplasts instead of mitochondria".
Mission 5

The vacuole

  • Plant cells have a large, permanent vacuole filled with cell sap.
  • Its membrane is the tonoplast.
  • Water enters the vacuole; the contents press against the wall: the cell is turgid (firm).
  • Animal cells have vacuoles too: smaller, more plentiful and often temporary; they store materials.
  • In IB terms this is a sap vacuole.

Mission 5

Atypical cells: counting nuclei

  • Most eukaryotic cells have one nucleus. Some do not:
  • Aseptate fungal hyphae: no cross-walls; many nuclei in one continuous cytoplasm.
  • Skeletal muscle fibres: formed by fusion of many cells; many nuclei.
  • Red blood cells (mammals): lose the nucleus as they mature: no nucleus.
  • Phloem sieve tube elements: living, but lose the nucleus as they mature: no nucleus; a companion cell supports each one.
Quick check

Mission 5

Built for ATP: mitochondria and chloroplasts

  • Mitochondrion:
  • Small intermembrane space: protons pumped into it quickly reach a high concentration, giving a steep gradient for ATP synthase.
  • Cristae: a large area of inner membrane for electron transport chains and ATP synthase.
  • Matrix: Krebs cycle enzymes and substrates concentrated together.
  • Chloroplast:
  • Thylakoid membranes: a large area holding many photosystems.
  • Small volume inside thylakoids: a steep proton gradient forms quickly.
  • Stroma: Calvin cycle enzymes and substrates concentrated, next to the thylakoids that supply ATP and reduced NADP.
Mission 5

Why the nucleus has a double membrane with pores

  • The double membrane separates the DNA, and transcription, from the cytoplasm.
  • mRNA and proteins are large polar molecules: they cannot cross bilayers. Pores let mRNA out and proteins (e.g. enzymes for transcription) in.
  • In mitosis and meiosis the envelope breaks into vesicles, so spindle microtubules can reach the chromosomes.
  • Afterwards, vesicles fuse to re-form an envelope around each new nucleus.
Quick check

Mission 5

Drawing from an electron micrograph

  • Draw only what the micrograph shows, in proportion; clear single lines; no shading.
  • Double membranes (nucleus, mitochondrion, chloroplast) as two lines.
  • Label with ruled lines; add each structure's function in the annotation.
  • Your required list: nucleus, mitochondria, chloroplasts, sap vacuole, Golgi apparatus, rough and smooth ER, chromosomes, cell wall, plasma membrane, secretory vesicles, microvilli.
  • Microvilli: finger-like folds of the plasma membrane that increase surface area. They do not beat.
Mission check

A cellulose wall and a large vacuole are the reliable plant features. Chloroplasts appear only in cells that receive light.

Mission 6

One cell becomes hundreds of cell types

  • You began as one cell: a zygote.
  • Mitosis made many genetically identical, unspecialised cells.
  • Differentiation: each cell becomes specialised, with a structure suited to one function.
  • A nerve cell and a muscle cell keep the same genes.
  • They differ in which genes are switched on.
  • Visible differentiation comes from expressing genes for tissue-specific proteins.
  • Say: "different genes are expressed" — not: "different genes are kept".
Mission 6

From switched-on gene to specialised cell

  • Switched-on gene → transcribed to active mRNA → translated to protein.
  • Proteins build the cell's structure and run its processes.
  • Muscle cell: genes for actin and myosin on. Red cell precursor: globin genes (for haemoglobin) on.
  • Genes not needed stay in the nucleus, silent.
Quick check
?In a human, a neuron and a liver cell are both descended by mitosis from the same zygote. What is the difference between them once they have differentiated?
Mission 6

What tells a cell which genes to switch on?

  • Identical cells must receive different signals.
  • In an early embryo, some cells release a signalling chemical. It spreads out, forming a concentration gradient.
  • Each cell responds to the concentration it receives: different genes switch on above different thresholds.
  • So position in the gradient sets each cell's fate.
  • Differentiation is often triggered by changes in a cell's environment, such as signals from neighbouring cells.

Mission 6

Cells → tissues → organs → systems

  • Tissue: a group of specialised cells working together to perform the same function.
  • Organ: several tissues grouped together to perform an overall function.
  • Organ system: organs working together, e.g. the digestive system.
  • Stomach (organ): epithelium + muscle + connective tissue.
  • Leaf (organ): epidermis + mesophyll + xylem + phloem.
  • A tissue can hold more than one cell type, e.g. ciliated cells and goblet cells in airway epithelium.
  • Cells in a tissue stick together by cell-adhesion molecules (CAMs) in their membranes. Different CAMs form different types of cell–cell junction.
Mission 6

The whole does what no part can

  • Integration: the parts of a system are coordinated so that together they perform an overall function.
  • Body systems are integrated by nerves, hormones and the blood.
  • Emergent property: a property of the whole that no part has on its own.
  • A cheetah's muscle cells contract. They cannot hunt.
  • Hunting needs muscles, skeleton, nerves, heart, lungs and blood working together.
  • So the cheetah's skill as a predator emerges from integration of its body systems.
Quick check
?A cheetah can sprint at over 100 km per hour and catch fast-moving prey. Why is its effectiveness as a predator described as an emergent property?
Mission check

?In a hypothetical species of plant, leaf cells make large amounts of a protein that binds chlorophyll, but the root cells of the same plant make none of it. Which statement best explains this difference?

Same genes in every cell; different genes switched on. That is what makes a leaf cell, a root cell, a nerve cell or a muscle cell.

Mission 7

Every organism is made of cells

  • Cell theory: all living organisms are made of one or more cells.
  • The cell is the basic structural unit of life.
  • A new organism is found. Prediction: it will be made of cells.
  • That is deductive reasoning: a general theory → a specific prediction.
  • Examining the organism then tests the prediction.
Quick check
?A biologist collects an organism from a deep-sea vent that has never been examined before. Before looking at it under a microscope, she predicts that it will consist of one or more cells. Which statement best describes this reasoning?
Mission 7

Eight functions of life in one cell

  • A unicellular organism's one cell must do everything. Paramecium shows how:
  • Nutrition: cilia sweep food into the oral groove → food vacuole.
  • Metabolism: all the enzyme-catalysed reactions, building up and breaking down.
  • Excretion: metabolic wastes such as CO2 diffuse out across the membrane.
  • Homeostasis: contractile vacuoles expel water that enters by osmosis.
  • Movement: cilia beat. Response to stimuli: it swims away from harmful chemicals.
  • Growth: the cell enlarges between divisions. Reproduction: it divides in two.
  • Say: "the contractile vacuole carries out homeostasis" — not: "excretion". The water is not a metabolic waste.
Mission 7

Protoctists: one cell, two ways to feed

  • Protoctists are eukaryotic: nucleus, mitochondria, membrane-bound organelles.
  • Paramecium: freshwater; heterotroph, takes in organic food made by other organisms. Cilia, oral groove, food vacuoles, contractile vacuoles.
Quick check
?Paramecium is a unicellular organism that lives in fresh water. Which statement correctly identifies one of its functions of life?
Mission 7

The smallest unit of self-sustaining life

  • Given nutrients and energy, a cell sustains itself: metabolism, growth, response, reproduction.
  • Nothing smaller can. The cell is the smallest unit of self-sustaining life.
  • Viruses: genetic material in a protein coat. No cytoplasm, no ribosomes, no metabolism.
  • A virus replicates only inside a host cell, using the cell's machinery → considered non-living.
  • A mitochondrion has DNA and respires, but needs the cell for most of its proteins → not self-sustaining.
Quick check
?Which statement gives a reason that viruses are considered to be non-living?
Mission 7

From one cell to many

  • Unicellular: one cell does every job.
  • Multicellular: cells specialise and share out the jobs.
  • Specialised cells form tissues, organs and systems, integrated in one organism.
  • Multicellularity has evolved repeatedly, in separate lineages: animals, plants, fungi and several groups of algae.
  • All plants and animals, and many fungi and algae, are multicellular.
  • Advantages: larger body size (built from many small cells) and cell specialisation.
  • Why not one giant cell? As a cell grows, its surface area to volume ratio falls, limiting exchange.
  • Unicellular life is not "primitive": most eukaryotic lineages are still unicellular and thrive.
Quick check
?Which statement about the evolution of multicellularity is correct?
Mission check

?Volvox is a green alga: a hollow ball of over a thousand cells. Most of its cells beat flagella to swim but never divide; a few large cells only make new colonies. Its close relative Chlamydomonas is a single cell that both swims and reproduces. What does Volvox gain from being multicellular?

One cell can do it all. Many cells can share out the jobs.

Many small cells also allow a larger body than one cell could reach.

Mission 4

  • Nucleus, mitochondrion: double membranes; RER (ribosomes), SER, Golgi, lysosomes: single membranes; ribosomes: no membrane, 80S in the cytoplasm.
  • Many of one organelle means a lot of its job: lots of RER and Golgi means protein export.

Mission 5

  • Plant cells add a cellulose wall, chloroplasts (in cells that get light) and a large permanent vacuole; fungal walls are chitin; animal cells have no wall.
  • Mitochondria and chloroplasts concentrate enzymes in compartments, pack membranes into large areas and use small spaces for steep proton gradients.

Mission 6

  • Differentiation: cells with the same genes become specialised by expressing different genes.
  • Specialised cells form tissues; tissues form organs; organs form systems.
  • Integrated systems give emergent properties, such as a cheetah's hunting.

Mission 7

  • All organisms are made of cells; a unicellular organism carries out every function of life in one cell.
  • Multicellularity evolved repeatedly; it allows larger bodies and specialised cells.
