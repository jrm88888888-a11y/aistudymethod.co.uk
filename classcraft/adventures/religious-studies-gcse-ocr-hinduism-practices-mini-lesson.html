Mission 1

Yoga means union

  • Yoga comes from a root meaning "to join": union of the self with the divine.
  • It is a whole way of life, not just exercise. (The postures many people do are one small part of one path.)
  • The goal is moksha: liberation from samsara, the cycle of birth and death.
  • Hindus speak of four paths (margas) or yogas, suited to different people:
  • karma (action) · jnana (knowledge) · astanga / raja (meditation) · bhakti (devotion).
Mission 1

Karma yoga: the path of action

  • Doing your duty (dharma) selflessly, without craving its rewards.
  • "Let right deeds be / Thy motive, not the fruit which comes from them." (Bhagavad Gita 2.47, Arnold)
  • Work is offered to God; so it does not create binding karma.
  • Suits active people. Examples: a nurse who works for patients, not praise; seva (service) at a mandir or food bank.
  • The Gita tells Arjuna that, for a warrior, fighting a just war is his own duty (svadharma) and he should not shrink from it (Bhagavad Gita 2.31, paraphrase).
Mission 1

Jnana yoga: the path of knowledge

  • Gaining wisdom about the true self (atman) and ultimate reality (Brahman).
  • Practice: study of scripture with a guru; hearing, reflecting and meditating on the teaching.
  • Viveka (discrimination): telling the lasting from the passing.
  • Linked especially with Advaita Vedanta: knowing that the atman is one with Brahman.
  • Suits thoughtful, reflective people; demanding and often slow.
  • Desire hides knowledge "As smoke blots the white fire, as clinging rust / Mars the bright mirror" (Bhagavad Gita 3.38, Arnold); so the knower must govern the senses (3.39–41).
Mission 1

Astanga (raja) yoga: the path of meditation

  • Controlling the mind through meditation, set out in the Yoga Sutras of Patanjali.
  • Eight limbs (astanga): yama (restraints) · niyama (observances) · asana (posture) · pranayama (breath control) · pratyahara (withdrawing the senses) · dharana (concentration) · dhyana (meditation) · samadhi (absorption).
  • Also called raja ("royal") yoga.
  • Suits disciplined people who can practise patiently.
  • "Let each man raise / The Self by Soul, not trample down his Self" (Bhagavad Gita 6.5, Arnold): the mind can be your friend or your enemy.

Mission 1

Bhakti yoga: the path of devotion

  • Loving devotion to a personal God: e.g. Krishna, Rama, Shiva or the Goddess.
  • Practice: puja, singing (bhajan, kirtan), repeating God's names (japa), pilgrimage, surrender.
  • "A leaf, a flower, a fruit, water poured forth, / That offering I accept, lovingly made" (Bhagavad Gita 9.26, Arnold).
  • Open to everyone, whatever their learning or caste; God's grace helps the devotee.
  • Often described as the most widely followed path in Hinduism today.
  • The next verses ask that all actions be offered to God, which frees the devotee from the bonds of karma (Bhagavad Gita 9.27–28, paraphrase).

Mission 1Diversity

Which path is best?

  • Advaita (Shankara): jnana is the direct path; knowledge alone ends ignorance. Other paths purify the mind for it.
  • Vaishnavas (Ramanuja; ISKCON): bhakti is highest; God's grace saves.
  • Gandhi and others: karma yoga in the world, serving others as service of God.
  • Swami Vivekananda (1863–1902): all four lead to the same goal; each suits a different temperament (active, reflective, emotional, meditative).
  • Many Hindus read the Gita as weaving the paths together rather than ranking them.
Mission 1

Paths, karma and moksha

  • Karma: selfless action makes no binding karma; selfish action keeps the self in samsara.
  • Jnana: knowledge burns up karma "As the kindled flame / Feeds on the fuel till it sinks to ash" (Bhagavad Gita 4.37, Arnold).
  • Bhakti: God's grace releases the devotee from karma.
  • Moksha means different things: for Advaita, realising oneness with Brahman; for devotional Hindus, eternal loving relationship with God.
Mission 1

One life, many paths

  • Most Hindus combine paths rather than choose one.
  • Morning: meditation and breathing (raja).
  • Work: doing the job well, without selfishness (karma).
  • Evening: arti at the home shrine; singing bhajans (bhakti).
  • Weekend: a Gita study class (jnana).
  • Why different types matter: people differ, and so do the stages of life (ashramas): a student, a parent and a retired person have different time and strengths.
Mission 1Evaluate

"Bhakti is the best path to moksha."

  • For: open to everyone, whatever their learning; the Gita calls the devoted yogi "truest and best" (6.47).
  • For: relies on God's grace, not on human strength alone; the most widely practised path.
  • Against: Advaita says only knowledge removes ignorance; bhakti prepares for it.
  • Against: without selfless action (karma yoga), devotion can become private and ignore others' needs.
  • Against: many Hindus say no path is "best": each suits a different person.
  • Judge it by: What is moksha (union or relationship)? Does the answer depend on the person? Do the paths really separate?

Mission 2

Dana and sewa

  • Dana = giving: money, food, clothes, time or knowledge.
  • Sewa (seva) = selfless service: work for others with no reward.
  • Both are part of dharma, especially for householders (grihastha), who earn and so can give.
  • Both are karma yoga: action done as an offering, not for praise.
  • The Gita calls the best gift one given gladly, "when he who takes / Can render nothing back", at the right place and time (Bhagavad Gita 17.20, Arnold 1885).
  • Giving to gain status or with a grudge is a lower kind of giving.
Mission 2Read the source

The thief who gives nothing back

> "Spake Prajapati-- / In the beginning, when all men were made, / And, with mankind, the sacrifice-- 'Do this! / Work! sacrifice! Increase and multiply / With sacrifice! ... But if one eats / Fruits of the earth, rendering to kindly Heaven / No gift of toil, that thief steals from his world.'" (Bhagavad Gita 3.10–12, Arnold 1885)

  • Prajapati, the creator, made people together with yajna (sacrifice, offering).
  • Life is a cycle of giving: the gods give rain and food; humans give back in offering and work.
  • To take without giving back breaks the cycle: such a person is a thief.

Show answer

Sacrifice, yajna.

Show answer

Mission 2

Svadharma: your own duty to serve

  • Svadharma = one's own duty, according to stage of life and situation.
  • A student's duty is to learn; a householder's to support family and give; a retired person's to guide and serve.
  • "Let right deeds be / Thy motive, not the fruit which comes from them." (Bhagavad Gita 2.47, Arnold 1885)
  • So sewa is done because it is right, not for reward.
  • Gita 9.26–28: even "a leaf, a flower, a fruit, water" offered with love is accepted; all actions, including "giving gifts", can be offered to God.

Mission 2

Sewa International

  • A Hindu faith-based charity working in many countries; Sewa UK (founded 1991, based in Birmingham) is its British branch.
  • It serves people of any religion, caste or gender.
  • Well-being: disaster relief and rebuilding after earthquakes and floods; community health and education projects.
  • Social inclusion: support for disabled children (schooling, health care, equipment) and for poor communities often left out of mainstream services.
  • Women's rights: in India, women's centres offering skills training and help to start small businesses, so women can earn their own income.
  • In Britain: volunteers, charity shops, fundraising events.
  • Why: sewa as karma yoga; the same atman in every person.
Mission 2

Social inclusion

  • Teaching: the same atman is in everyone: the wise see the same self in a learned Brahmin, a cow, an elephant, a dog and an outcaste (Bhagavad Gita 5.18, paraphrase).
  • Problem: caste (jati) discrimination, especially against Dalits (once called "untouchables").
  • India's constitution (1950) abolished untouchability; reformers opened temples to all.
  • B. R. Ambedkar (1891–1956), a Dalit leader, argued caste itself must end.
  • Hindu charities include people of every background, disabled people and refugees.
  • In Britain, some Hindus say caste no longer matters; others report it still affects marriage and community life.
Mission 2

Women's rights

  • Teaching: the divine is also Shakti, feminine power; women seers such as Gargi and Maitreyi appear in the Upanishads.
  • The Laws of Manu say the gods rejoice where women are honoured (Manusmriti 3.56, paraphrase), but other verses limit women's independence: Hindus read these texts differently.
  • Problems: dowry demands; preference for sons; poor treatment of widows.
  • Change: sati banned (1829); dowry banned in India (1961); reformers and charities work for girls' education, health and income.
  • Today some women train as priests and lead puja and weddings.
Mission 2

Gandhi's example

  • Mohandas K. Gandhi (1869–1948) drew his ethics from the Gita, which he read as a guide to selfless action.
  • Sarvodaya: the welfare of all, starting with the poorest.
  • He worked to end untouchability, calling Dalits "Harijans" (people of God): a name many Dalits now reject as patronising.
  • He brought women into the freedom movement in large numbers.
  • Ahimsa and satyagraha (holding to truth): non-violent action for justice.
  • His example inspires many Hindu charities' sewa today.

Mission 2Evaluate

"Sewa is better than giving money."

  • For: sewa costs the self (time, effort, pride); it is karma yoga in its purest form.
  • For: serving in person builds relationships and humility; volunteers who serve free meals (such as ISKCON Food for Life) meet those they feed.
  • Against: money can do more at a distance: disaster relief needs funds; the Gita praises the right gift at the right time.
  • Against: not everyone can serve in person (age, health, work); dana is open to all.
  • Another view: both are needed; what matters is the attitude: done without wanting reward.
  • Judge it by: Which helps the person in need more? Which changes the giver more? Does the motive matter more than the result?

Mission 3

Worship anywhere

  • Hindus believe the divine is everywhere (Brahman) and within the heart (antaryami).
  • So worship can happen in many places: the mandir (temple), the home, outdoor shrines, rivers and hills, and the heart.
  • Each place helps in a different way: focus, community, daily habit, closeness to nature, inner peace.
  • Most Hindus' daily worship happens at home; the mandir is for special visits, festivals and community.
Mission 3

The mandir: a home for God

  • Mandir: a "dwelling"; the deity is treated as a royal guest.
  • Garbhagriha ("womb-chamber"): the inner shrine, small and dark, holding the main murti.
  • Murti: sacred image in which, after a consecration rite (prana pratishtha), the deity is believed present.
  • Shikhara ("peak"): the tower above the garbhagriha, like a sacred mountain linking earth and heaven.
  • Vahana ("vehicle"): the deity's animal, facing the shrine: Nandi the bull for Shiva, Garuda the eagle for Vishnu, a lion for Durga, a mouse for Ganesha.
  • Mandapa: hall for worshippers; a bell rung on entering; a path to walk clockwise round the shrine (pradakshina).
Mission 3

Worship together in the mandir

  • Priests (pujaris) wake, bathe, dress and offer food to the murtis through the day.
  • Devotees come for darshan: to see, and be seen by, the deity.
  • Congregational worship: many gather for arti (lamp offering) with singing; prasad (blessed food) is shared.
  • Festivals, weddings and life rites bring the whole community together.
  • The mandir is also a community centre: classes in language, music and scripture; meals; care for elders.

Mission 3

The home shrine

  • Most Hindu homes have a shrine: a room, a corner or a cupboard.
  • It holds murtis or pictures of the family's chosen deities (ishta devata), a lamp (diya), incense, a bell, water, flowers.
  • Importance: makes God part of daily life; brings the family together; children learn by joining in.
  • Food may be offered before meals and eaten as prasad.
  • The shrine is kept clean; shoes are left outside.
Mission 3

Outdoors and shrines

  • Outdoor shrines: by roads, under trees, at village edges; a stone or murti marked with red powder, flowers and a lamp.
  • Village deities protect the local area; passers-by stop briefly to pray.
  • Sacred trees (e.g. the peepal) and the tulsi plant in a courtyard are worshipped.
  • Rivers and hills: bathing at the Ganges ghats; walking round a sacred hill.
  • Importance: God is present in nature; worship is woven into ordinary journeys.
Mission 3Read the source

Worship in the heart

  • The space of the heart: God within (antaryami); worship by meditation and inner prayer, anywhere.

Bhagavad Gita 9.13–14 (Edwin Arnold's translation, 1885):

> "But My Mahatmas, those of noble soul / Who tread the path celestial, worship Me / With hearts unwandering ... Unendingly / They glorify Me"

  • Krishna says worship of the unmanifest, formless God is harder for embodied beings (Bhagavad Gita 12.5, paraphrase): one reason for temples and murtis.

Mission 4

What worship is for

  • Hindu worship is mostly bhakti (loving devotion): treating God as an honoured, beloved guest.
  • It uses all the senses: sight (murti, lamps), sound (bells, mantras, songs), smell (incense, flowers), touch, taste (prasad).
  • Purposes: to show love and thanks; to receive blessing; to focus the mind; to purify the heart.
  • Offerings are returned as prasad (grace): blessed food shared by everyone.
Mission 4

Puja: honouring God as a guest

  • Puja: worship with offerings to a murti, at home or in the mandir.
  • Typical steps: ring the bell; light a lamp and incense; offer water, flowers, rice, fruit or sweets; say or sing mantras; bow.
  • In the mandir, priests may bathe and dress the murti (a fuller puja of up to 16 offerings).
  • The puja tray (thali) holds lamp, incense, kumkum (red powder), rice, flowers, a small bell and a water pot.
  • Food offered is then taken back and eaten as prasad.
Mission 4

Arti and darshan

  • Arti: a tray with lamps of ghee (clarified butter) is waved in circles before the murti, while people sing (e.g. Om Jai Jagdish Hare) and bells ring.
  • Then worshippers pass their hands over the flame and draw them to their eyes and head: receiving the deity's blessing and light.
  • Darshan ("seeing"): to see the deity, and be seen by the deity. Eye contact with the murti is a moment of blessing.
  • Darshan can also be of a holy person or a holy place.
  • In the mandir, the curtain before the shrine is drawn back for darshan at set times.
Mission 4

Havan: the fire offering

  • Havan (or homa): offerings of ghee, grains and herbs (samagri) placed into a sacred fire.
  • It comes from the oldest Vedic worship: fire (Agni) carries offerings to the gods.
  • Led by a priest chanting Vedic mantras; worshippers add offerings with each "svaha".
  • Used at life rites (weddings are witnessed by the fire), new homes and festivals, and to purify.
  • The Arya Samaj makes havan central and does not use murtis.

Mission 4

Sacred sound: mantra, japa, bhajan, kirtan

  • Mantra: a sacred word or verse believed to carry spiritual power. Om (Aum) is the most sacred sound.
  • Gayatri Mantra (Rig Veda 3.62.10), recited daily by many Hindus at dawn: "May we attain that excellent glory of Savitar the God: So may he stimulate our prayers." (Griffith's translation, 1896)
  • Japa: repeating a mantra or God's name, often on a mala (108 beads): e.g. Om Namah Shivaya; Hare Krishna.
  • Bhajan: devotional song. Kirtan: call-and-response singing of God's names, with drums, cymbals and harmonium.
Mission 4

Meditation

  • Dhyana: stilling and focusing the mind on God, on Om, or on the true self.
  • The Gita describes the setting: "In a fair, still spot / Having his fixed abode,—not too much raised, / Nor yet too low" (Bhagavad Gita 6.11, Arnold), with the mind fixed on the One (6.12).
  • Can be done anywhere: at home, in the mandir, outdoors.
  • Valued especially in raja yoga and by Advaita Hindus; devotional Hindus meditate on a form of God.
Mission 4Read the source

Offer it with love

Krishna to Arjuna, Bhagavad Gita 9.26 (Edwin Arnold's translation, 1885):

> "Whoso shall offer Me in faith and love / A leaf, a flower, a fruit, water poured forth, / That offering I accept, lovingly made / With pious will."

  • 9.13–14: great souls worship "with hearts unwandering" and glorify God unendingly; 12.5: worship of the formless is harder for embodied beings (paraphrase).

Mission 4Diversity

Vaishnava and Shaiva worship

  • Vaishnavas (devotees of Vishnu and his avatars, Krishna and Rama): murtis dressed, decorated and offered food many times a day; tulsi leaves; a vertical tilak mark on the forehead; kirtan of God's names (e.g. ISKCON's Hare Krishna).
  • Shaivas (devotees of Shiva): worship of the Shiva linga with abhisheka (pouring water, milk or honey over it); bilva leaves; three horizontal lines of sacred ash; Om Namah Shivaya; all-night worship at Maha Shivaratri.
  • Both are bhakti: loving devotion to a personal God.
Mission 4Diversity

How important is worship? What are its benefits?

  • Devotional Hindus: worship is the heart of religion; through it God's grace is received.
  • Advaita Hindus: worship of a form of God is a valuable step; it prepares the mind for knowledge of formless Brahman.
  • Arya Samaj: havan and Vedic prayer, but no murtis.
  • Some Hindus: service of others and right living matter more than ritual.
  • Benefits named by Hindus: peace and focus; closeness to God; good karma and purification; family and community bonds; passing on tradition to children.
Mission 4Evaluate

"Worship is essential for every Hindu."

  • For: bhakti is the most widely followed path; the Gita praises the one who worships with the inmost self (6.47).
  • For: daily puja shapes family life and passes on the faith.
  • Against: jnana and karma yoga are also paths to moksha; a Hindu may follow them with little ritual.
  • Against: Advaita treats worship of forms as a stage, not the goal.
  • Against: some Hindus say living by dharma matters more than ritual.
  • Judge it by: What counts as "worship" (ritual only, or service and meditation too)? Which tradition? What is the goal of worship?

Mission 5

One God, personal or beyond form

  • Most Hindus believe in one ultimate reality, Brahman.
  • Some worship it as non-personal: beyond all form and qualities (nirguna).
  • Many more worship it as a personal, loving God (saguna, Bhagavan), often as Vishnu, Krishna, Shiva or the Goddess.
  • The Gita says both kinds of worshipper reach God, but the path to the unmanifest is "hard ... for such as bend their minds / To reach th' Unmanifest" (Bhagavad Gita 12.5, Arnold).
  • So many Hindus find it easier to love God in a form.
Mission 5

Many deities

  • Hindus may worship several deities (devas and devis), e.g. Ganesha before a new start, Lakshmi for prosperity, Saraswati before exams.
  • Many see them as forms or aspects of the one God.
  • Each family often has its ishta devata (chosen deity) and a family or village deity.
  • In the Gita, Krishna says that those who worship other gods in faith are really worshipping him, though not in the proper way (Bhagavad Gita 9.23, paraphrase).
  • Different traditions: Vaishnavas see Vishnu as supreme; Shaivas, Shiva; Shaktas, the Goddess; Smartas honour five deities as equal forms of Brahman.
Mission 5

The murti

  • A murti is a sacred image of a deity, in stone, metal, wood or painting.
  • After consecration (prana pratishtha), the deity is believed to be present in it: it is served, dressed and fed.
  • It represents God: its features carry meaning (many arms: power to help; objects held: qualities; a raised palm: "do not fear").
  • Not an "idol": Hindus do not worship the material, but God present through it.
  • It focuses the mind and allows darshan: seeing and being seen by God.

Mission 5

Gurus, holy people and elders

  • A guru is a spiritual teacher who leads from darkness to light (a traditional explanation of the word).
  • Disciples honour the guru as a channel of God's grace; Guru Purnima is a festival honouring gurus.
  • Sadhus and sadhvis: men and women who renounce ordinary life for God. Hindus seek their darshan and blessing.
  • Elders: many Hindus touch the feet of parents, grandparents and teachers in respect (pranam) and receive a blessing.
  • Honour is given not because they are God, but because God is seen and served in them.
Mission 5

Holy land, hills and rivers

  • Holy land: many Hindus see India (Bharat) as sacred, full of places where God appeared or acted.
  • Rivers are honoured as goddesses: the Ganges (Ganga Ma, "Mother Ganga"), Yamuna and others. Bathing in them is believed to purify.
  • Hills: Mount Kailash (Shiva's home); Govardhan (lifted by Krishna to shelter villagers); devotees walk round them in worship.
  • Tirthas ("crossing places"): holy sites where the human and divine meet.
  • Darshan can be of a holy place, as well as of a murti or a holy person.
Mission 5

Sacred plants and animals

  • Tulsi (holy basil): sacred to Vishnu; grown in courtyards, watered and circled daily.
  • Peepal (ashvattha) tree: among the things Krishna says he is: "Aswattha, the fig-tree, of all the trees that grow" (Bhagavad Gita 10.26, Arnold). Bilva leaves are offered to Shiva.
  • The cow: honoured as a mother who gives milk; linked with Krishna the cowherd; protected by ahimsa.
  • Vahanas: each deity's animal (Nandi the bull, Garuda the eagle).
  • Others: monkeys (Hanuman), snakes (Nag Panchami festival).
  • Why: God is present in all living things; caring for them is worship and ahimsa.
Mission 5Read the source

God in all things

Krishna to Arjuna, Bhagavad Gita 6.29–30 (Edwin Arnold's translation, 1885):

> "He so vowed, / So blended, sees the Life-Soul resident / In all things living, and all living things / In that Life-Soul contained. And whoso thus / Discerneth Me in all, and all in Me, / I never let him go"

  • 9.13–14: great souls worship "with hearts unwandering" (Arnold).

Mission 5Diversity

Different focuses, different traditions

  • Vaishnavas: Vishnu and his avatars; tulsi; ISKCON (founded 1966) worships Krishna as the Supreme Person.
  • Shaivas: Shiva, especially as the linga; Kailash; Varanasi.
  • Shaktas: the Goddess (Durga, Kali, Parvati) as the supreme power.
  • Smartas: five deities honoured as equal forms of Brahman.
  • Advaita: forms help the mind towards formless Brahman.
  • Arya Samaj: one formless God; no murtis.
  • Many Hindus are not strictly one of these: they honour many focuses in daily life.
Mission 5Evaluate

"Hindus should focus their worship on one form of God only."

  • For: a single chosen deity (ishta devata) gives deep, focused devotion.
  • For: some traditions focus on one form: ISKCON worships Krishna as the Supreme Person.
  • Against: if all deities are forms of one God, worshipping many is still worshipping one.
  • Against: honouring rivers, cows, gurus and elders expresses the belief that God is in all things (Gita 6.29–30).
  • Against: different focuses suit different needs, places and stages of life.
  • Judge it by: Are the deities forms of one God? Does variety help or distract devotion? Which tradition's view?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Yoga means union with the divine; the four paths are karma (action), jnana (knowledge), astanga/raja (meditation) and bhakti (devotion), all aiming at moksha.
  • Gita 6.46–47: the yogi is above ascetics, scholars and doers; the best yogi worships with devotion.
  • Hindus disagree about which path is highest but most combine paths to suit temperament and stage of life.

Mission 2

  • Dana (giving) and sewa (selfless service) are part of dharma and karma yoga; Gita 3.10–12: one who takes without giving back is a thief.
  • Sewa International: well-being, inclusion, women's rights; same atman in all.

Mission 3

  • Hindus worship in the mandir, the home, at outdoor shrines and rivers, and in the space of the heart.
  • Mandir features: garbhagriha with murti, shikhara, vahana facing the shrine, mandapa; congregational arti, darshan and prasad.

Mission 4

  • Forms of worship: puja (offerings), arti (lamps), darshan (seeing), havan (fire), japa (repetition), bhajan and kirtan (song), meditation; offered food returns as prasad.
  • Gita 9.26: a leaf, flower, fruit or water offered with love is accepted.
  • Vaishnava and Shaiva worship differ in deity, offerings and marks; Hindus differ on how important ritual worship is.

Mission 5

  • Focuses of worship: one God (personal or non-personal); many deities as forms of one God; the murti in which God is present; gurus, sadhus and elders; holy land, hills and rivers; sacred plants and animals.
  • Gita 6.29–30: the yogi sees God in all things and all things in God.
  • Traditions differ: Vaishnava, Shaiva, Shakta, Smarta, Advaita, Arya Samaj.
