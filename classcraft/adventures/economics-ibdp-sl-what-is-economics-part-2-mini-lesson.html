Mission 1

Mission 1: Why does each extra unit of one thing cost a country more and more of another?

Make a guess: A country moves workers from farms to factories. Each extra batch of machines costs it more food than the last. What is the most likely reason?

Mission 1

The curve of the possible

  • A production possibility curve (PPC) shows the maximum combinations of two goods an economy can make with its resources and technology.
  • Assumptions: only two goods; a fixed quantity and quality of resources; fixed technology; on the curve, all resources are fully and efficiently used.
  • Resources are limited, so the curve has an edge: scarcity.
  • Picking one point means giving up others: choice and trade-offs.
Mission 1

Worked example: opportunity cost on the curve

  • Illustrative data: an economy makes food and machines.
PointMachinesFood (tonnes)
A0150
B10140
C20120
D3090
E4050
F500
  • Opportunity cost = the next best alternative given up.
  • Step 1: B → C adds 10 machines and loses 140 − 120 = 20 tonnes of food.
  • Step 2: per machine: 20 ÷ 10 = 2 tonnes of food.
  • Step 3: repeat along the curve: 1, 2, 3, 4, then 5 tonnes per machine (E → F).
  • Say: "the opportunity cost of 1 machine is 2 tonnes of food" — not: a money price, or "costs 20 machines".

Mission 1

Shape: why the curve bows outward

  • Increasing opportunity cost → the curve is concave to the origin (bowed outward).
  • Why: resources are not equally suited to both goods. The first workers moved to machines are those best at machines and worst at farming. Later ones are expert farmers.
  • Constant opportunity cost → a straight line. Resources are equally good at both goods.
  • The slope of the curve at any point shows the opportunity cost there.
  • Be ready to draw both: concave (increasing) and straight-line (constant).
Mission 1Graph lab

Slide back along the curve

  • Takeaway: rising opportunity cost works in both directions. The more food is made, the more machines each extra tonne costs. On a straight line the cost is constant.
Mission 1

Inside, on and outside the curve

  • On the curve (A–F): all resources used → productively efficient.
  • Inside (point U): some resources are unemployed or used inefficiently.
  • Why an economy can be inside: a recession, unemployed workers, idle factories, strikes, resources stuck in the wrong uses.
  • Outside (point X): unattainable with today's resources and technology.
  • Allocative efficiency: the one point on the curve that best matches what society wants. The curve alone can't show which point that is.
  • From U, more of both goods is possible with no opportunity cost: the resources were idle.
Quick check
?On a PPC for food and machines, an economy is at a point inside the curve. Which statement is correct?
Mission 1

Movements along and shifts of the curve

  • Movement along the curve: resources are reallocated between the goods. The curve stays put.
  • Shift outward: more or better resources, or better technology or productivity (education, training, investment, new discoveries).
  • Shift inward: resources lost or damaged: war, natural disaster, emigration, depleted resources.
  • Skewed (non-parallel) shift: the change affects one good only. A better machine-making method moves the machines end only; the food end stays put.
  • Real case: the 2011 earthquake and tsunami in Japan destroyed buildings, ports, roads and equipment. Japan's government estimated the damage at about 16.9 trillion yen. Lost capital → an inward shift.
  • Say: "a movement along the PPC" (reallocation) — not: "a shift" for a change of mix.
Mission 1Graph lab

Better machines only

  • Takeaway: a skewed outward shift. Each machine now costs half as much food.
Mission 1

More output: two routes

  • Actual growth: output rises, e.g. from U (inside) to a point on the curve. Uses spare resources. Short run.
  • Potential growth: the curve shifts outward. The economy's capacity rises. Long run.
  • IB calls an outward shift growth in production possibilities.
  • Both together: the economy reaches a point beyond today's curve.
  • Say: "actual growth" (more output) vs "potential growth" (more capacity) — not: "growth" for both.
Mission 1

The PPC and AD/AS

  • The LRAS curve is vertical at full-employment output (Yf): the most the economy can produce sustainably, just as on the PPC.
  • Outward PPC shift ↔ LRAS shifts right.
  • Point inside the PPC ↔ real GDP below Yf.
  • An increase in AD from below Yf raises real GDP: actual growth, shown on the PPC as a move from inside towards the curve.
  • IB calls output below potential a deflationary (recessionary) gap.
Quick check
?A country's farms and factories are unchanged, but thousands of skilled workers emigrate. At the same time, the government moves some workers from farming into building machines. How should this be shown on a PPC for food and machines?
Mission 1Evaluate

What the PPC can and can't tell you

  • It shows what is possible, not what is best. Choosing a point needs a value judgement about what society wants.
  • It depends on assumptions: two goods, fixed resources and technology. Real economies make millions of goods and change constantly.
  • Growth from an outward shift takes time. Investment, training and research pay off years later.
  • The curve shows output, not who gets it or the environmental cost of producing it.
  • It depends on why an economy is inside the curve: a short recession and a long-run problem (e.g. skills in the wrong places) need different fixes.

Mission 2: Why must a country's total spending, total income and total output always be equal?

Make a guess: Why must a country's total spending, total income and total output always be equal?

Mission 2

Households and firms

  • The simplest model has two groups:
    • Households: own the factors of production (land, labour, capital, enterprise) and buy goods and services.
    • Firms: hire factors and produce goods and services.
  • Two flows go round in opposite directions:
    • Real flow: factor services → firms; goods and services → households.
    • Money flow: factor incomes (wages, rent, interest, profit) → households; spending → firms.
  • Assumptions of this simple model: no saving, no government, no trade. Households spend all their income.
Mission 2

Income = output = expenditure

  • Illustrative data. In one year, firms produce final goods worth 500 billion.
  • Households buy them: expenditure = 500 billion.
  • Firms pay out what they receive:
    • wages 320 + rent 40 + interest 30 = 390 billion
    • the rest is profit: 500 − 390 = 110 billion, income to the firms' owners.
  • Income = 390 + 110 = 500 billion.
  • Because profit is whatever is left, total income must equal the value of output.
  • And every unit spent is someone's income. So:
    • national output = national income = national expenditure
  • Three ways to measure national income: output (value added), income (factor incomes), expenditure (spending on final output).
  • In real data, the three measures differ a little, because of measurement errors (a "statistical discrepancy").

?Why must total income in an economy equal the total value of output, in principle?
Mission 2

Withdrawals and injections

  • Leakages (also called withdrawals): income not passed on as spending on domestic output.
    • Saving (S), Taxation (T), Imports (M).
  • Injections: spending on domestic output that does not come from households' consumption.
    • Investment (I) by firms, Government spending (G), Exports (X).
  • Withdrawals shrink the flow; injections enlarge it.
  • Say: "investment is spending by firms on capital goods" — not: buying shares, which is saving.
  • Say: "government spending (G) on goods and services" — not: transfer payments, which only move income between households.
Mission 2

The five sectors

  • Households: supply factors; spend, save, pay tax, buy imports.
  • Firms: produce; pay factor incomes; invest.
  • Government: collects taxes (T); buys goods and services (G); pays transfers.
  • Banks and the financial sector: take households' savings; lend to firms for investment.
  • Foreign sector: buys exports (X); sells imports (M).
  • Every sector depends on the others: one group's spending is another's income.
Quick check
?Which of these is an injection into the circular flow of Canada?
Mission 2

Equilibrium

  • National income is in equilibrium when total withdrawals (W) = total injections (J):
    • S + T + M = I + G + X
  • Injections > withdrawals: more is entering the flow than leaving → national income rises.
  • Withdrawals > injections: more is leaving than entering → national income falls.
  • As income changes, withdrawals change too (more income → more saving, tax, imports), until W = J again.
  • The pairs need not match: S can be above I, as long as the totals are equal.

Mission 2

Case: spring 2020

  • April 2020, United States, during COVID-19 lockdowns (first estimates):
    • Household spending fell 13.6% in one month.
    • Households saved 33% of disposable income (12.7% in March).
  • More saving = a bigger withdrawal. Less spending = less income for firms and their workers.
  • Personal income actually rose 10.5%, mainly from government benefit payments: a reminder that transfers move income but are not G.
  • Many countries saw the same pattern: saving up, spending and output down.

Mission 3

Mission 3: How can you sum up who gets what in a whole country with a single number?

Make a guess: How can you sum up who gets what in a whole country with a single number?

Mission 3

Income is a flow, wealth is a stock

  • Income: a flow of money over a period, e.g. per year.
    • wages, rent, interest, dividends, profit, government benefits
  • Wealth: a stock of assets owned at a point in time, minus debts.
    • property, savings, shares, pension funds
  • They feed each other: wealth earns income (rent, interest, dividends); saved income builds wealth.
  • So inequality in wealth tends to create inequality in income, and the reverse.
  • Say: "wealth is a stock; income is a flow" — not: "wealthy" means "high income". A retired homeowner can be wealthy on a low income.
Quick check
?Which of these is a change in a household's wealth rather than its income?
Mission 3

Equality or equity?

  • Equality: everyone has the same: equal incomes, wealth or opportunities.
  • Equity: fairness. What is fair is a value judgement: a normative question.
  • Equality of outcome: the same result for all. Equality of opportunity: the same chances, results may differ.
  • Horizontal equity: people in the same position are treated the same.
  • Vertical equity: people in different positions are treated differently, e.g. higher incomes pay a higher share in tax.
  • Say: "equity means fairness" — not: "equity means equality". An equitable distribution need not be equal.
Mission 3

The Lorenz curve

  • Line up everyone from poorest to richest.
  • Horizontal axis: cumulative % of population. Vertical axis: cumulative % of income (or wealth).
  • Line of equality: the 45° line. The bottom 20% get 20%, the bottom 40% get 40%, and so on.
  • The Lorenz curve shows the actual shares. It sags below the line of equality.
  • Read it: a point at (40, 15) means the poorest 40% receive 15% of income.
  • The further the curve from the line of equality, the more unequal the distribution.
  • A change in distribution: curve moves towards the line → more equal; away → less equal.
Mission 3

The Gini coefficient

  • Gini coefficient = A ÷ (A + B).
    • A: area between the line of equality and the Lorenz curve.
    • B: area under the Lorenz curve.
  • 0 = perfect equality. 1 = perfect inequality (one person has everything). Often shown as 0–100.
  • UK disposable household income: Gini 32.9 on the 0–100 scale (financial year ending 2024, ONS).
  • Great Britain household wealth: Gini about 0.6, or 60 on the 0–100 scale (2018–2020, ONS). The richest 10% of households held 43% of all wealth; the poorest half held 9%.
  • World Bank Gini estimates (0–100 scale): South Africa 63.0 (2014); Brazil 48.9 (2020); Slovak Republic 23.2 (2019).
  • Countries survey in different years, so compare with care.
  • Limits: two different Lorenz curves can give the same Gini; a Gini does not show where the inequality lies, or how rich the country is.

Mission 3

Why is income unequal?

  • Human capital: education and skills raise productivity and pay.
  • Ownership of wealth: land, property and shares earn rent, interest and dividends; inheritance passes them on.
  • Inequality of opportunity: family background affects schooling, contacts and health.
  • Discrimination by gender, ethnicity and others.
  • Power: bargaining power of unions, employers with monopsony power, top executives.
  • Tax and benefit policy: progressive taxes and benefits narrow gaps; regressive taxes widen them.
  • Globalisation and technological change: raise the pay of scarce skills; some routine jobs move abroad or are automated.
  • Market-based supply-side policies: lower top tax rates, weaker union power and labour market deregulation can widen inequality.
Mission 3

Inequality as a market failure, and its effects

  • Markets allocate goods to those willing and able to pay. A market can be efficient and still leave some with very little.
  • Effects:
    • individuals: worse health, education and housing for those on low incomes
    • growth: big rewards can create incentives to work and invest; but very unequal societies may waste talent, and an OECD study (2014) found rising inequality lowered growth in OECD countries
    • living standards and social stability: high inequality is linked to lower trust and more political tension
  • The evidence on growth points both ways, so economists disagree on how much inequality harms it.
Mission 3Evaluate

How much inequality is a problem?

It depends on:

  • Values. Fairness is a normative judgement; people disagree on how equal outcomes should be.
  • Causes. Inequality from effort and skill is viewed differently from inequality from inheritance, luck or discrimination.
  • Mobility. Wide gaps matter more if children of poor families stay poor.
  • Living standards of the poorest. Rising inequality with rising absolute incomes differs from falling incomes at the bottom.
  • Measurement. Income or wealth? Before or after taxes and benefits? Wealth is distributed far more unequally than income.
Mission check

?In country P, the Gini coefficient for household income is 0.30 and for household wealth is 0.65. (Illustrative data.) What can you conclude?

A Gini coefficient measures how evenly something is shared, not how much there is: here wealth is shared less evenly than income.

Mission 4

  • Economic ideas respond to the problems of their age.
PeriodThinker or schoolProblem of the time
18th century (1776)Adam SmithTrade restrictions; early industry
19th century (1867)Karl MarxPoverty and harsh conditions in industrial cities
19th century (1870s)Marginal revolutionHow to explain value and prices
20th century (1936)John Maynard KeynesMass unemployment in the Great Depression
20th century (1970s)Monetarists, new classicalHigh inflation with high unemployment

  • Each school answers one big question differently: what role should the state play?
Mission 4

Adam Smith and laissez faire

  • The Wealth of Nations (1776).
  • People pursuing their self-interest in competitive markets are "led by an invisible hand" to serve society, though that is not their aim.
  • Division of labour raises productivity (the pin factory).
  • Laissez faire (French: "let do", that is, leave people free to act): keep government out of most market decisions.
  • Smith's state still has duties: defence, justice (law and property rights) and certain public works that markets will not provide.
  • He also warned that business owners often conspire to raise prices.
Mission 4

19th century: the margin and Say's law

  • Classical microeconomics: value comes from utility, and choices are made at the margin (Jevons, Menger, Walras, 1870s).
  • Example: water is vital but cheap; diamonds are not vital but costly. Price reflects the utility of one more unit (marginal utility), not total usefulness.
  • Say's law (classical macroeconomics): "supply creates its own demand". Producing goods generates the incomes to buy them.
  • So there can be no lasting general shortage of demand: markets self-correct, and full employment is normal.
Mission 4

Karl Marx: the critique

  • Das Kapital (1867).
  • Workers create value, but owners of capital (the means of production) keep part of it as profit: exploitation.
  • Competition drives firms to cut wages and replace workers with machines, leading to recurring crises.
  • Class conflict between workers and owners would end capitalism.
  • Role of the state: in Marx's vision, collective ownership of the means of production and planning replace private ownership and markets.
  • Marx's work was a direct critique of classical economics.
Quick check
?Which statement matches a thinker with their view of the role of the state?
Mission 4

20th century: the Keynesian revolution

  • Great Depression: in the early 1930s, unemployment reached about a quarter of the US labour force.
  • Keynes, The General Theory (1936): an economy can get stuck with too little total demand and high unemployment for a long time.
  • This rejects Say's law: income can be saved rather than spent.
  • Role of the state: manage demand (government spending, taxes, interest rates) to keep employment high.
  • After 1945, macroeconomic policy became a normal task of governments.
Mission 4

The monetarist and new classical counter-revolution

  • 1970s: stagflation: high inflation and high unemployment together, which demand management struggled to explain.
  • Monetarists (Milton Friedman): inflation is caused by too-rapid growth of the money supply. Control money; avoid fine-tuning.
  • New classical economists: people form rational expectations, so predictable attempts to boost demand raise prices rather than output.
  • Role of the state: stable rules, low inflation, freer markets; more focus on the supply side.
Quick check
?In the 1970s, many economies had rising inflation and rising unemployment at the same time. Why did this strengthen the monetarist and new classical counter-revolution?
Mission 4

21st century: new conversations

  • Behavioural economics: economics in dialogue with psychology (Nobel prizes: Kahneman 2002, Thaler 2017).
  • Real people have limited information, willpower and selfishness; policy can use this (nudges).
  • Growing awareness that the economy, society and environment depend on each other.
  • Circular economy: keep materials in use (reduce, reuse, repair, recycle) instead of the linear take → make → dispose model.
Quick check
?A firm leases washing machines instead of selling them, repairs them, and takes them back at the end of their life to reuse the parts. Which idea does this illustrate?
Mission 4Evaluate

Why the answers differ

  • Views on the state rest on beliefs about how well markets work:
    • Do they self-correct quickly (classical) or get stuck (Keynes)?
    • Do markets serve everyone (Smith) or exploit workers (Marx)?
  • And on values: freedom, equality, security. These are normative judgements.
  • Market failure suggests a role for the state; government failure suggests caution.
  • Real economies are mixed: almost all combine markets with some state provision and regulation.
Mission check

?An economist argues: "Factory owners pay workers less than the value their work creates. Competition between owners will bring repeated crises. Ownership of industry should pass to society as a whole." Which thinker's ideas is this economist drawing on, and what role for the state follows?

Each thinker's role for the state follows from their picture of how markets work and whom they serve.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • A PPC shows the maximum combinations of two goods; on it = productively efficient, inside = idle resources, outside = unattainable.
  • Bowed out = increasing opportunity cost (resources not equally suited); straight line = constant.
  • Reallocation = movement along; more or better resources or technology = outward shift (skewed if one good only).

Mission 2

  • Spending by one sector is income to another, so national output = national income = national expenditure.
  • Withdrawals (S, T, M) leave the flow; injections (I, G, X) enter it. Equilibrium: S + T + M = I + G + X.

Mission 3

  • Income is a flow; wealth is a stock. Wealth is usually distributed more unequally than income.
  • The Lorenz curve shows cumulative shares; the further it sags from the line of equality, the higher the Gini coefficient (0 = perfect equality, 1 = perfect inequality).
  • Causes include human capital, wealth ownership, discrimination, power, tax and benefits, and technology.

Mission 4

  • Smith: self-interest plus competition serves society; a limited state.
  • Marx: capitalism exploits workers and brings crises; collective ownership and planning.
  • Keynes: demand can stay too low, so the state manages it; the 1970s counter-revolution stressed money, rules and supply; the 21st century adds psychology and the environment.
