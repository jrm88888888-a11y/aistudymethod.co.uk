Mission 1

Mission 1: Two stars look equally bright in the night sky. How can we tell which one really gives out more light?

Make a guess: Two stars look equally bright in the night sky. How can we tell which one really gives out more light?

Mission 1

Luminosity

  • Luminosity, L: the total power a star emits, in watts.
  • Stars are close to black bodies.
  • L = 4πr²σT⁴: Stefan's law.
  • Same temperature, twice the radius → 4 × the luminosity.
Mission 1

Distance units

  • Astronomical unit (AU): mean Earth–Sun distance, 1.50 × 10¹¹ m.
  • Light-year (ly): distance light travels in one year, 9.46 × 10¹⁵ m.
  • Parsec (pc): distance at which 1 AU subtends an angle of 1 arcsecond.
  • 1 pc = 3.09 × 10¹⁶ m = 3.26 ly.
  • 1 arcsecond = 1/3600 of a degree.
Mission 1

Stellar parallax

  • Observe a nearby star twice, six months apart: from opposite sides of Earth's orbit.
  • It shifts against the very distant background stars.
  • Parallax angle p: half the total shift. It is the angle the Earth–Sun distance (1 AU) subtends at the star.
  • Closer star → bigger p.
  • d (pc) = 1/p (arcsec)
  • Only works for nearby stars: further away, p is too small to measure.
Mission 1

Worked example

  • A star has a parallax angle of 0.20 arcseconds.
  • d = 1/0.20 = 5.0 pc.
  • In metres: 5.0 × 3.09 × 10¹⁶ = 1.5 × 10¹⁷ m. In light-years: 1.55 × 10¹⁷ ÷ 9.46 × 10¹⁵ = 16 ly.
Quick check
?Two measurements of the position of a nearby star against very distant background stars, made six months apart, differ by an angle of 0.0700 arc-second. Take 1 pc = 3.09 × 10¹⁶ m, 1 ly = 9.46 × 10¹⁵ m and 1 AU = 1.50 × 10¹¹ m. What is the distance to the star in AU?
Mission check

?Stars X and Y have the same surface temperature. X has twice the radius of Y. The parallax angle of X is half that of Y. Which statement is correct?

How bright a star looks depends on both its luminosity and its distance. Parallax gives the distance; temperature and size give the luminosity.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why does a passing siren drop in pitch, and what can that shift tell us about speed?

Make a guess: Why does a passing siren drop in pitch, and what can that shift tell us about speed?

Quick check
?Predict first. Imagine a wavefront diagram of the sound from a source moving at constant speed through still air towards a stationary observer X. Each wavefront is drawn as a circle centred on the point where the source was when that wavefront was emitted. Compared with the waves the same source emits when it is at rest, how do the waves reaching X differ?
Mission 2

A moving source

  • A source emits wavefronts at a steady frequency. Each spreads out from where the source was when it was emitted.
  • Moving source: wavefronts bunched ahead (shorter λ), spread out behind (longer λ).
  • Wave speed through the air is unchanged. So ahead: higher f; behind: lower f.
  • Approaching: higher pitch. Receding: lower pitch. Constant velocity → constant (shifted) pitch.
  • Faster relative motion → bigger shift. Same velocity as the listener → no shift.
Mission 2

Doppler shift of light

  • Same effect for light and all EM waves.
  • Receding source: λ longer → red shift. Approaching: λ shorter → blue shift.
  • For v ≪ c:
  • Δf/f = Δλ/λ ≈ v/c
  • v is the radial velocity: the part along the line of sight.
Mission 2

Worked example

  • A hydrogen line: 486.1 nm in the lab, 487.6 nm in a star's spectrum.
  • Δλ = 487.6 − 486.1 = 1.5 nm. Longer → moving away.
  • v = c × Δλ/λ = 3.00 × 10⁸ × 1.5 ÷ 486.1 = 9.3 × 10⁵ m s⁻¹ (about 0.3% of c).
  • v ≪ c, so the approximation is fine.
Quick check
?A spectral line of hydrogen has a wavelength of 656.3 nm when measured in the laboratory. A galaxy is moving directly away from Earth at 3.00 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹. Take c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹. At what wavelength is this line observed in the spectrum of the galaxy?
Mission check

?A calcium absorption line has a wavelength of 393.4 nm in the laboratory. In the spectrum of a distant galaxy the same line is at 401.3 nm. Take c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹. What is the galaxy's velocity relative to Earth?

Approaching: waves bunched, higher f; receding: stretched, lower f. The size of the shift grows with the relative speed, so measuring it measures the speed.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Light from almost every distant galaxy arrives stretched towards the red. How fast is it moving away?

Make a guess: Light from almost every distant galaxy arrives stretched towards the red. How fast is it moving away?

Mission 3

Cosmological vs Doppler red shift

  • Doppler red shift: the galaxy moves through space away from us.
  • Cosmological red shift: space itself expands while the light is travelling. The light's wavelength is stretched with it.
  • The longer the journey, the more stretching: more distant galaxies show larger red shifts.
  • For nearby galaxies (small red shifts), both give Δλ/λ ≈ v/c.
  • A galaxy's own motion through space adds a Doppler shift, towards or away from us.
Quick check
?Andromeda, our nearest large neighbour galaxy, shows a blue shift. Does this contradict the expansion of the universe?
Mission 3

What the red shifts tell us

  • Almost every distant galaxy is red-shifted: almost all are receding.
  • The further away, the faster they recede.
  • Not because we are at the centre: space is expanding everywhere.
  • This is the key evidence for the model of an expanding universe.
Mission check

?A hydrogen absorption line has a laboratory wavelength of 656.28 nm. In the spectrum of a star in our own galaxy, it is observed at 656.08 nm. What is the star's velocity along the line of sight?

The same measurement works for any source: a shift of 0.03% means about 90 km s⁻¹. For nearly all distant galaxies the shift is to the red: they are receding, because space itself is expanding while their light travels to us.

Mission 4

Mission 4: If every distant galaxy is rushing away from us, can we work out how long ago they were all together?

Make a guess: If every distant galaxy is rushing away from us, can we work out how long ago they were all together?

Mission 4

Hubble's law

  • Plot recession speed (from red shift) against distance (measured independently, e.g. from stars of known luminosity).
  • Distant galaxies: a straight line through the origin. Speed ∝ distance.
  • v = H0d
  • H0, the Hubble constant: the gradient of the graph.
  • Twice as far → receding twice as fast.
  • Nearby galaxies scatter about the line: their own motions through space matter more.
Mission 4

Worked example: how far?

  • A galaxy's red shift gives v = 6.0 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹. Take H0 = 2.4 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹.
  • d = v/H0 = 6.0 × 10⁶ ÷ 2.4 × 10⁻¹⁸ = 2.5 × 10²⁴ m.
  • In light-years: 2.5 × 10²⁴ ÷ 9.46 × 10¹⁵ = 2.6 × 10⁸ ly.
  • The light we see left that galaxy about 260 million years ago.
Quick check
?A galaxy at a distance of 1.2 × 10²⁴ m recedes from the Earth at 2800 km s⁻¹. What value of the Hubble constant does this give?
Mission 4

Units of H0

  • Astronomers quote H0 in km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹: extra recession speed (km s⁻¹) for each megaparsec of distance.
  • 1 Mpc = 10⁶ pc = 3.09 × 10²² m.
  • H0 ≈ 70 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹ → 70 × 10³ m s⁻¹ ÷ 3.09 × 10²² m = 2.3 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹.
  • To go back: multiply by 3.09 × 10²² m, then divide by 10³ m km⁻¹.
  • SI unit: s⁻¹ (speed ÷ distance).
Quick check
?A value of the Hubble constant is 2.4 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹. Take 1 Mpc = 3.09 × 10²² m. What is this in km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹?
Mission 4

The age of the universe

  • Run the expansion backwards: every galaxy was once in the same place as every other.
  • Time for a galaxy to cover distance d at speed v: d/v.
  • Hubble's law: d/v = 1/H0, the same for every galaxy.
  • t = H0⁻¹ (assuming constant expansion)
  • H0 = 2.4 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹ → t = 4.2 × 10¹⁷ s ÷ 3.16 × 10⁷ s per year = 1.3 × 10¹⁰ years.
  • Only an estimate: it assumes the galaxies' speeds have never changed.
Quick check
?A measured value of the Hubble constant is 2.0 × 10⁻¹⁸ s⁻¹. What is the estimated age of the universe? (1 year = 3.16 × 10⁷ s)
Mission check

?Galaxy P is 2.0 × 10²⁴ m away and recedes at 5.2 × 10⁶ m s⁻¹. Galaxy Q is 6.0 × 10²⁴ m away and recedes at 1.56 × 10⁷ m s⁻¹. Assuming each has always moved at its present speed, how long ago was each at our position? (1 year = 3.16 × 10⁷ s)

Yes: every galaxy gives about the same distance ÷ speed, so they were all together about 1/H0 ago: roughly 13 to 15 billion years.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Where in the universe did the Big Bang happen?

Make a guess: Where in the universe did the Big Bang happen?

Mission 5

The cosmological principle

  • On large enough scales, the universe is:
  • Homogeneous: the same density of matter everywhere.
  • Isotropic: the same in every direction.
  • And the laws of physics are the same everywhere (universal).
  • So no place is special: there is no centre and no edge.
Mission 5

Space itself expands

  • Galaxies are not flying apart through space. Space-time itself is expanding, carrying them apart.
  • Picture dots on a balloon being blown up: every dot moves away from every other.
  • From any galaxy, the others recede with v ∝ d. Every observer sees Hubble's law.
  • The Big Bang was not an explosion at one point into empty space. Expansion began everywhere at once.
  • Galaxies themselves do not expand: gravity holds them together.
Quick check
?As the universe expands, which of these distances increases?
Mission 5

The Big Bang theory

  • The universe began about 13.8 billion years ago in an extremely hot, dense state.
  • Since then it has been expanding and cooling.
  • Main evidence:
  • Galactic red shift and Hubble's law: the universe is expanding.
  • Cosmic microwave background (CMB): the cooled glow of the hot early universe.
  • Also: the observed amounts of hydrogen and helium match the predictions.
Mission 5

The cosmic microwave background

  • Early universe: so hot that matter was a plasma. Photons scattered constantly: opaque.
  • About 380 000 years in: cooled to about 3000 K. Atoms formed; the universe became transparent.
  • That radiation has travelled ever since, stretched by the expansion (about 1100 times).
  • Now: microwaves, a near-perfect black-body spectrum at 2.7 K. Wien: λmax ≈ 1 mm.
  • It arrives almost equally from every direction (variations about 1 part in 100 000).
  • Discovered in 1965 (Penzias and Wilson). It had been predicted from the Big Bang model.
Quick check
?The cosmic microwave background peaks at about 1.1 mm today. Since it was released, its wavelengths have been stretched by a factor of about 1100. At about what wavelength did it peak when it was released?
Quick check
?Which list puts these events in the order they happened?
Mission 5

From the Big Bang to now

  • First tiny fraction of a second: extremely hot and dense; very rapid expansion (inflation, in current models).
  • About 10⁻⁵ s: quarks join to form protons and neutrons.
  • First few minutes: fusion makes hydrogen and helium nuclei (about 75% H, 25% He by mass).
  • About 380 000 years: atoms form; the CMB is released.
  • A few hundred million years: gravity pulls gas together; the first stars and galaxies form.
  • About 9 billion years: the Solar System forms (4.6 billion years ago).
  • Now: about 13.8 billion years.
Mission check

?Galaxies A and B lie in opposite directions in our sky, each at the same distance d from the Milky Way. Each recedes from us at speed v. What would an astronomer in galaxy A measure for the recession speed of galaxy B?

The Big Bang happened everywhere. Every galaxy sees the others rushing away; none is at the centre.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Stars at a galaxy's edge orbit as fast as stars much nearer its centre. What is holding them in?

Make a guess: Stars at a galaxy's edge orbit as fast as stars much nearer its centre. What is holding them in?

Mission 6

What orbits should look like

  • Circular orbit: gravity provides the centripetal force.
  • GMm/r² = mv²/r → v = √(GM/r), where M = mass inside the orbit.
  • Solar System: almost all the mass is in the Sun, so v falls as r grows. Neptune orbits at about a fifth of Earth's speed.
  • Spiral galaxy: most of the light comes from the crowded centre.
  • So we expect outer stars to be slower: v ∝ 1/√r.
Quick check
?In a spiral galaxy, stars and gas far beyond the region where almost all its stars are orbit at the same speed as stars further in. Using v = √(GM/r), what does this show about the mass inside radius r, in that outer region?
Mission 6

What galaxies actually do

  • Orbital speeds come from the Doppler shift of spectral lines: stars, and the 21 cm radio line of hydrogen gas far beyond the visible disc.
  • A graph of v against r is a rotation curve.
  • Measured: v rises, then stays flat far beyond where the light fades.
  • Flat v: M = v²r/G, so M ∝ r. Mass keeps growing where there is almost no light.
  • Leading explanation: each galaxy sits in a halo of dark matter: mass that emits and absorbs no detectable light, several times the visible mass. (A minority of physicists instead modify the law of gravity at low accelerations.)
Mission 6

What the universe is made of

  • Current model, from satellite maps of the cosmic microwave background and other data:
  • Dark energy ≈ 68% · dark matter ≈ 27% · ordinary matter ≈ 5% of the total energy density.
  • Ordinary matter: atoms. Stars, gas, dust, planets, us.
  • Dark matter: emits and absorbs no light. Detected only by its gravity: galaxy rotation curves, the bending of light from distant galaxies.
  • Dark energy: makes the expansion of the universe accelerate (found from distant supernovae, 1998). Its nature is unknown.
  • About 95% of the universe is in forms we have not identified.
Quick check
?A student says: "Ordinary matter is only about 5% of the universe because the rest is too faint for our telescopes. Bigger telescopes will reveal it." Which response is correct?
Mission check

?The galaxies in a large cluster move so fast that the gravity of all the cluster's visible stars and gas could not hold them together. Yet the cluster has existed for billions of years. Which is the best conclusion?

Speeds reveal mass. The edge of a galaxy orbits fast because a halo of dark matter, several times the mass of the stars, pulls on it.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Luminosity is a star's total power output: L = 4πr²σT⁴.
  • Parallax: d (pc) = 1/p (arcsec); 1 pc = 3.26 ly.

Mission 2

  • Approaching → higher f, shorter λ; receding → lower f, longer λ; bigger relative speed → bigger shift.
  • Light, v ≪ c: Δλ/λ ≈ v/c; red shift means moving away.

Mission 3

  • Red shift: Δλ/λ ≈ v/c for v ≪ c; a longer observed wavelength means the source is receding.
  • Cosmological red shift: space expands while light travels, stretching its wavelength.
  • Almost all distant galaxies are red-shifted: the universe is expanding.

Mission 4

  • Hubble's law: v = H0d; H0 in km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹ or s⁻¹.
  • Age of the universe ≈ 1/H0, roughly 13–15 billion years (assumes constant expansion).

Mission 5

  • Cosmological principle: homogeneous, isotropic, same laws everywhere: no centre, no edge.
  • The Big Bang was the beginning of the expansion of space-time everywhere, not an explosion at a point.
  • CMB: 2.7 K black-body microwaves from every direction, released when atoms formed: key evidence for the Big Bang.

Mission 6

  • Flat galaxy rotation curves: mass inside r grows with r where there is little light: evidence for dark matter.
  • Universe: about 68% dark energy, 27% dark matter, 5% ordinary matter.
