Prose Theme: Women and Society

Component 2 is the Prose paper: one comparative essay on two thematically linked texts, at least one written pre-1900. This lesson teaches the theme of Women and Society as conventions and critical frames, plus the comparative method — for any pairing your centre studies.

  • One comparative essay; AO4 (connections) is heavily weighted.
  • At least one text is pre-1900 — its legal and social world for women is genuinely not ours.
  • The theme is the lens: ideal and fallen images of women, confinement, and resistance.

AO4 · the comparative method

How to compare two prose texts

AO4 wants an argument in which the texts illuminate each other, not two separate essays.

  1. Lead with a thematic proposition: e.g. both texts show that a woman’s virtue is defined for her by others and punished by them.
  2. Interleave in theme-paragraphs (the marriage market; confinement; revolt) drawing on both texts.
  3. Prize the productive difference — where one heroine is destroyed and the other survives to speak.
  4. Respect the pre-1900 text’s law and morality around marriage, property and reputation.

Stem: “Where [A] can only imagine escape as death, [B], written after suffrage, can imagine it as work.”

Theme · images of women

The angel, the fallen woman, the monster

Nineteenth-century fiction repeatedly sorts women into fixed images:

  • The angel in the house — the pure, self-sacrificing domestic ideal (the phrase from Coventry Patmore’s poem).
  • The fallen woman — punished, often fatally, for sexual transgression under a brutal double standard.
  • The monster / madwoman — the woman whose anger or desire is pathologised and confined.

Sandra Gilbert and Susan Gubar, in The Madwoman in the Attic (1979), argue that Victorian women writers split the female self into the angel and the monster, the monster often carrying the author’s own rebellious energy.

Comparative angle: ask which images each text endorses and which it exposes as a trap.

Theme · confinement and the double standard

Marriage, property and the cage

A second strand is constraint:

  • The marriage market — marriage as economic survival, not romance; the woman as property to be transferred.
  • Legal erasure — under coverture, a wife’s legal identity was absorbed into her husband’s; the Married Women’s Property Acts (1870, 1882) only slowly changed this.
  • The domestic interior — the home rendered as a cage; confinement literal (the locked room) and social.
  • The double standard — sexual conduct that ruins a woman and barely touches a man.

Simone de Beauvoir (The Second Sex, 1949) argues woman is constructed as the Other against a male norm: “one is not born, but rather becomes, a woman.”

Theme · resistance and the woman question

Revolt, the New Woman and reform

The theme also stages resistance:

  • The woman question — nineteenth-century debate over women’s education, work, marriage and the vote.
  • The New Woman — the late-Victorian figure seeking independence, education and self-determination, often demonised in the fiction of the time.
  • Voice and authorship — Virginia Woolf’s A Room of One’s Own (1929) argues a woman needs money and private space to write at all.

Showalter’s map: Elaine Showalter (A Literature of Their Own) traces women’s writing through feminine, feminist and female phases — a useful frame for comparing texts of different periods.

AO3 · context

The changing law and life of women

Track how women’s legal and social position differs across your texts’ periods:

  • Coverture and reform — from a wife’s legal invisibility to the Property Acts and, later, the vote (1918, 1928).
  • Education and work — the slow opening of universities and professions.
  • Waves of feminism — first-wave suffrage; second-wave critique of the private sphere.

Weld it: because the earlier heroine cannot legally own her earnings, her only rebellion is refusal; the later heroine can leave — an AO3 difference that drives an AO4 comparison.

AO5 · interpretation

Feminist criticism, used precisely

Feminist criticism is the natural lens, but it must yield a textual claim:

  • Representation — who is granted interiority and who is only looked at?
  • Structure — does the plot reward conformity and punish desire?
  • Voice — does the narration ventriloquise or genuinely give the woman speech?

Discipline: not “this is patriarchal”, but “the novel grants the heroine a rich inner voice for two hundred pages, then silences her with a marriage in a single sentence — form itself enacts the closing of her options.”
