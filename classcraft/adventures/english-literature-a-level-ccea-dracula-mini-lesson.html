The novel in four movements

Dracula (1897) is told in 27 chapters plus a closing Note.

ChaptersWhat happens
I–IVHarker, a young solicitor, travels to Castle Dracula to sell the Count a London house, Carfax. He finds he is a prisoner, meets three vampire women and sees the Count sail for England in earth-filled boxes.
V–XVIAt Whitby a ship runs aground in a storm, a dead man lashed to the wheel. Lucy sleepwalks and sickens; Van Helsing gives her blood, but she dies (Chapter XII). Undead, she preys on children until her fiancé Arthur stakes her (Chapter XVI).
XVII–XXIVMina collates the papers; the men sterilise the boxes. Dracula attacks Mina (Chapter XXI), flees London and sails home.
XXV–XXVIIUsing Mina's hypnotic link, the band chases him east; Harker and Quincey Morris destroy him at sunset, and Quincey dies. A Note, seven years later, closes the book.

Dracula · the epistolary novelScreen 5

A novel made of documents

  • An epistolary novel is told through letters; Dracula widens this into a documentary collage: journals, letters, telegrams, cuttings, a ship's log, a phonograph diary.
  • Like Collins's The Moonstone (1868), it uses several narrators.
  • Each writer has limited knowledge, so readers often know more: dramatic irony.
  • The Count writes no document: we meet him only through his enemies' records.

?Journals, letters, cuttings: why does Dracula never get a diary?

The novel's documents belong to the people hunting Dracula, and Mina's typing turns them into one ordered case against him. Dracula never speaks except as they record him, so he stays a mystery and a threat. The closing Note admits there is "hardly one authentic document", a final twist that leaves the record's truth in doubt.

Dracula · invasion and the foreignScreen 1

The 1890s: who is coming?

  • Invasion literature: after The Battle of Dorking (1871), stories of Britain overrun by foreign forces were bestsellers; Le Queux's The Great War in England in 1897 came out in 1894.
  • From 1881 about 150,000 Jewish refugees from Eastern European pogroms settled in Britain, many in London's East End; calls to curb migration led to the Aliens Act 1905.
  • Dracula (1897) brings the invader by sea, unopposed.

Dracula · invasion and the foreignScreen 4

The route of the invasion

  • The Count ships himself from Varna on the Demeter, which runs aground at Whitby in a storm (Chapter VII); a great dog leaps ashore.
  • He sends fifty boxes of "common earth" to Carfax, near Purfleet, then spreads lairs to Mile End, Bermondsey and Piccadilly.
  • His blood makes victims his kind, an "ever-widening circle".
  • Mina's verdict: "So he came to London to invade a new land" (Chapter XXV).

Dracula · invasion and the foreignScreen 7

Reading the prejudice

  • Harker's travel journal sets "the West" against "the East": the East as superstitious and backward.
  • Stephen Arata reads the novel as reverse colonisation: the coloniser fears being colonised, a fear mixed with guilt, since the Count mirrors Britain's own conquests.
  • Antisemitism: in Chapter XXVI Harker describes Hildesheim, a Jewish agent, through crude stereotypes of looks and money. Jack Halberstam links the Count's nose, gold and rootlessness to antisemitic caricature; others stress his suspicion of all foreigners.

The Count studies England, its law, its accent and even its railway timetables, so that he can arrive "like the rest" and spread from within. Stoker turns 1890s fears of invasion and immigration into a vampire who reverses the direction of empire, and the novel's own prejudices about the East and about Jews are part of what you analyse.

Science, faith and a foreigner

  • Van Helsing joins modern science (transfusion, morphia, hypnotism, Charcot) with Catholic ritual (crucifix, Sacred Wafer) and folklore (garlic, the stake).

Dracula · Van Helsing and the huntersScreen 7

Reading the band critically

  • Craft argues that the men's desire for one another can be expressed only through women: their blood mingles in Lucy's veins through Van Helsing's transfusions.
  • The band's violence falls on women's bodies: Arthur stakes Lucy with "high duty" in his face (Chapter XVI).
  • Mina is shut out to protect her, which leaves her open to attack (XDRA4).
  • Some readers see brave fellowship; others see a patriarchal brotherhood defending itself.

Van Helsing names the band's strength as "power of combination": shared records, science, faith and an unselfish cause against one selfish will. Stoker blesses that brotherhood as a crusade, but critics such as Craft show how it is built on men's bonds with each other and on control of women's bodies.

Mission 4

Mina mocks the New Woman. So why does she do the men's work?

Make a guess: Mina mocks the New Woman. So why does she do the men's work?

Dracula · Mina, Lucy and the New WomanScreen 1

The New Woman

  • In 1894 the novelist Sarah Grand used the term New Woman for independent women seeking change; Ouida replied in an article of that title.
  • New Women sought education, paid work, freer dress and cycling, and choice in marriage and sexual partners.
  • Mina is an assistant schoolmistress who learns shorthand and the typewriter to be "useful to Jonathan".
  • Lucy, wealthy and idle, receives three proposals in one day (Chapter V).

Dracula · Mina, Lucy and the New WomanScreen 7

The cost of shutting her out

  • Excluded on 30 September, Mina is visited by the Count that night. Next day she writes, "It is strange to me to be kept in the dark" (Chapter XIX).
  • Later her hypnotic link to him guides the chase.
  • Carol Senf reads the novel as Stoker's response to the New Woman: the sexual Lucy is destroyed; Mina is the ideal set against the type.
  • The Note calls her "a brave and gallant woman", a mother.

?Mina mocks the New Woman. So why does she do the men's work?

Mina's shorthand, typing and timetables make her the band's most useful mind, yet she mocks the New Woman and is praised only as an exception with "man's brain". The novel admires her skills while keeping them inside marriage, and it destroys Lucy, the woman who wished she could "marry three men". The strongest answers weigh both sides.

Mission 5

Harker is terrified of the vampire women. So why does he wait?

Make a guess: Harker is terrified of the vampire women. So why does he wait?

Dracula · desire and the bodyScreen 1

Reading desire in a Victorian novel

  • Stoker never describes sex. Desire is displaced onto mouths, lips, teeth, blood and the bite.
  • Transgression: crossing a moral or bodily boundary; the vampire crosses many (life and death, male and female roles, marriage).
  • Ambivalence: wanting and fearing the same thing.
  • Context: Wilde's trials in 1895 showed what public discovery of homosexual acts could cost; Stoker had begun the novel's notes in 1890, though.

Dracula · desire and the bodyScreen 4

Inversions

  • In Chapter III the women advance and Harker lies passive: the roles Victorian readers expected are inverted.
  • The Count claims him: "This man belongs to me!"
  • Christopher Craft argues the novel's deepest fear is the Count's desire for Harker, which the plot turns aside onto women.
  • Elaine Showalter sets the novel in a fin de siècle of 'sexual anarchy', where New Women and male homosexuality unsettled gender roles.

Dracula · desire and the bodyScreen 7

Punishment and restoration

  • Arthur stakes Lucy (Chapter XVI); Seward calls her "the Thing" and pictures Arthur "like a figure of Thor".
  • Afterwards her face shows "unequalled sweetness and purity": order is restored.
  • Phyllis Roth argues much of the novel's appeal comes from its hostility to female sexuality; Showalter finds the staking's sexual meaning hard to miss.
  • The Count calls Mina "flesh of my flesh", echoing Adam's words of Eve in Genesis, an image of marriage; she cries "Unclean".

Mission checkScreen 9

?Harker is terrified of the vampire women. So why does he wait?

Harker waits because Stoker fuses terror with desire: "some longing and at the same time some deadly fear". The vampires make desire visible through mouths and blood and invert expected roles, and the novel punishes that desire most harshly in women, as Lucy's fate shows.

Mission 6

Typewriters, phonographs, transfusions. Can 1890s science beat him?

Make a guess: Typewriters, phonographs, transfusions. Can 1890s science beat him?

Dracula · science and degenerationScreen 1

The modern toolkit

  • Records: shorthand (Harker, Mina), the typewriter with carbon "manifold" copies (Mina), Seward's phonograph diary on wax cylinders; Edison's phonograph dates from 1877.
  • Movement: telegrams, railway timetables, Winchester rifles.
  • Medicine: four blood transfusions for Lucy, before blood groups were discovered in 1901, so each was a gamble; morphia; hypnotism, where Van Helsing mourns the French expert Charcot.

Dracula · science and degenerationScreen 4

Degeneration and the "criminal type"

  • Cesare Lombroso's Criminal Man (1876) claimed born criminals were atavistic throwbacks with visible physical marks.
  • Max Nordau's Degeneration (1892–93; in English 1895), dedicated to Lombroso, warned that modern society was in decline.
  • The Count's eyebrows almost meet, his ears are pointed and there are hairs in his palms (Chapter II).
  • Van Helsing calls his mind a "child-brain"; Seward labels Renfield "zoöphagous".

The limits of science

  • Van Helsing: "it is the fault of our science that it wants to explain all" (Chapter XIV).

  • The Count learns too: he is "creeping into knowledge experimentally".
  • Victory needs the Host, the crucifix, knives at sunset and Mina's hypnotic link.
  • The Note admits the record is "nothing but a mass of typewriting".

Mission checkScreen 9

?Typewriters, phonographs, transfusions. Can 1890s science beat him?

Science helps the band to record, predict and travel, and degeneration theory lets Mina read the Count as a "criminal type". But Harker's warning holds: "the old centuries had, and have, powers of their own". Stoker's hunters win only by joining the modern with the ancient, and the Note's "mass of typewriting" leaves even the modern record in doubt.

Dracula · critical readingsScreen 2

Empire: reverse colonisation

  • Arata: the coloniser fears being colonised, and the fear carries guilt.
  • The Count studies English, law and Bradshaw so he can pass: a mirror of the Western traveller who studies the East.
  • He is vigorous; his English opponents are pale and drained.
  • Test it: Mina's son is born after she has drunk the Count's blood (Chapter XXI). Whose blood runs in the new generation?
Dracula · critical readingsScreen 3

Mothers and women

  • Phyllis Roth reads Lucy and Mina as one figure, the mother, in one story told twice with different endings: the sexual woman destroyed, the safer one saved.
  • Carol Senf reads the novel as Stoker's response to the New Woman: Lucy's staking defeats the sexually dangerous woman and restores male authority.
  • Elaine Showalter places the novel in a fin de siècle of 'sexual anarchy' and finds the staking's sexual meaning plain.
Dracula · critical readingsScreen 4

Desire between men

  • Craft argues the novel's deepest fear is the Count's desire for Harker ("This man belongs to me!"), which the plot turns aside onto women.
  • The men's blood mixes in Lucy's veins: their bond runs through her body.
  • Talia Schaffer reads the novel against Wilde's trials (1895) and Stoker's friendship with Wilde; others note Stoker was writing from 1890.
Dracula · critical readingsScreen 5

Money: Moretti

  • Franco Moretti reads the Count as an aristocrat only in name: his thirst for blood is capital's drive to accumulate.
  • He behaves like a rational investor, putting his hoard to work to expand his power.
  • Textual support: he makes Harker's bed himself, as there are no servants; a "great heap of gold" lies in his castle; cut, his coat spills bank-notes and "a stream of gold".

?Short analysis, ungraded.

In 3–4 sentences, apply one critical reading from this mission to one quotation from Dracula. Say what the reading reveals and where it strains.

Model answer

Moretti reads the Count as capital rather than a true aristocrat, and the moment when Harker's knife releases "a bundle of bank-notes and a stream of gold" supports him: the vampire's body is literally full of money. The image makes his threat economic as well as physical. Yet the reading strains in Chapter III, where the horror is desire, not wealth. Moretti explains how the Count acts in London better than why the women frighten Harker.

Mission checkScreen 9

?One vampire, five critics. Which reading is the right one?

No single reading owns Dracula. Empire, capital, motherhood and desire each light up different evidence, and each strains somewhere. Strong answers use a critic as a claim to test, not a verdict to quote.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Dracula is a documentary novel: dated journals, letters, cuttings and a phonograph diary, each from a writer with limited knowledge, which creates dramatic irony.
  • Mina's typing turns scattered records into evidence; the Count never writes a document of his own, so he is known only through his enemies.
  • The prefatory note claims the records are "exactly contemporary", but the closing Note admits there is "hardly one authentic document".
  • Dracula draws on 1890s invasion fiction and fears about immigration: the invader arrives by sea and spreads through London's lairs.
  • The Count studies English to pass unnoticed; Harker's "filthy leech" simile makes the threat parasitic.
  • Arata's "reverse colonisation" reading sees fear and guilt about empire; the novel's xenophobic and antisemitic elements should be named and analysed critically.
  • The band joins professions and classes: a foreign professor, a doctor, a lord, a Texan and a solicitor, with Mina ordering their papers.
  • Van Helsing mixes science, Catholic ritual and folklore; he presents the hunt as a crusade.
  • Craft's "Crew of Light" reading sees the band's bonds routed through women; its violence falls on Lucy's body.
  • Use a critic's view of the band as one interpretation to test against the text (AO5).
  • The New Woman (term used by Sarah Grand, 1894) stood for education, work and choice; Mina has the skills but mocks the type.
  • Lucy, who jokes about marrying three men, is drained, made undead and staked; Mina is attacked, saved and made a mother.
  • Roth reads the two plots as one story told twice; Senf sees Lucy's staking as the defeat of the dangerous New Woman. Weigh them against how much the band needs Mina.
  • A stimulus statement on Mina or Lucy invites you to weigh such readings (AO5).
  • Desire is displaced onto mouths, lips and blood; the vampire transgresses the boundaries of life, gender and marriage.
  • Chapter III fuses fear and longing; Lucy's undead desire is shown as virtue turned inside out, then "corrected" by her staking.
  • Craft reads a hidden desire between men; Showalter places the novel in fin-de-siècle 'sexual anarchy'; Roth stresses hostility to female sexuality. The Wilde trials (1895) are a reading context, not proof of Stoker's intention.
  • Test each critic against the text: where does a reading fit, and where does it strain? (AO5)
  • The hunters' modern toolkit: shorthand, typewriter, phonograph, telegrams, timetables, transfusion and hypnotism.
  • Degeneration theory (Lombroso, Nordau) shapes the Count's "criminal type" and Van Helsing's "child-brain"; Mina uses it to predict his flight.
  • Science alone fails; old belief and ritual finish the work, and Harker's "mere 'modernity'" line sets up the tension.
  • 1897 reviewers set Dracula in the Gothic tradition; later critics read it through empire (Arata), capital (Moretti), psychoanalysis (Roth), queer theory (Craft) and gender history (Showalter, Senf).
  • Each reading rests on particular evidence: Bradshaw and invasion; gold and accumulation; Lucy's and Mina's fates; "This man belongs to me!".
  • Use a critic as a claim to test: where does it fit, and where does it strain?
  • AO5 is assessed directly: weigh at least two readings in your answer.

Key quotations to learn

  • all the records chosen are exactly contemporary (Prefatory note) Claims the record is immediate and true.
  • Kept in shorthand. (Ch. I, heading to Harker's Journal) The first document is private, coded writing.
  • In this matter dates are everything (Ch. XVII, Mina's Journal) Ordering the papers becomes a weapon.
  • I used manifold, and so took three copies of the diary (Ch. XVII, Mina's Journal) The novel shows its own making.
  • there is hardly one authentic document; nothing but a mass of typewriting (Note, Harker) The ending undercuts the claim to proof.
  • We want no proofs; we ask none to believe us! (Note, Van Helsing) The novel ends by giving up on evidence.
  • we were leaving the West and entering the East (Ch. I, Harker's Journal) Frames the journey as crossing a border of civilisations.
  • But a stranger in a strange land, he is no one (Ch. II, the Count) He knows that to rule he must pass unseen.
  • I am content if I am like the rest (Ch. II, the Count) The threat of the invader who blends in.
  • He lay like a filthy leech (Ch. IV, Harker's Journal) Invasion as parasitism.
  • amongst its teeming millions, satiate his lust for blood (Ch. IV, Harker's Journal) London as the host body.
  • So he came to London to invade a new land. (Ch. XXV, Mina (Seward's Diary)) Mina names the plot as an invasion.
  • He is a philosopher and a metaphysician, and one of the most advanced scientists of his day (Ch. IX, Seward's letter to Arthur) Van Helsing joins science and philosophy.
  • We have on our side power of combination (Ch. XVIII, Van Helsing (Mina's Journal)) The band's strength is unity.
  • it is the fault of our science that it wants to explain all (Ch. XIV, Van Helsing (Seward's Diary)) He rebukes narrow science.
  • Thus are we ministers of God’s own wish (Ch. XXIV, Van Helsing (Mina's Journal)) The hunt is sanctified.
  • we go out as the old knights of the Cross (Ch. XXIV, Van Helsing (Mina's Journal)) The chase east as a crusade.
  • high duty seemed to shine through it (Ch. XVI, Seward's Diary, of Arthur) Violence presented as duty.
  • Why can’t they let a girl marry three men, or as many as want her, and save all this trouble? (Ch. V, Lucy's letter to Mina) Lucy's joke about desire, then "heresy".
  • she will do the proposing herself (Ch. VIII, Mina's Journal) Mina imagines and mocks the New Woman.
  • She has man’s brain (Ch. XVIII, Van Helsing (Seward's Diary)) Praise that codes intelligence as male.
  • it is no part for a woman (Ch. XVIII, Van Helsing (Seward's Diary)) The exclusion that leaves Mina exposed.
  • It is strange to me to be kept in the dark (Ch. XIX, Mina's Journal) Mina feels the exclusion.
  • Unclean, unclean! (Ch. XXI, Mina (Seward's Diary)) Mina sees herself as contaminated.
  • some longing and at the same time some deadly fear (Ch. III, Harker's Journal) Fear and desire fused.
  • an agony of delightful anticipation (Ch. III, Harker's Journal) The oxymoron of the scene.
  • This man belongs to me! (Ch. III, the Count) The Count claims Harker.
  • the purity to voluptuous wantonness (Ch. XVI, Seward's Diary) Female desire as inverted virtue.
  • He looked like a figure of Thor (Ch. XVI, Seward's Diary) Arthur's staking made heroic.
  • flesh of my flesh; blood of my blood; kin of my kin (Ch. XXI, the Count to Mina (Seward's Diary)) A parody of the marriage bond.
  • It is nineteenth century up-to-date with a vengeance. (Ch. III, Harker's Journal) Modernity, proudly asserted.
  • the old centuries had, and have, powers of their own which mere “modernity” cannot kill (Ch. III, Harker's Journal) The past survives the present.
  • it is the fault of our science that it wants to explain all (Ch. XIV, Van Helsing (Seward's Diary)) The limits of materialist science.
  • The Count is a criminal and of criminal type. (Ch. XXV, Mina (Seward's Diary)) Degeneration theory applied.
  • Nordau and Lombroso would so classify him (Ch. XXV, Mina (Seward's Diary)) The novel names its sources.
  • that big child-brain of his is working (Ch. XXIII, Van Helsing (Seward's Diary)) The Count learns and grows.
  • reading, of all things in the world, an English Bradshaw’s Guide (Ch. II, Harker's Journal) Evidence for reverse colonisation.

  • This man belongs to me! (Ch. III, the Count) Craft's key moment.
  • Why can’t they let a girl marry three men (Ch. V, Lucy's letter) Feminist and psychoanalytic readings of Lucy.
