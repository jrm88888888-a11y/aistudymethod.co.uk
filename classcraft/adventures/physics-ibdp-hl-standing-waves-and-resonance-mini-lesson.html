Mission 1

Mission 1: Why does a flute or a guitar string sound only certain notes, never the ones in between?

Make a guess: Why does a flute or a guitar string sound only certain notes, never the ones in between?

Mission 1

Two waves, opposite directions

  • Two waves with the same frequency, wavelength and amplitude travel in opposite directions and superpose.
  • Usually a wave and its own reflection from a fixed end or the end of a pipe.
  • Add the displacements at each point at t = 0, T/4, T/2: some points never move; others swing the most.
  • The pattern does not travel. No energy is carried along it.
  • This is a stationary (standing) wave.
Mission 1

Nodes and antinodes

  • Node: zero amplitude at all times. Antinode: maximum amplitude.
  • Node to next node: λ/2. Node to next antinode: λ/4.
  • Amplitude varies with position: zero at nodes, largest at antinodes.
  • Between two neighbouring nodes, all points move in phase.
  • Points on opposite sides of a node are in antiphase (phase difference π rad).
Mission 1

Two kinds of wave compared

  • Energy: progressive carries it along; stationary does not (stored, swapping between kinetic and potential).
  • Amplitude: progressive, the same everywhere (no losses); stationary, varies from zero (node) to maximum (antinode).
  • Phase: progressive, changes steadily along the wave; stationary, in phase within a loop, antiphase across a node.
  • Wavelength: progressive, crest to crest; stationary, twice the node-to-node distance.
Quick check
?A string fixed at both ends vibrates in a standing wave. Its nodes are at x = 0, 0.40 m, 0.80 m and 1.20 m, measured from one end. Points P, Q and R on the string are at x = 0.10 m, 0.20 m and 0.70 m. Which statement about the oscillations of P, Q and R is correct?
Mission 1

Strings

  • A fixed end must be a node.
  • Fixed at both ends: a whole number of loops fits, L = nλ/2.
  • A free (loose) end, e.g. a ring sliding on a smooth rod, is an antinode.
  • Fixed–free: L = λ/4, 3λ/4, … → odd harmonics only, f = nv/4L (n = 1, 3, 5, …). Free–free: like fixed–fixed, L = nλ/2.
  • n = 1: the first harmonic (one loop, λ = 2L). Then n = 2, 3, …: second, third harmonics.
  • Wave speed v fixed by the string, so f = v/λ = nv/2L: only these frequencies "fit".
Mission 1

Air in pipes

  • Closed end: air cannot move → displacement node. Open end: displacement antinode.
  • Open at both ends: antinode–antinode, L = nλ/2, all harmonics: f = nv/2L.
  • Closed at both ends: node–node, also L = nλ/2.
  • Closed at one end: node–antinode, L = λ/4, 3λ/4, 5λ/4, …
  • Closed at one end: odd harmonics only, f = nv/4L, n = 1, 3, 5, …
Mission 1

Worked example

  • A pipe 0.50 m long; speed of sound 340 m s⁻¹.
  • Closed at one end: λ = 4L = 2.0 m → f = 340 ÷ 2.0 = 170 Hz.
  • Next: third harmonic, λ = 4L/3 → f = 3 × 170 = 510 Hz. No 340 Hz.
  • Open at both ends: λ = 2L = 1.0 m → 340 Hz, then 680 Hz, 1020 Hz, …
Quick check
?A pipe of length 0.68 m is closed at one end and open at the other. The speed of sound in the air in the pipe is 340 m s⁻¹. Which gives, in increasing order, the lowest frequencies at which the air in the pipe resonates?
Mission check

?The diagram shows the envelope of a standing wave on a string of length L = 1.20 m that is fixed at both ends. What is the wavelength of the waves on the string?

The ends must be nodes, so only whole numbers of half-wavelengths fit. With v fixed, only the matching frequencies can build up: the instrument's notes.

Mission 2

Free and forced oscillations

  • Free oscillation: displace it, let go, no further force. It oscillates at its natural frequency, f0.
  • Forced oscillation: a periodic driving force keeps acting.
  • Once steady, a forced system oscillates at the driving frequency, not at f0.
Quick check
?A lightly damped mass–spring system has a natural frequency of 2.0 Hz. It is driven by a periodic force of constant amplitude with a frequency of 3.5 Hz. Which describes its oscillation once the amplitude has become steady?
Mission 2

Resonance

  • Resonance: driving frequency = natural frequency → maximum amplitude.
  • Why: each push is in step with the motion, so the driver does positive work on every cycle. Energy builds up.
  • Amplitude–driving frequency graph: small at low frequency, a peak at f0, then falls towards zero at high frequency.
  • The peak height is limited by damping: the amplitude stops growing when energy lost per cycle = energy supplied per cycle.
Quick check
?In its spin cycle, a washing machine's drum speeds up steadily. At one rotation rate the whole machine shakes violently; at higher rates it runs smoothly again. Why does it shake at that one rate?
Mission 2

Damping

  • Damping: a resistive force (air resistance, friction, a fluid) takes energy out of the oscillation. The energy ends up as thermal energy of the surroundings.
  • Undamped: kinetic ⇄ potential energy, total energy constant. Damped: total energy falls, so the amplitude falls.
  • Light damping: keeps oscillating; amplitude decays gradually; period almost unchanged.
  • Critical damping: returns to equilibrium in the shortest time, without oscillating.
  • Heavy damping (also called over-damping): no oscillation; creeps back more slowly than critical.
Quick check
?The graph shows displacement–time curves X, Y and Z for the same mass–spring system, released from rest at the same displacement with three different amounts of damping. Which statement about the curves is correct?
Mission 2

Damping changes the resonance curve

  • More damping:
    • lower peak (smaller maximum amplitude),
    • broader, flatter curve: the resonance is less sharp,
    • peak at a slightly lower frequency.
  • Light damping: tall, sharp peak, very close to f0.
  • Far from f0 (very low or very high driving frequency), damping makes little difference.
Quick check
?The same oscillator is driven by a periodic force of the same constant amplitude in two experiments, X and Y, with different damping. In X the maximum amplitude is 25 mm, at 5.00 Hz, and the amplitude falls to half this value at 4.82 Hz and 5.17 Hz. In Y the maximum amplitude is 3.4 mm, at 4.89 Hz, and the amplitude falls to half this value at 3.3 Hz and 6.1 Hz. Which conclusion do the data support?
Mission 2

Resonance: useful and harmful

  • Useful: tuning a radio to one station; musical instruments (strings and air columns); playground swings.
  • Harmful: footbridges and buildings driven by walkers, wind or earthquakes; machine parts and car panels rattling at certain engine speeds.
  • 2000: London's Millennium Bridge swayed sideways as crowds fell into step with it. It closed after two days; dampers were fitted.
  • Engineers prevent harmful resonance by adding damping, or by moving f0 away from the driving frequency (change stiffness or mass).
Mission check

?A singer shatters a wine glass by singing one steady note close to it. Which condition is essential for this to happen?

Same idea as the swing. A driver at the natural frequency adds energy on every cycle, so small pushes build a large amplitude; at other frequencies the pushes work against the motion for part of each cycle.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Stationary wave: two identical waves travelling opposite ways; nodes λ/2 apart; no energy carried along.
  • Fixed–fixed string or open–open pipe: L = nλ/2, all harmonics. Node at one end, antinode at the other: odd harmonics only.

Mission 2

  • A forced oscillator settles at the driving frequency; resonance (maximum amplitude) happens when the driving frequency equals the natural frequency.
  • More damping: lower, broader resonance peak at a slightly lower frequency. Light damping oscillates and decays; critical returns fastest without oscillating; heavy (over-) damping returns slowly.
