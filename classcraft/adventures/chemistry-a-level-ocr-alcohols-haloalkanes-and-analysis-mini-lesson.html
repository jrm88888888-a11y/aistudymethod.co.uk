Mission 1

Mission 1: How does warm sodium hydroxide turn a bromoalkane into an alcohol?

Make a guess: How does warm sodium hydroxide turn a bromoalkane into an alcohol?

Mission 1

Haloalkanes

  • OCR calls them haloalkanes.
  • Halogen X is more electronegative than carbon: the C–X bond is polar, Cδ+–Xδ−.
  • The δ+ carbon can be attacked by electron-rich species.
  • Classify by the carbon bonded to X: bonded to 1, 2 or 3 other carbons.
  • Primary: CH3CH2CH2Br · secondary: CH3CHBrCH3 · tertiary: (CH3)3CBr.
Mission 1

Nucleophiles

  • A nucleophile is an electron-pair donor.
  • It donates both electrons of a new covalent bond, usually from a lone pair.
  • Examples: OH⁻, CN⁻, NH3, H2O. No charge needed: NH3 and H2O are neutral.
  • "Nucleus-loving": it is attracted to a δ+ atom.
Quick check
?Which set contains only species that can act as nucleophiles?
Mission 1

Heterolytic fission and curly arrows

  • Heterolytic fission: both bonding electrons go to one atom, so ions form.
  • The more electronegative atom takes the pair and becomes negative: C–Br → C⁺ + Br⁻.
  • A curly arrow (two barbs) shows an electron pair moving.
  • Forming a bond: arrow from a lone pair (or a bond) to the atom it bonds to.
  • Breaking a bond: arrow from the bond to the atom that takes the pair.
  • Arrows may also start from a negative charge. Always show relevant dipoles.
Mission 1

The mechanism, step by step

  • Bromoethane + warm aqueous NaOH (or KOH): CH3CH2Br + OH⁻ → CH3CH2OH + Br⁻
  • 1 Draw the dipole: Cδ+–Brδ−.
  • 2 Arrow from a lone pair on O of OH⁻ to the δ+ C: the new C–O bond.
  • 3 Arrow from the C–Br bond to Br: Br leaves as Br⁻, taking the pair.
  • Products: ethanol and bromide ion.
  • Name: nucleophilic substitution. With OH⁻ it is also called hydrolysis.
Quick check
?Hydroxide ions react with 1-chloropropane: CH3CH2CH2Cl + OH⁻ → CH3CH2CH2OH + Cl⁻. Which describes the curly arrows correctly?
Mission 1

Which reacts fastest? Bond enthalpy

  • The C–X bond must break, so its strength controls the rate.
  • Bond enthalpies: C–F 467 · C–Cl 346 · C–Br 290 · C–I 228 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Rate: iodo > bromo > chloro (fluoro hardly reacts).
  • Polarity predicts the opposite order (C–F most polar), so bond enthalpy wins.
Mission 1

Comparing rates with silver nitrate

  • Add the haloalkane to aqueous silver nitrate in ethanol (ethanol dissolves both). Warm all tubes equally, e.g. 50 °C water bath.
  • Water is the nucleophile: R–X + H2O → R–OH + H⁺ + X⁻
  • X⁻ meets Ag⁺: Ag⁺(aq) + X⁻(aq) → AgX(s). Time how long until the precipitate appears.
  • Precipitates: AgI yellow (first) · AgBr cream · AgCl white (last).
  • The precipitate colour also identifies the halogen.
Quick check
?1-chlorobutane, 1-bromobutane and 1-iodobutane are each added to aqueous silver nitrate in ethanol at 50 °C. Which tube shows a precipitate first, and what colour is it?
Mission 1

Cyanide: a longer chain

  • Reagent: KCN in ethanol (with a little water), heat under reflux.
  • CH3CH2Br + CN⁻ → CH3CH2CN + Br⁻. Product: propanenitrile.
  • Arrow from the lone pair on C of CN⁻ to the δ+ carbon; arrow from C–Br to Br.
  • A nitrile forms, and the carbon chain grows by one.
  • KCN is very toxic.
Mission 1

Ammonia: making amines

  • Reagent: excess ammonia in ethanol, heated in a sealed tube (under pressure).
  • Step 1: lone pair on N of NH3 → δ+ C; C–Br → Br. Forms CH3CH2NH3⁺ + Br⁻.
  • Step 2: a second NH3 removes H⁺ from N: lone pair on N → H; N–H bond → N.
  • Overall: CH3CH2Br + 2NH3 → CH3CH2NH2 + NH4Br. Product: ethylamine, a primary amine.
  • The amine has a lone pair too, so it can attack again: secondary → tertiary amines.
  • Learn the reagent, conditions and product; the arrows show how the amine forms.
Quick check
?Bromoethane is heated with excess ammonia in ethanol: CH3CH2Br + 2NH3 → CH3CH2NH2 + NH4Br. What does the second NH3 molecule do?
Mission check

?Iodoethane, CH3CH2I, is warmed with aqueous sodium hydroxide, and ethanol forms. Which statement about the electron pairs is correct?

OH⁻ donates a lone pair to the δ+ carbon; the C–X pair leaves with the halogen as X⁻. That is how the warm alkali turns a bromoalkane into an alcohol.

Mission 2

Mission 2: What does acid do to an alcohol that lets its OH group break away?

Make a guess: What does acid do to an alcohol that lets its OH group break away?

Mission 2

Alcohols from alkenes and steam

  • Hydration: water adds across C=C.
  • C2H4(g) + H2O(g) ⇌ C2H5OH(g)
  • Catalyst: phosphoric acid, H3PO4, on a solid support.
  • Typical industrial conditions: about 300 °C, about 6–7 MPa.
  • Only about 5% converts per pass; unreacted ethene is recycled.
  • Unsymmetrical alkenes give two alcohols: propene → mainly propan-2-ol.
  • Continuous process; very pure ethanol; but ethene comes from crude oil (finite).
Mission 2

Why acid lets the OH go

  • OH⁻ is a strong base: it is a poor leaving group. The C–O bond will not break on its own.
  • H⁺ bonds to a lone pair on the O: –OH becomes –OH2⁺.
  • Now the C–O bond breaks and a neutral, stable H2O molecule leaves.
  • Then either:
  • a neighbouring C loses H⁺ → alkene (elimination)
  • a halide ion bonds to the C → haloalkane (substitution)
Mission 2

Dehydration: alcohol → alkene

  • Heat the alcohol with concentrated H3PO4 (or conc H2SO4).
  • C2H5OH → CH2=CH2 + H2O
  • Cyclohexanol → cyclohexene + H2O: the alkene distils off as it forms.
  • If the OH carbon has different neighbours, several alkenes can form, including E/Z isomers.
Quick check
?Butan-2-ol, CH3CH(OH)CH2CH3, is heated with concentrated phosphoric acid. How many different alkenes can form?
Mission 2

OH → halogen: halide ions plus acid

  • Alcohol + hydrogen halide → haloalkane + water.
  • CH3CH2OH + HBr → CH3CH2Br + H2O
  • HBr is made in the flask: NaBr + H2SO4 → NaHSO4 + HBr.
  • OCR example: NaBr/H2SO4, warm.
Quick check
?Propan-2-ol is warmed with potassium bromide and sulfuric acid. What is the main organic product?
Mission check

?Cyclopentanol is treated in two ways:
(1) heated with concentrated phosphoric acid
(2) warmed with potassium bromide and sulfuric acid
What are the main organic products?

Acid lets the OH leave as water. Whether a halide ion is present decides between alkene and haloalkane.

Mission 3

Mission 3: How does a breath tester detect alcohol?

Make a guess: How does a breath tester detect alcohol?

Mission 3

Primary, secondary, tertiary alcohols

  • Find the carbon bonded to the –OH. Count the carbons attached to it.
  • Primary: one (methanol: none). Ethanol, CH3CH2OH.
  • Secondary: two. Propan-2-ol, CH3CH(OH)CH3.
  • Tertiary: three. 2-methylpropan-2-ol, (CH3)3COH.
  • Primary and secondary alcohols have an H on the C–OH carbon; tertiary alcohols have none.
Quick check
?Which of these is a secondary alcohol?
Mission 3

The oxidising agent

  • Acidified potassium dichromate(VI): K2Cr2O7 in dilute H2SO4.
  • Cr2O7²⁻ is orange. As it oxidises the alcohol it is reduced to Cr³⁺, which is green.
  • In equations, write the oxidising agent as [O].
  • The alcohol loses two H atoms: one from the O–H, one from the C–OH carbon. C=O forms.
  • No H on the C–OH carbon (tertiary) → no oxidation.
Mission 3

What oxidation gives

  • Primary → aldehyde: CH3CH2OH + [O] → CH3CHO + H2O (ethanal).
  • The aldehyde → carboxylic acid: CH3CHO + [O] → CH3COOH (ethanoic acid).
  • Overall: CH3CH2OH + 2[O] → CH3COOH + H2O.
  • Secondary → ketone: CH3CH(OH)CH3 + [O] → CH3COCH3 + H2O (propanone). It stops there.
  • Tertiary: not oxidised. The dichromate stays orange.
Mission 3

Distil or reflux?

  • Boiling points: ethanal 20 °C, ethanol 78 °C, ethanoic acid 118 °C. The aldehyde has no O–H, so no hydrogen bonds between its molecules.
  • Aldehyde: alcohol in excess; warm, and distil the aldehyde off as it forms, so it is not oxidised further. Cool the receiver in ice.
  • Acid: excess oxidising agent; heat under reflux. The condenser is vertical and open at the top, so the aldehyde returns and is oxidised. Then distil off the acid.
  • Ketone: heat under reflux, then distil. It cannot be oxidised further.
  • Use anti-bumping granules; heat with a water bath or electric heater, not a flame.
Quick check
?Propan-1-ol is heated with an acidified oxidising agent in apparatus set up for distillation, and the distillate is collected. What is the main organic product collected, and why?
Quick check
?Pentan-3-ol is heated under reflux with excess acidified potassium dichromate(VI). Which equation is correct?
Mission 3

The dichromate test

  • Warm a few drops with acidified potassium dichromate(VI) in a water bath.
  • Orange → green: a primary or secondary alcohol (or an aldehyde).
  • Stays orange: a tertiary alcohol (or a ketone).
  • To tell an aldehyde from a ketone, use Tollens' reagent.
  • Early roadside breath tubes used this test: orange crystals turned green with ethanol on the breath.
Quick check
?Three colourless liquids, X, Y and Z, are butan-1-ol, butan-2-ol and 2-methylpropan-2-ol, in some order. Each is warmed with acidified potassium dichromate(VI). X and Y turn the mixture green; Z leaves it orange. Which conclusion is correct?
Mission 3

Background: adding hydrogen

  • Reduction is the reverse: hydrogen is added. [H] stands for the reducing agent.
  • Aldehyde + 2[H] → primary alcohol: CH3CHO + 2[H] → CH3CH2OH.
  • Ketone + 2[H] → secondary alcohol: CH3COCH3 + 2[H] → CH3CH(OH)CH3.
  • Reducing agents such as NaBH4 do this. LiAlH4 also reduces acids: CH3COOH + 4[H] → CH3CH2OH + H2O.
  • Hydrogenation: H2 adds across C=C over a nickel catalyst. A C≡C triple bond takes two H2.
Mission check

?A few drops of cyclohexanol, a six-carbon ring with an –OH on one ring carbon, are warmed with acidified potassium dichromate(VI). What do you see, and why?

A breath tester works the same way. Ethanol is oxidised. Old tubes showed it as dichromate turning green; most modern devices oxidise ethanol in a fuel cell and measure the current.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Spray-can gases that are safe to breathe thinned the ozone layer. How can a little gas destroy so much ozone?

Make a guess: Spray-can gases that are safe to breathe thinned the ozone layer. How can a little gas destroy so much ozone?

Mission 4

Ozone: the UV shield

  • Ozone, O3, forms naturally in the upper atmosphere (stratosphere).
  • UV splits O2: O2 → 2O. Then O + O2 → O3.
  • Ozone absorbs UV radiation: O3 → O2 + O. The UV is used up, not passed on.
  • So the stratosphere always contains some oxygen atoms, O.
  • Less ozone → more harmful UV reaches the surface → more skin cancer, cataracts and crop damage.
Mission 4

CFCs: why chemists loved them

  • CFCs: chlorofluorocarbons, e.g. CCl2F2 (also written CF2Cl2) and CCl3F.
  • Non-toxic, non-flammable, very unreactive, easily liquefied.
  • Used as refrigerants, aerosol propellants, solvents and for blowing foam.
Mission 4

What happens high up

  • CFCs are so unreactive that they last for decades and slowly diffuse up to the stratosphere.
  • There, high-energy UV breaks the weakest bond: C–Cl.
  • Bond enthalpies: C–F 467 · C–H 413 · C–Cl 346 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Homolytic fission makes chlorine radicals: CCl2F2 → CClF2• + Cl•
Quick check
?In the stratosphere, UV radiation breaks one bond in CCl2F2. Which bond breaks, and why?
Mission 4

The catalytic cycle

  • Step 1: Cl• + O3 → ClO• + O2
  • Step 2: ClO• + O → Cl• + O2 · Overall: O3 + O → 2O2
  • Cl• is used in step 1 and regenerated in step 2: it is a catalyst.
  • Each Cl• goes round the cycle many thousands of times before it is removed.
  • That's how a little CFC destroys a lot of ozone.
Mission 4

Other radicals do it too

  • Nitrogen monoxide, NO•, from aircraft and from N2O, also catalyses the breakdown.
  • NO• + O3 → NO2• + O2
  • NO2• + O → NO• + O2
  • Overall: O3 + O → 2O2. NO• is regenerated.
  • Same pattern for any radical X•: X• + O3 → XO• + O2, then XO• + O → X• + O2.
Quick check
?Why are chlorine radicals described as a catalyst in the breakdown of ozone?
Mission 4

Evidence, a ban and replacements

  • 1974: chemists (Molina and Rowland) predicted that Cl atoms from CFCs would destroy stratospheric ozone.
  • 1985: British scientists measured a large springtime ozone loss over Antarctica (the "ozone hole"). Satellite data confirmed it.
  • Aircraft measurements found high ClO where ozone was low.
  • Evidence from independent groups agreed, so governments acted.
  • The Montreal Protocol (1987) and its later tightening phased out CFCs.
  • Replacements contain no chlorine: HFCs (e.g. CH2FCF3) and hydrocarbons (e.g. 2-methylpropane in fridges).
  • The ozone layer is now slowly recovering.
Quick check
?HFC-134a, CH2FCF3, replaced CFCs in fridges. Why doesn't it damage the ozone layer?
Mission check

?Halon 1301, CBrF3, was used in fire extinguishers. Like CFCs, it reaches the stratosphere. Why can a small amount of halon destroy a large amount of ozone?

Light frees a halogen atom; the atom is a catalyst, regenerated every cycle.

Br• + O3 → BrO• + O2, then BrO• gives Br• back.

Mission 5

Molecular ion and fragments

  • In the spectrometer a molecule loses one electron: the molecular ion, M⁺. Its m/z = Mr.
  • M⁺ is the peak at highest m/z (apart from tiny isotope peaks). It can be small.
  • M⁺ can break into a fragment ion (detected) and a neutral piece (not detected).
  • The tallest peak is the base peak. It is often a fragment.
  • Mass lost = Mr − m/z of the fragment. Common losses: 15 CH3, 17 OH, 29 C2H5 or CHO, 31 OCH3 or CH2OH.
  • A small M+1 peak, one unit above M⁺, comes from molecules containing one ¹³C atom (about 1.1% of carbon atoms).
Mission 5

Worked example: ethanol

  • Ethanol, CH3CH2OH: Mr = 46, so M⁺ at m/z 46.
  • Base peak at m/z 31: mass lost 46 − 31 = 15, a CH3.
  • So the ion is CH2OH⁺: the C–C bond broke.
  • m/z 29: mass lost 46 − 29 = 17, an OH, leaving C2H5⁺.
  • Each fragment ion carries the + charge; the lost piece is neutral.
Quick check

Quick check
?Methyl butanoate, CH3CH2CH2COOCH3 (Mr = 102), gives a peak at m/z 71. Which neutral fragment was lost to form this ion?
Mission 5

Radiation and molecular motion

  • Infrared radiation makes covalent bonds vibrate more: they stretch and bend.
  • A photon is absorbed only if its energy matches the gap between two levels.
Mission 5

Reading an IR spectrum

  • Each bond absorbs IR at its own wavenumber, in cm⁻¹ (= 1 ÷ wavelength in cm).
  • The spectrum plots % transmittance against wavenumber, 4000 cm⁻¹ on the left. Absorptions are dips.
  • Above about 1500 cm⁻¹: bands you can assign to bonds.
  • Below about 1500 cm⁻¹: the fingerprint region, unique to each compound.
  • Match the whole spectrum with a database spectrum: same fingerprint, same compound.
Mission 5

  • In exams your board's data sheet gives the exact ranges. Use those.
Mission 5

Worked example: alcohol, ketone or acid?

  • Butan-1-ol: broad dip near 3300 (O–H), no C=O.
  • Butanone: strong sharp dip near 1715 (C=O), nothing broad above 3000.
  • Butanoic acid: C=O near 1710 plus a very broad O–H from 2500 to 3300, overlapping C–H.
  • C–O (1000–1300) can't decide: alcohols, acids and esters all have it.
Quick check
?Compound Q, C3H6O, absorbs strongly at 1725 cm⁻¹ and does not absorb between 3200 and 3600 cm⁻¹. Which conclusion is correct?
Mission 5

Greenhouse gases

  • The Earth's surface emits infrared. Greenhouse gases absorb it, then re-emit it in all directions, some back down.
  • A molecule absorbs IR only if a vibration changes its dipole.
  • CO2 is non-polar, but its asymmetric stretch and bend change the dipole: it absorbs IR.
  • N2 and O2: stretching never makes them polar, so they don't absorb IR.
  • CO2, H2O and CH4 (C=O, O–H and C–H bonds) absorb IR. More of them in the air: more IR trapped, linked to global warming.
Mission 5

IR at work

  • Breathalysers: IR passes through a breath sample. Absorption by ethanol's C–H bonds (near 2950 cm⁻¹) measures how much ethanol is present.
  • Not the O–H band: breath is full of water vapour, which also absorbs there.
  • More absorption → more ethanol in the breath.
  • Pollution monitors measure CO and NO from car exhausts at their own IR wavenumbers.
Mission check

?Carbon monoxide and nitrogen both have Mr = 28. Exhaust-gas analysers use infrared to measure CO, even though exhaust gas is mostly N2. Why does CO absorb infrared but N2 does not?

Same rule as for CO2: IR is absorbed only when a vibration changes the dipole. N2 has no such vibration.

Mission 6: Propanal and propanone share the formula C3H6O. Which spectrum tells the two bottles apart at a glance?

Make a guess: Propanal and propanone share the formula C3H6O. Which spectrum tells the two bottles apart at a glance?

Mission 6

A strategy

  • 1. Molecular formula: % composition → empirical formula; Mᵣ from the M⁺ peak.
  • 2. IR: which bonds are present (O–H? C=O? both? neither?).
  • 3. MS fragments: mass lost → pieces such as CH3 (15), C2H5 (29), OH (17).
  • 4. ¹H NMR: signals = environments; areas = H ratio; splitting = neighbouring H; shift = what is nearby.
  • 5. ¹³C NMR: signals = carbon environments; shift = type of carbon (e.g. aldehyde/ketone C=O near 200; ester/acid C=O near 170).
  • Finally: propose a structure, then check it against every piece of data.
Mission 6

Worked example: compound W (step 1, the formula)

  • W contains C 54.5%, H 9.1%, O 36.4% by mass.
  • Divide by Ar: C 54.5 ÷ 12.0 = 4.54 · H 9.1 ÷ 1.0 = 9.1 · O 36.4 ÷ 16.0 = 2.28.
  • Divide by the smallest (2.28): C 2.0 : H 4.0 : O 1.0.
  • Empirical formula C2H4O (empirical formula mass 44.0).
  • MS: M⁺ at m/z 44, so the molecular formula is also C2H4O.
Mission 6

Worked example: W (step 2, MS and IR)

  • MS: a peak at m/z 29: mass lost 44 − 29 = 15, a CH3.
  • IR: strong band at 1730 cm⁻¹: C=O.
  • IR: no broad band at 3200–3600 cm⁻¹: no O–H.
  • So far: a C=O and a CH3, with only two carbons.
  • Exact IR ranges: use the data sheet you get in your exam.
Mission 6

Worked example: W (step 3, NMR and confirming)

  • ¹H NMR: δ 9.8, 1H, quartet: an aldehyde H (–CHO), next to a CH3.
  • δ 2.2, 3H, doublet: CH3 next to C=O, next to one H.
  • ¹³C NMR: two signals, near δ 200 (C=O) and δ 31 (CH3).
  • W is ethanal, CH3CHO.
  • Confirm: Mᵣ 44 ✓ · C=O, no O–H ✓ · CHO⁺ at 29 ✓ · 1H quartet, 3H doublet ✓.
  • ¹³C: two carbon environments ✓.

Mission 6

Propanal vs propanone: every technique

  • M⁺: both at m/z 58. No help.
  • IR: both have a strong C=O band, no O–H. No help.
  • MS fragments: propanone's base peak is m/z 43 (CH3CO⁺); propanal gives a strong peak at 29 (C2H5⁺ or CHO⁺). A clue, not a glance.
  • ¹H NMR: propanone 1 signal (singlet); propanal 3 signals, including the aldehyde H near δ 9–10. The glance answer.
  • ¹³C NMR: propanone 2 signals; propanal 3. Also decisive.
  • Rule: pick the technique that "sees" the difference: here, the environments.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Nucleophiles donate an electron pair to the δ+ carbon; the halogen leaves as X⁻ (heterolytic fission).
  • Curly arrows: from lone pair to C; from C–X bond to X.
  • Rate: C–I fastest, C–Cl slowest: bond enthalpy, not polarity.
  • CN⁻ gives nitriles (chain + 1 C); excess NH3 gives primary amines.

Mission 2

  • Acid protonates –OH; water leaves; then lose H⁺ (alkene) or add a halide (haloalkane).

Mission 3

  • Primary → aldehyde (distil) → carboxylic acid (reflux); secondary → ketone; tertiary: no oxidation.
  • Acidified dichromate(VI) turns orange → green with primary and secondary alcohols (and aldehydes).

Mission 4

  • Ozone absorbs UV. UV breaks the C–Cl bond in CFCs, releasing chlorine atoms.
  • Each Cl atom is regenerated, so it catalyses the breakdown of many ozone molecules.
  • Cl• + O3 → ClO• + O2; then ClO• gives back Cl•.

Mission 5

  • MS: M⁺ gives Mr; mass lost (Mr − fragment m/z) gives the piece that broke off.
  • IR: broad O–H near 3300, an alcohol; strong C=O near 1700; C=O plus a very broad O–H, a carboxylic acid.
  • Microwave ↔ rotation; infrared ↔ vibration; UV/visible ↔ electrons. IR is absorbed only when a vibration changes the dipole.

Mission 6

  • Formula from % composition (and M⁺) → bonds from IR → pieces from MS → environments, ratios and neighbours from ¹H NMR → check against everything.
  • ¹³C NMR counts carbon environments; it is simpler to read than ¹H NMR.
  • Isomers: M⁺ and IR often match; NMR usually tells them apart.
