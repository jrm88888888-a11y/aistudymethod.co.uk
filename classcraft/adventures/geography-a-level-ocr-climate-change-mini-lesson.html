Mission 1: Air bubbles trapped in Antarctic ice are up to 800,000 years old. What can they tell us about past climate?

Make a guess: Air bubbles trapped in Antarctic ice are up to 800,000 years old. What can they tell us about past climate?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR A-level Geography (H481)

  • 3.1-1.a reconstructing past climate: marine and lake sediments, ice cores, tree rings, fossils
  • 3.1-1.a greenhouse and icehouse Earth: long-term cooling, Antarctic glaciation about 35 million years ago, Quaternary glaciation, the Holocene
Mission 1

Why we need proxies

  • Thermometer records cover only the last century or two. Older climates are read from proxy data: natural records that respond to climate.
  • Historical documents reach back centuries: harvest dates for grapes were used to reconstruct summer temperatures in Paris "from 1370 to 1879".
  • Long-term proxies (hundreds of thousands to millions of years): ice cores, ocean-floor deposits.
  • Medium-term proxies (thousands of years): pollen, lake sediments, tree rings.
  • Pollen in lake sediments records the vegetation that grew nearby, and so the conditions that suited it.
Mission 1

Ice cores

  • Snow builds up in layers that "contain dust, air bubbles, or isotopes of oxygen".
  • The ice "encloses small bubbles of air that contain a sample of the atmosphere": a direct record of past greenhouse gases.
  • Temperature comes from "the ratios of different water isotopes": less heavy oxygen in the ice "means that temperatures were cooler".
  • Oldest continuous record: EPICA Dome C, Antarctica, back 800,000 years.
Mission 1

What the ice shows

  • CO₂ was "low … during cold times, and high … during warm periods".
  • Glacials were "interspersed roughly every 100,000 years" by warmer interglacials.
  • In the ice-age cycles of the last million years or so, CO₂ "didn't get any higher than 300 ppm". In 2025 it reached 425.7 ppm.
  • Methane and nitrous oxide are higher than at any time in at least 800,000 years.
Quick check
?A section of an Antarctic ice core has less heavy oxygen than the layers above and below it, and its air bubbles hold less CO₂. What does it most likely record?
Mission 1

Ocean-floor deposits

  • Deep-sea sediments hold the shells of tiny plankton, including foraminifera; their chemistry, and which species are present, record past oceans.
  • In glacials, light oxygen is locked up in ice sheets, so seawater, and shells grown in it, gain heavy oxygen; colder water adds more heavy oxygen too.
  • So a shell's oxygen-isotope ratio (δ¹⁸O) tracks ice volume and temperature together.
  • The LR04 stack combines δ¹⁸O records from 57 sites and covers 5.3 million years.
Mission 1

Pollen analysis

  • Pollen grains are "well preserved in the sediment layers in the bottom of a pond, lake, or ocean", and in peat bogs.
  • Counting each type in each layer shows what plants grew nearby, and so what climate suited them.
  • Layers are dated, often by radiocarbon: one review used 150 radiocarbon dates from 139 sites in Britain and Ireland.
  • Caution: plants respond to people and disease too. The elm decline in British and Irish pollen records began between 6,343 and 6,307 calibrated years BP; disease, climate and human activity were probably all involved.
Mission 1

Tree rings, fossils and lake sediments

  • Tree rings: in temperate regions trees "generally produce one ring a year"; rings are wider when conditions are favourable.
  • Trees can hold "annual records of climate for centuries to millennia".
  • Fossils: the mix of foraminifera species in sediment cores is used to estimate past sea-surface temperatures.
  • Lake sediments can form yearly layers (varves); the proposed Anthropocene marker is a varved lake sequence at Crawford Lake, Canada.
Quick check
?Which proxy gives the longest continuous record?
Mission 1

From greenhouse to icehouse Earth

  • Benthic foraminifera records over the last 66 million years show four climate states: Hothouse, Warmhouse, Coolhouse, Icehouse.
  • Antarctica's ice sheet formed at the Eocene/Oligocene boundary, about 34 million years ago.
  • A model study found falling CO₂ was the main driver, with Southern Ocean gateways playing "a secondary role".
  • OCR's "100-million-year" cooling trend: these records show the Cenozoic (last 66 million years) part of it.
Mission 1

The Pleistocene: glacials and interglacials

  • The Pleistocene (and Quaternary) begins 2.58 million years ago; the Holocene begins 0.0117 million years (11,700 years) ago.
  • Over the last 800,000 years, cold glacials were broken by warmer interglacials roughly every 100,000 years.
  • The last interglacial, about 125,000 years ago, had sea level likely 5–10 m higher than today.
  • Last Glacial Maximum: nearly all ice sheets were at their largest from 26,500 to about 19,000–20,000 years ago.
Quick check
?How long ago did the Pleistocene begin, and roughly how often did glacials recur in the last 800,000 years?
Mission 1

End of the last glaciation and the Holocene

  • Northern deglaciation began 19,000–20,000 years ago, driven by rising northern summer sunlight.
  • Warming was not smooth: a last cold phase, the Younger Dryas (in Scotland, the Loch Lomond Stadial), ended abruptly.
  • The base of the Holocene is fixed at 11,700 years before AD 2000 in the NGRIP Greenland ice core, at the first sign of warming after the Younger Dryas.
  • The Holocene varied too: its most recent multi-century warm period was about 6,500 years ago.

Ice cores, ocean-floor deposits and pollen record past climate at different timescales. Together they show glacial–interglacial cycles through the Pleistocene (from 2.58 million years ago), an abrupt last cold phase, and the start of the Holocene 11,700 years ago.

Mission 2

The atmosphere as an energy system

  • Energy arrives as shortwave radiation from the Sun and leaves as longwave (infrared) radiation from Earth.
  • IPCC global averages: 340 W/m² of sunlight arrives at the top of the atmosphere; 100 W/m² is reflected straight back to space.
  • Of the rest, 80 W/m² is absorbed by the atmosphere and 160 W/m² by the surface.
Mission 2

The outgoing side

  • The surface emits 398 W/m² of longwave radiation.
  • Greenhouse gases and clouds absorb much of it and send 342 W/m² back down (back radiation).
  • 239 W/m² of longwave leaves the top of the atmosphere.
  • Incoming slightly exceeds outgoing: an imbalance of 0.7 W/m², so the climate system is gaining heat.
Quick check
?340 W/m² of sunlight arrives and 100 W/m² is reflected. What is Earth's planetary albedo?
Mission 2

The natural greenhouse effect

  • Greenhouse gases let most sunlight through but absorb outgoing longwave radiation and re-emit it, partly back towards the surface.
  • "Water vapour is the most important greenhouse gas, and carbon dioxide (CO2) is the second-most important one".
  • To emit about 240 W/m², a surface would need to be about −19 °C; the actual global mean is about 14 °C.
  • The natural greenhouse effect adds about 33 °C (our calculation) and makes Earth habitable.
Mission 2

Carbon dioxide and methane

  • Global average CO₂: 425.62 ppm in 2025; methane: 1,936 ppb.
  • Before the Industrial Revolution, CO₂ was 280 ppm or less.
  • IPCC: in 2019, CO₂ was higher than at any time in at least 2 million years; methane and nitrous oxide higher than at any time in at least 800,000 years.
  • More of these gases absorbs more outgoing longwave radiation: the enhanced greenhouse effect.
Mission 2

Natural forcing: the Sun and Earth's orbit

  • A forcing is anything that changes the energy balance.
  • Solar output: over the roughly 11-year sunspot cycle, total solar irradiance varies by about 0.1%.
  • Milankovitch cycles: eccentricity about 100,000 years, obliquity about 41,000 years, precession about 23,000 years on average. They redistribute sunlight by season and latitude.
  • Earth's current orbital positions "predict our planet should be cooling, not warming".
Mission 2

Natural greenhouse gases in the past

  • Ice cores show that during the ice-age cycles of the past million years or so, CO₂ "didn't get any higher than 300 ppm".
  • Warm interglacials began with a small rise in Northern Hemisphere summer sunlight (Milankovitch). Warming oceans released CO₂, which "greatly amplified the initial, solar-driven warming".
  • So natural greenhouse gases act as a feedback that turns a small orbital push into a large climate change.
Mission 2

Plate tectonics: continental drift

  • About 34 million years ago, at the Eocene–Oligocene boundary, Antarctica became glaciated.
  • It "has hitherto been thought to result from the tectonic opening of Southern Ocean gateways", which let the Antarctic Circumpolar Current isolate the continent thermally.
  • A model study found declining CO₂ was the main trigger, with the gateways playing "a secondary role".
  • Drift also moves continents across latitudes over millions of years, changing where ice can form.
Quick check
?Which conclusion about Antarctica's glaciation 34 million years ago is best supported by the model study?
Mission 2

Volcanic forcing: Pinatubo, 1991

  • In June 1991 Mount Pinatubo injected about 15 million tons of sulfur dioxide into the stratosphere.
  • The gas formed tiny droplets (aerosols) that "scatter and absorb incoming sunlight": less sunlight reached the surface.
  • Over the next 15 months, average global temperature fell by about 0.6 °C; measurable cooling lasted almost two years.
  • Over geological time, very large eruptions also release CO₂, which can warm the climate.
Mission 2

How humans change the global mean energy balance

  • Human forcings include aerosols, which "absorb and reflect incoming sunlight"; deforestation, which changes how the surface reflects and absorbs sunlight; and greenhouse gases, which "decrease heat radiated to space".
  • IPCC, 1850–1900 to 2010–2019: human-caused warming best estimate 1.07 °C; greenhouse gases contributed 1.0 to 2.0 °C; other human drivers, mainly aerosols, a cooling of 0.0 to 0.8 °C.
  • Natural drivers changed temperature by only −0.1 to +0.1 °C over the same period.
Mission check

?A large tropical eruption injects sulfur gas high into the stratosphere. What happens to the global energy balance, and for how long?

Pinatubo put about 15 million tons of sulfur dioxide into the stratosphere. Droplets scattered incoming sunlight, changing the incoming side of the energy balance, and global temperature fell by about 0.6 °C over 15 months. The droplets fell out within about two years, so the cooling was temporary.

Mission 3

Water stored in ice

  • Ice sheets hold over 68% of Earth's fresh water.
  • Fully melted: Antarctica's ice sheet would raise sea level about 58 m, Greenland's about 7.4 m.
  • Both are losing ice. 2006–2015 averages: Greenland 278 billion tonnes a year, Antarctica 155.
Mission 3

Sea level is rising faster

  • IPCC: global mean sea level rose 0.20 m between 1901 and 2018.
  • The rate rose from 1.3 mm a year (1901–1971) to 1.9 mm (1971–2006) and 3.7 mm (2006–2018).
  • Satellites: about 10 cm since 1993; the yearly rate has more than doubled.
  • Faster since 1900 than in any century for at least 3,000 years.
Quick check
?Sea level rose 0.20 m between 1901 and 2018. What was the average rate in mm per year?
Mission 3

Where the water comes from

  • Thermal expansion: seawater expands as it warms.
  • Land ice: glaciers and ice sheets melt or flow into the sea.
  • IPCC, 1971–2018: thermal expansion 50%, glaciers 22%, ice sheets 20%, land-water storage 8%.
  • In 2024 the rise was 0.59 cm; unusually, two-thirds came from thermal expansion (usually about two-thirds comes from land water).
  • Floating sea ice adds almost nothing: it already displaces its own weight of water.
Quick check
?Which of these adds least to global sea-level rise?
Mission 3

More evidence of warming since the late 19th century

  • Temperature: 2025 was 1.44 °C (± 0.13) above 1850–1900; the ocean has taken up more than 90% of the excess heat since 1970.
  • Glaciers: almost all retreating together, unprecedented in at least 2,000 years.
  • Sea ice: Arctic September area fell about 40% between 1979–1988 and 2010–2019.
  • Snow: human influence very likely contributed to less Northern Hemisphere spring snow cover since 1950.
  • Water vapour: total column water vapour very likely increased since the 1980s.
Mission 3

Projections and uncertainty

  • IPCC projections for 2100 (vs 1995–2014): likely 0.28–0.55 m (very low emissions) to 0.63–1.01 m (very high).
  • Approaching 2 m by 2100 "cannot be ruled out" because of deep uncertainty in ice sheets.
  • Local sea levels that were once-a-century events are projected to occur at least yearly at most places by 2100.
  • These are scenarios, not forecasts: they depend on emissions.
Mission check

?A news report says: "Melting glaciers and ice sheets explain most of the sea-level rise since 1971." How accurate is it?

The extra water comes from melting land ice (glaciers 22%, ice sheets 20%) and the ocean taking up heat and expanding (50%), with land-water storage 8% (1971–2018). In recent years water added from land (such as melting ice sheets and glaciers) has usually made up about two-thirds of each year's rise; in 2024 expansion did.

Mission 4

The enhanced greenhouse effect

  • Fossil fuels, deforestation, farming and industry add greenhouse gases faster than natural processes remove them.
  • Higher concentrations absorb more outgoing longwave radiation: the enhanced greenhouse effect.
  • IPCC: in 2019, CO₂ was higher than at any time in at least 2 million years; methane and nitrous oxide higher than in at least 800,000 years.
  • This is why some scientists propose naming a new epoch, the Anthropocene: one in which human activity is a dominant influence on climate.
Mission 4

The additional gases

  • 2024 global averages: CO₂ 423.9 ppm, methane 1,942 ppb, nitrous oxide 338.0 ppb.
  • That is 52%, 166% and 25% above pre-industrial (before 1750) levels.
  • CO₂ rose 3.5 ppm in 2024, the largest rise since measurements began in 1957.
  • Fluorinated gases (F-gases) showed the highest relative growth since 1990, from low levels.
Mission 4

Why emissions have risen since pre-industrial times

  • Cumulative net CO₂ 1850–2019: about 2,400 billion tonnes; 42% of it emitted in just 1990–2019.
  • 2019 total: 59 billion tonnes CO₂-eq, 54% higher than 1990.
  • In 2010–2019, cleaner energy and more efficient GDP cut emissions less than rising activity in industry, energy supply, transport, farming and buildings has added.
  • 2019 shares by sector: energy supply 34%, industry 24%, agriculture/forestry/land use 22%, transport 15%, buildings 6%.

  • Shares: China 29.5%, USA 11.1%, India 8.3%, EU27 5.8%, Russia 4.8%, Indonesia 2.5%: together 62%.

Mission 4

History: cumulative emissions

  • Shares of all fossil and industrial CO₂ emitted 1750–2024: USA 23.5%, EU27 16.3%, China 15.5%, Russia 6.6%, UK 4.3%, India 3.6%.
  • The least developed countries: less than 0.4% of fossil CO₂ in 1850–2019.
  • CO₂ stays in the air for centuries, so cumulative emissions drive today's warming.
Mission 4

Case study: the UK over time

  • Fossil CO₂: 663 Mt in 1970 (4.5% of the world) → 318 Mt in 2024 (0.8%).
  • All greenhouse gases 2025: 367 Mt CO₂-eq (provisional), 54% below 1990.
  • Real change at home: the last coal power station closed in September 2024.
  • Defra also points to "a shift from manufacturing towards a service-based economy, alongside an increased reliance on imported goods" (see "Whose emissions?" below).
  • Still hard: domestic transport is now the largest source, 31% of net emissions.
  • The UK is an AC (advanced country).
Mission 4

Case study: China over time

  • Fossil CO₂: 802 Mt in 1970 (5.5% of the world) → 12,401 Mt in 2024 (32.1%).
  • Per person: 0.97 t → 8.74 t.
  • Cumulative share 1750–2024: 15.5%, below the USA and EU27.
  • Industrialisation and coal-fired power drove the rise; much of the output is exported (see "Whose emissions?" below).
  • China is an EDC (emerging and developing country).
  • Note: these are fossil CO₂ (Global Carbon Project); "The global balance" screen uses all greenhouse gases (EDGAR), so the numbers differ.

Whose emissions? Territorial v consumption

  • Since 1996: UK territorial emissions −50%, footprint only −15%.

Mission 5

The early science, 1824–1938

  • 1824: Fourier proposes that Earth "would be far colder if it lacked an atmosphere".
  • 1859: Tyndall shows "that some gases block infrared radiation".
  • 1896: Arrhenius "publishes first calculation of global warming from human emissions of CO2".
  • 1938: Callendar argues that greenhouse warming "is underway".
  • For decades these were minority ideas, debated mainly among scientists.
Mission 5

Measuring and modelling, 1960–1979

  • 1960: Keeling "accurately measures CO2 in the Earth's atmosphere and detects an annual rise".
  • 1979: a US National Academy of Sciences report finds it "highly credible" that doubling CO₂ will bring 1.5–4.5 °C of warming.
  • The question shifts from "is CO₂ rising?" to "how much warming, and how soon?"
Mission 5

From science to politics, 1988–1997

  • 1988: news coverage of global warming "leaps upward following record heat and droughts".
  • 1988: the IPCC is set up by UNEP and the WMO, endorsed by the UN General Assembly.
  • 1989: US fossil-fuel and other industries form the Global Climate Coalition "to persuade politicians and the public".
  • 1992: the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) at Rio; 1997: the Kyoto Protocol sets targets for industrialised nations.
Quick check

Mission 5

How the scientific position changed

  • IPCC 1990: the world "has been warming and future warming seems likely". 2001: warming "very likely". 2021: "It is unequivocal that human influence has warmed the atmosphere, ocean and land".
  • Warming from doubled CO₂: 1979, 1.5–4.5 °C; IPCC 2013 range the same; IPCC 2021 narrowed it to 2.5–4 °C, best estimate 3 °C.
  • A 2013 study of 11,944 climate abstracts (1991–2011): 66.4% stated no position; of those that did, 97.1% endorsed human-caused warming.
  • Debate moved from whether humans cause warming to how much, how fast and what to do.
Mission 5

The UN: the IPCC and the UNFCCC

  • The IPCC assesses published research; "IPCC reports should be neutral with respect to policy".
  • Its reports go through "peer review by experts and review by governments". Its 1990 report "played a decisive role in the creation of the UNFCCC".
  • The IPCC and Al Gore jointly won the Nobel Peace Prize in 2007.
  • The UNFCCC's yearly meetings (COPs) produced Kyoto (1997) and Paris (2015); Copenhagen (2009) "fails to negotiate binding agreements".
Mission 5

Governments and the EU

  • EU: launched the world's first carbon market in 2005. The European Climate Law (in force 29 July 2021) sets climate neutrality by 2050 and cuts of at least 55% by 2030 and 90% by 2040, on 1990 levels.
  • National governments take different positions over time:
    • the USA signed Kyoto in 1998 but did not ratify it; Canada withdrew from Kyoto with effect from December 2012;
    • the USA's withdrawal from the Paris Agreement took effect on 27 January 2026.
  • Governments shape the debate by what they sign, fund and say.
Quick check

Mission 5

The media: framing and balance

  • Coverage rises and falls with events: it "leaps upward" in 1988 after record heat.
  • False balance: giving a minority view equal time can suggest the evidence is evenly split. A 2004 study of the US "prestige press" was titled "Balance as bias".
  • In 2009, "excerpts from stolen e-mails of climate scientists fuel public skepticism".
  • Errors are seized on: an IPCC report said 55% of the Netherlands was below sea level; the Dutch agency corrected it to 26% below sea level and 55% at risk of flooding.
Mission 5

Interest groups

  • Business groups: the Global Climate Coalition (1989) set out "to persuade politicians and the public" that the science was too uncertain to justify action.
  • A 2017 study of 187 ExxonMobil climate communications (1977–2014): 83% of peer-reviewed papers and 80% of internal documents acknowledged that climate change is real and human-caused; only 12% of paid advertorials did, and 81% expressed doubt. The authors conclude the public was misled.
  • Campaign groups and think tanks on all sides also choose frames that suit their aims.
  • Public opinion: in a 2024 UN survey of 77 countries, 80% wanted their governments to take stronger climate action.

Mission check

The science moved from early physics (1820s–1890s) to measurement (1960) and assessment (IPCC, 1988 on). Governments, the UN, the EU, the media and interest groups then shaped the public debate, often over action and cost rather than over the basic science.

Mission 6

What a climate model is

  • A climate model is a computer program that solves equations for the physical, chemical and biological processes of the atmosphere, ocean, ice and land.
  • It divides the planet into grid cells, typically 1–2° across, so it cannot see detail smaller than that.
  • Models are tested on the past: the CMIP6 models' average reproduces observed global warming to within 0.2 °C over most of the historical period.
  • Some individual models still run warmer or cooler than observations. That is why scientists use many models together (an ensemble).
Mission 6

Why the carbon cycle matters to a model

  • A model needs to know how much CO₂ stays in the air. That depends on the carbon cycle: the stores (atmosphere, ocean, land) and the flows between them.
  • Today, about half of the CO₂ emitted each year stays in the atmosphere; land ecosystems and the ocean absorb the rest.
  • In 2010–2019, the ocean took up 23% of CO₂ emissions and land vegetation 31%.
  • In 2024, weaker uptake by land and ocean, plus wildfire emissions, gave the largest one-year CO₂ rise since measurements began in 1957.

Mission 6

Positive and negative feedback

  • Positive feedback amplifies the first change; negative feedback reduces it.
  • Water vapour (+): warmer air holds more water vapour, a greenhouse gas. With the lapse-rate effect, it is the largest single feedback adding to warming.
  • Ice–albedo (+): snow-covered sea ice reflects up to 90% of sunlight; open ocean only 6%. Melting ice exposes darker surfaces, which absorb more heat and melt more ice.
  • Clouds (+, on balance): the net cloud feedback is positive, but it is the largest source of uncertainty.
  • Planck response (−): a warmer Earth gives off more heat to space, which limits warming.
Mission 6

Carbon-cycle feedbacks

  • More CO₂ in the air makes land and ocean absorb more carbon: a negative feedback. On land, extra plant growth from CO₂ fertilisation is part of this.
  • A warmer climate makes land and ocean absorb less: a positive feedback.
  • Under higher emissions, sinks take up more carbon in total but a smaller proportion, so more of each tonne stays in the air.
  • Permafrost holds 1,460–1,600 Gt of organic carbon, almost twice the carbon in the atmosphere. Models project 3–41 PgC released per 1 °C of warming by 2100.
  • Some responses (wetland and permafrost CO₂ and CH₄, wildfires) are not yet fully included in models; they would raise concentrations further.

Mission 6

Emission scenarios

  • Models cannot know future emissions, so they are run under several emission scenarios. The IPCC's latest report uses five, starting in 2015.
  • SSP1-1.9 and SSP1-2.6: very low and low emissions; CO₂ falls to net zero around or after 2050.
  • SSP2-4.5: intermediate; CO₂ emissions stay around current levels until mid-century.
  • SSP3-7.0 and SSP5-8.5: high and very high; CO₂ emissions roughly double by 2100 and 2050 respectively.
  • Scenarios differ in socio-economic assumptions, mitigation and air-pollution control. They are "what if" pathways, not forecasts: the IPCC does not judge how likely each is.

Warming above 1850–1900, best estimate (very likely range), 2081–2100:

  • SSP1-1.9: 1.4 °C (1.0–1.8)
  • SSP1-2.6: 1.8 °C (1.3–2.4)
  • SSP2-4.5: 2.7 °C (2.1–3.5)
  • SSP3-7.0: 3.6 °C (2.8–4.6)
  • SSP5-8.5: 4.4 °C (3.3–5.7)
  • In 2021–2040 the best estimates are almost the same (1.5–1.6 °C) for all five. The choices made now show up most strongly after mid-century.

Mission 6

How sure are the models?

  • Equilibrium climate sensitivity (warming from doubled CO₂): best estimate 3 °C, likely range 2.5–4 °C.
  • With no feedbacks (the Planck response alone), doubling CO₂ would warm the planet about 1.2 °C. Feedbacks roughly multiply this by 2.5 (3 ÷ 1.2, calculated).
  • CMIP6 models average a higher sensitivity than the IPCC's best estimate, traced to a cloud feedback about 20% larger.
  • By 2100, the uncertainty in CO₂ levels is dominated by the differences between scenarios, not by the models. Our choices matter more than model error.
  • Exam move ("Assess"): weigh what models do well (match past warming) against limits (clouds, ice sheets, missing feedbacks), then conclude.

Risk

  • IPCC: climate risk results "from the interaction of climate related hazards with vulnerabilities of societies and systems exposed". Often written Risk = Hazard × Exposure × Vulnerability.
  • Hazard: a physical event or trend that may cause loss (storm surge, sea-level rise).
  • Exposure: people, livelihoods, ecosystems and assets in places that could be harmed.
  • Same hazard, different exposure and vulnerability → different risk.
Mission 9

Vulnerability

  • Vulnerability: "the propensity or predisposition to be adversely affected", including lack of capacity to cope and adapt.
  • Higher with poverty, governance challenges, limited basic services and climate-sensitive livelihoods; worsened by inequity linked to gender, ethnicity and low income.
  • Adaptation can lower exposure (moving people) or vulnerability (building capacity); it cannot change the hazard.

Two ways to intervene in the carbon cycle

Putting a price on carbon

  • Maladaptation: a response that raises risk later. "Maladaptive responses to climate change can create lock-ins of vulnerability, exposure and risks".
  • Bangladesh: islands enclosed by embankments in the 1960s "have lost 1.0–1.5 m of elevation", while the nearby mangrove forest stayed stable, because sediment was kept out.
  • "Soft limits to some human adaptation have been reached, but can be overcome", mainly through finance and governance.
  • "The higher the sea levels rise, the more challenging is coastal protection".
Quick check
?Why did embanked islands in south-west Bangladesh end up lower than the mangrove forest beside them?
Mission 11

Future homes, cities, transport and economies

  • Homes: raise, flood-proof, or rebuild away from risk (the New Isle's 37 raised, pier-and-beam homes).
  • Cities: store water in public spaces (Rotterdam); Singapore's Long Island plan would also add a new reservoir.
  • Transport: barriers such as Maeslant stay open in normal conditions and close only when a storm surge is forecast, so the route stays in use.
  • Economies: the IPCC notes "more financing is directed at physical infrastructure than natural and social infrastructure".
  • Exam move (OCR "Assess"): compare options on cost, effectiveness, lock-in and who can afford them, then judge which fits each place.
Mission check

?Three places face rising seas: (1) a wealthy, densely built port city; (2) a small village whose land has mostly gone; (3) a poor delta region where millions live. Which pairing of strategies is best supported?

Retreat makes most sense when land is mostly gone, protection is unaffordable or ineffective, and a safe place to move to exists. Dense, wealthy places can protect; poorer places often accommodate first. Each choice can fail if it locks in risk.

Mission 12

Mitigation and adaptation

  • Mitigation: reducing the causes: cutting emissions or removing greenhouse gases (renewables, carbon pricing, forests, carbon capture).
  • Adaptation: adjusting to actual or expected climate to moderate harm or use opportunities (flood defences, heat alerts, drought-tolerant crops).
  • Mitigation needs global action; adaptation is mostly local, but poorer places need help to pay for it.
Mission 12

The IPCC: role

  • Role: to assess "on a comprehensive, objective, open and transparent basis" the science of human-induced climate change, its impacts and options for adaptation and mitigation.
  • Reports "should be neutral with respect to policy": policy-relevant, not policy-prescriptive.
  • Works in support of the UN climate convention process; reports are reviewed by experts and governments, and approved by consensus where possible.
  • Its findings frame negotiations: e.g. policies in place at the end of 2020 implied about 3.2 °C by 2100.
Mission 12

Global agreements

  • UNFCCC (Convention): adopted 9 May 1992, in force 1994.
  • Kyoto Protocol: adopted 1997, in force 2005; binding targets for 37 industrialised countries and economies in transition only.
  • Limits: the USA signed but never ratified; Canada withdrew with effect from 15 December 2012.
  • Paris Agreement: adopted 12 December 2015, in force 4 November 2016; US withdrawal took effect 27 January 2026.
Mission 12

Kyoto: did it work?

  • Target: an average 5% cut below 1990 over 2008–2012, with emissions trading, the Clean Development Mechanism and joint implementation.
  • Of the 36 countries that fully participated (not the USA or Canada), only 9 emitted more than committed; after trading, all complied; together they beat their commitment by 2.4 Gt CO₂-eq a year.
  • But the authors put 2.2 Gt of that surplus down to "hot air": targets that needed little or no policy to meet.
  • IPCC: Kyoto cut emissions in some countries and built capacity for reporting and carbon markets. Yet global emissions in 2019 were 54% above 1990.
Quick check
?Which judgement on the Kyoto Protocol's success is best supported?
Mission 12

The Paris Agreement

  • Goal: "well below 2°C" and pursue efforts to limit warming to 1.5 °C.
  • Every country sets its own target, a nationally determined contribution (NDC), on a five-year cycle, with a global stocktake.
  • Principle: "common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities"; developed countries "should take the lead".
  • Strength: near-universal. Weakness: targets are self-chosen.
Mission 12

The gap

  • 2024 was likely the first calendar year above 1.5 °C (1.55 ± 0.13 °C); 2025 was 1.44 °C. Paris is judged over decades, not single years.
  • UNEP (2025): full NDCs → 2.3–2.5 °C this century; current policies → up to 2.8 °C.
  • Carbon budget left for 1.5 °C: 170 billion tonnes CO₂. 2025 emissions: 38.1 Gt fossil + 4.1 Gt land-use change.
Quick check
?At 2025 levels of total CO₂ emissions, how many years would the remaining 1.5 °C budget last?
Mission 12

Who pays? Sources far from impacts

  • Least developed countries: under 0.4% of fossil CO₂ (1850–2019).
  • Deaths from floods, droughts and storms were 15 times higher in highly vulnerable regions (2010–2020).
  • Finance: the US$100 billion goal was first met in 2022 (US$115.9 billion); new goal US$300 billion a year by 2035.
  • A Loss and Damage Fund assists developing countries particularly vulnerable to climate change. These are goals and funds, not delivered outcomes.
Quick check
?What is the main argument for high-emitting countries funding loss and damage in poorer ones?
Mission 12

Beyond international directives: national and sub-national

  • National (UK): the Climate Change Act 2008 set a legal 80% cut by 2050, raised to 100% (net zero) in 2019; five-year carbon budgets; an independent Committee on Climate Change. 2025: 54% below 1990.
  • Sub-national (Scotland): law requires net-zero emissions by 2045, five years ahead of the UK.
  • IPCC: by 2020, 56 countries had direct climate laws covering 53% of emissions; Paris has spurred national and sub-national targets.
  • Exam move ("Evaluate"): compare a target with delivery, e.g. UK transport is now its largest source.
Mission check

?Negotiators design a new global deal on shipping emissions. Which design best applies the lessons of Kyoto and Paris to "who cuts, who pays"?

The UN agreements' answer is common but differentiated responsibilities: every Party cuts through its NDC, developed countries lead, and finance (US$300 billion a year by 2035; the Loss and Damage Fund) flows to the most vulnerable. Pledges still point to 2.3–2.8 °C.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Proxies: ice cores (gases, isotopes, dust; 800,000 years), ocean-floor deposits (foraminifera δ¹⁸O; millions of years), pollen and lake sediments (thousands of years), tree rings (annual; centuries to millennia).
  • Earth cooled from greenhouse to icehouse (Antarctic ice about 34 million years ago).

Mission 2

  • Earth's energy balance: 340 W/m² in, 100 reflected, 239 longwave out; the natural greenhouse effect adds about 33 °C. Forcings, natural or human, shift the balance.
  • Sun, orbit, natural GHGs, tectonics and volcanoes drove past climate.

Mission 3

  • Sea level rose 0.20 m in 1901–2018 and is accelerating; thermal expansion and land ice are the main sources, sea ice almost none.
  • Warming evidence: temperatures, ocean heat, glaciers, sea ice, snow and water vapour all point the same way.

Mission 4

  • Extra CO₂, methane, N₂O and F-gases enhance the greenhouse effect; emissions rose because activity grew faster than efficiency improved.
  • The balance has shifted: the UK (AC) fell from 4.5% to 0.8% of fossil CO₂ while China (EDC) rose from 5.5% to 32.1%; per person, cumulative and consumption measures tell different stories.

Mission 5

  • History: Fourier → Tyndall → Arrhenius → Callendar → Keeling → 1979 NAS → IPCC (1988) → UNFCCC (1992) → Kyoto (1997) → Paris (2015) → "unequivocal" (2021).
  • The UN and EU frame and negotiate; governments sign and withdraw; media framing and interest-group messaging shape public perception, sometimes unlike the peer-reviewed record.

Mission 6

  • Climate models simulate the carbon cycle (how much CO₂ stays in the air) and feedbacks: positive (water vapour, ice–albedo, clouds, weaker sinks, permafrost) and negative (Planck response, CO₂-driven uptake).
  • Under five SSP scenarios, 2081–2100 warming ranges from 1.4 °C to 4.4 °C (best estimates) and 2100 sea-level rise from 0.28–0.55 m to 0.63–1.01 m; sea level keeps rising for centuries.

Mission 7

  • Heat extremes are more frequent and intense with every increment of warming; attribution studies measure how much more likely an event became.
  • Implications reach health (heat, disease, undernutrition) and ecosystems (coral bleaching, range shifts).

Mission 8

  • Tundra: observed greening, snow loss, fire and thaw; projected shrub spread (24–52% by 2050), boreal advance, loss of High-Arctic species.

Mission 9

  • Risk = hazard × exposure × vulnerability; the Netherlands is more exposed, Tuvalu more vulnerable.
  • Two contrasting countries face different impacts, opportunities and technological, socio-economic and political challenges.

Mission 10

  • Interventions either cut emissions (efficiency, fuel shifts, carbon pricing) or store carbon (forests, CCS, DAC). Capture is tiny today: about 50 Mt a year against 38.1 Gt emitted.
  • Trading and credits work only with falling caps and real, permanent cuts. SRM masks warming without removing CO₂.

Mission 11

  • Protect, accommodate or retreat (plus advance and ecosystem-based options); the choice depends on hazard, assets, money and people's ties to place.
  • Across the development continuum, wealthy places protect (Netherlands, Singapore); poorer and smaller places mix accommodation, migration and relocation (Bangladesh, Tuvalu, Isle de Jean Charles), and must avoid maladaptation.

Mission 12

  • Mitigation cuts causes; adaptation reduces harm. Kyoto bound few countries; Paris includes almost everyone but relies on self-set NDCs.
  • The IPCC assesses evidence neutrally and frames negotiations; national (UK) and sub-national (Scotland) laws go beyond international directives.
