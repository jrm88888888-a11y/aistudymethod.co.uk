Mission 1

Mission 1: How can a fatty streak inside an artery wall end in a heart attack years later?

Make a guess: How can a fatty streak inside an artery wall end in a heart attack years later?

Mission 1

The heart feeds itself

  • Heart muscle needs a constant supply of oxygen and glucose.
  • It gets them from the coronary arteries, which branch from the aorta.
  • Blood inside the chambers does not supply the heart wall.
  • Coronary heart disease (CHD): coronary arteries narrowed or blocked.
  • Say: "occlusion starves the heart muscle of oxygen" — not: "blood can no longer pass through the chambers".
Mission 1

Atherosclerosis, step by step

  • (1) Endothelial dysfunction: the inner lining is damaged, e.g. by high blood pressure, smoking or high LDL cholesterol.
  • (2) Inflammatory response: white blood cells enter the wall and take up LDL cholesterol.
  • (3) Plaque (atheroma) builds inside the wall: cholesterol, white cells, fibrous tissue, calcium salts. The lumen narrows; the wall stiffens.
  • (4) Narrower, stiffer arteries raise blood pressure, which damages more endothelium.
  • Atherosclerosis: hardening and narrowing of arteries by atheroma.
  • Say: "atheroma forms within the artery wall" — not: "fat sticks to the inside like grease in a drainpipe".
Mission 1

From plaque to blockage

  • A plaque can rupture, exposing the tissue beneath.
  • A clot (thrombus) forms in the artery: thrombosis.
  • Plaque plus clot can occlude (block) the artery. Or the clot breaks off and blocks a smaller artery downstream.
Quick check
?A blood clot completely occludes a branch of a coronary artery. What is the direct consequence?
Mission 1

What blockage does

  • Narrowed coronary artery: too little oxygen during exercise → chest pain (angina).
  • Blocked coronary artery: muscle beyond dies → myocardial infarction (heart attack).
  • Dead muscle is replaced by scar tissue, which does not contract.
  • A clot in an artery to the brain → stroke.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Countries that eat more fat have more heart disease. Does that prove the fat is the cause?

Make a guess: Countries that eat more fat have more heart disease. Does that prove the fat is the cause?

Mission 2

Correlation or cause?

  • Correlation: two variables change together.
  • Causal relationship: a change in one produces a change in the other.
  • A correlation can arise from a confounding variable (a third factor linked to both), from chance, or from cause running the other way.
  • Stronger evidence of cause: a mechanism; a controlled trial; more exposure, more disease (dose–response); many studies agreeing.
  • Say: "is correlated with" — not: "causes", until the evidence shows it.
Mission 2

Correlation coefficients

  • A correlation coefficient runs from −1 to +1.
  • The sign gives direction: + both rise together; − one rises as the other falls.
  • The size gives strength: near ±1 strong; near 0 little or none.
  • Sample size changes how far you can trust a coefficient, not how strong it is.
  • A low or zero coefficient can be evidence against a hypothesis. Even a strong one does not prove cause.

Mission 3

Mission 3: A difference between two groups could be real or just luck. How do biologists decide which?

Make a guess: A difference between two groups could be real or just luck. How do biologists decide which?

Mission 3

Could it just be chance?

  • Samples vary, so two groups almost never match exactly.
  • Start with the null hypothesis: there is no difference (or no association, or no correlation).
  • A statistical test turns the data into one number, the test statistic.
  • Compare it with the critical value at p = 0.05.
  • Above the critical value: if the null hypothesis were true, a result this extreme would happen by chance less than 5% of the time. The result is significant: reject the null hypothesis.
  • Below it: not significant. The difference could be due to chance.
  • Then the null hypothesis is not rejected.
  • Significant does not mean proved; not significant does not prove "no effect".
Mission 3

Worked example: chi-squared beyond genetics

Daisies counted in two areas of a field of equal size, one trampled and one not (illustrative data). Null hypothesis: no difference, so equal numbers are expected.

TrampledUntrampledTotal
Observed, O3862100
Expected, E5050100
(O − E)² ÷ E2.882.88
  • χ² = Σ (O − E)² ÷ E = 2.88 + 2.88 = 5.76.
  • Degrees of freedom = categories − 1 = 1. Critical value at p = 0.05: 3.84.
  • 5.76 > 3.84: significant. Reject the null hypothesis: daisies are not spread evenly between the two areas.
  • The test does not say why. Trampling is one explanation to test further.
  • Expected values come from the null hypothesis: here, equal areas → equal numbers.

Mission 3

Chi-squared for association

  • Do two species occur together more or less often than chance predicts? Record presence or absence of each in many random quadrats.
  • Arrange the counts in a 2 × 2 contingency table.
  • Expected count for each cell = row total × column total ÷ grand total.
  • Degrees of freedom = (rows − 1) × (columns − 1) = 1. Critical value at p = 0.05: 3.84.
  • Significant, and together less than expected: a negative association. Together more than expected: positive.
  • A negative association is consistent with competition, or with different habitat needs. It indicates; it does not prove.
Quick check
?Two lichen species were recorded as present or absent in 80 random quadrats on a stone wall: both present 16, only A present 32, only B present 24, neither 8. If the two species were distributed independently of each other, how many quadrats would be expected to contain both species?
Mission 3

Correlation

  • Plot two measured variables on a scatter graph (scattergram).
  • Positive correlation: both rise together. Negative: one rises as the other falls.
  • A correlation coefficient runs from −1 to +1.
  • The sign gives the direction; the size gives the strength. 0 means no correlation.
  • −0.85 is as strong as +0.85.
  • R² (coefficient of determination) = r². It is the proportion of variation in one variable explained by variation in the other. r = +0.90 → R² = 0.81: 81%.
  • Example: along a transect, plant cover can be correlated with an abiotic variable such as soil salinity or light intensity.
Quick check
?Across a range of animal species (illustrative data), the correlation coefficient between body size and nerve impulse conduction speed was r = −0.85. What does this value show?
Mission 3

Correlation is not cause

  • A correlation, even a strong one, can arise from:
    • chance (small samples);
    • a third variable linked to both (a confounding variable);
    • cause running the other way.
  • Evidence for cause: a mechanism; controlled experiments or randomised controlled trials; the effect grows with the dose; many studies agree.
  • Say: "there is a positive correlation between X and Y" — not: "X causes Y", unless there is evidence for cause.

Mission 4

Mission 4: How does water get from the soil into a root, and how do desert plants keep hold of it?

Make a guess: How does water get from the soil into a root, and how do desert plants keep hold of it?

Mission 4

Inside a root

  • Epidermis with root hairs: a huge surface for uptake.
  • Cortex: loosely packed cells; storage.
  • Endodermis: one ring of cells round the centre.
  • Central vascular cylinder (stele): xylem forms a star or X; phloem lies between its arms.
  • Root: xylem in the centre. Stem: xylem in a ring of bundles.
  • Water from the soil enters the root hairs by osmosis.
  • Desert plants (xerophytes) keep hold of water with deep or widespread roots, a thick waxy cuticle, sunken stomata, and rolled leaves or spines.
Quick check
?In a transverse section of a dicotyledonous root, where are the xylem and the phloem found?
Mission 4Practical

Drawing a root section

  • Draw a diagram from a micrograph.
  • Low power first: find the epidermis, cortex, endodermis and central cylinder.
  • Plan diagram: tissue outlines only, true proportions, no shading, ruled labels.
  • Check: xylem star in the centre, phloem between its arms. If you see a ring of bundles, it is a stem.
Mission check

?A micrograph shows a transverse section of an organ. It has an epidermis with long outgrowths, a wide cortex, and one central cylinder. In the cylinder, xylem forms a four-armed star with phloem between the arms. Which identification is correct?

Centre or ring: the pattern of the vascular tissue tells a root from a stem.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Cut every nerve to a heart and it keeps on beating. So what are those nerves for?

Make a guess: Cut every nerve to a heart and it keeps on beating. So what are those nerves for?

Mission 5

The heart starts its own beat

  • Cardiac muscle is myogenic: it contracts without any nerve impulse.
  • The sinoatrial node (SAN), in the wall of the right atrium, is the pacemaker.
  • It sends out a wave of electrical excitation at regular intervals. Each wave starts one heartbeat.
  • The wave spreads from the SAN across both atria, then on to the ventricles, so the chambers contract in order.
  • Nerves and hormones only change the rate of the SAN. They do not start the beats.
  • Say: "the SAN initiates each beat" — not: "the brain sends each heartbeat".
Mission 5

Nerves and hormones adjust the rate

  • A centre in the medulla oblongata controls heart rate.
  • Sympathetic nerve to the SAN → SAN fires faster → heart rate up; contractions stronger, so stroke volume up.
  • Parasympathetic nerve (the vagus) to the SAN → SAN fires more slowly → heart rate down.
  • The medulla sends impulses to the heart to change heart rate and stroke volume.
  • Say: "the medulla changes the rate of the SAN" — not: "the medulla makes the heart beat".
Mission 5

What the medulla senses

  • Baroreceptors (pressure receptors): stretch receptors in the walls of the aorta and carotid arteries. They monitor blood pressure.
  • Chemoreceptors: in the aorta and carotid arteries, and in the medulla. They monitor blood pH, CO2 and O2.
  • Blood pressure high → more baroreceptor impulses → medulla → more vagus impulses → rate falls.
  • Blood pressure low → fewer baroreceptor impulses → medulla → more sympathetic impulses → rate rises.
  • CO2 up, pH down (e.g. exercise) → chemoreceptors → medulla → sympathetic → rate rises → CO2 removed faster.
  • Each response reverses the change: negative feedback.

Mission check

?In a heart transplant, the nerves linking the donor heart to the medulla are cut, and they do not regrow for many months. Which statement about the transplanted heart is correct?

The heart makes its own beat; the nerves are the volume control, turning the rate up or down to match what the body needs.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Atheroma builds inside a damaged artery wall; it narrows the lumen and raises blood pressure.
  • A ruptured plaque triggers a clot; occlusion of a coronary artery starves heart muscle of oxygen.

Mission 2

  • A correlation coefficient gives direction (sign) and strength (size); even a strong correlation does not prove cause.

Mission 3

  • Null hypothesis → test statistic → compare with the critical value at p = 0.05 → significant or not. Neither outcome is proof.
  • χ² compares counts with expected counts; degrees of freedom = categories − 1.
  • A correlation coefficient measures direction and strength (−1 to +1); correlation alone never shows cause.

Mission 4

  • A dicot root has a central xylem star with phloem between the arms.

Mission 5

  • Cardiac muscle is myogenic: the SAN starts each beat; the AVN delays the wave; the bundle of His and Purkyne tissue carry it to the apex.
  • Baroreceptors and chemoreceptors → medulla → sympathetic (faster) or vagus (slower) nerves to the SAN.
