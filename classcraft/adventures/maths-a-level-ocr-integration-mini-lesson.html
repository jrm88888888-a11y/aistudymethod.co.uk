Integration · powers and areas

Integration reverses differentiation

  • If dxd​(F(x))=f(x)d/dx(F(x)) = f(x), then ∫f(x)dx=F(x)+c∫ f(x) dx = F(x) + c. This is the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus.
  • Power rule: add 1 to the power, then divide by the new power:
∫xndx=n+1xn+1​+c,n=−1∫ xⁿ dx = (xⁿ⁺¹)/(n+1) + c, n ≠ −1
  • cc is the constant of integration. Every indefinite integral needs it: x4+7x⁴ + 7 and x4−2x⁴ − 2 both differentiate to 4x34x³.
  • Integrate a sum term by term; constant multiples stay in front.

Integration · powers and areas

Definite integrals and area

  • A definite integral has limits and gives a number (no cc):
∫ab​f(x)dx=[F(x)]ab​=F(b)−F(a)∫_a^b f(x) dx = [F(x)]_a^b = F(b) − F(a)
  • Where the curve is above the xx-axis, this is the area between the curve, the axis and the lines x=ax = a, x=bx = b.
  • Below the axis the integral is negative. Split at the roots and add the sizes of the parts.
  • On a speed–time graph the area under the curve is the distance travelled: a rectangle at 20 m/s for 30 s gives 600 m.

Integration · standard integrals and areas

The one power the power rule misses (A-level only)

  • The power rule gives ∫xndx=n+1xn+1​+c∫ xⁿ dx = (xⁿ⁺¹)/(n+1) + c. For n=−1n = −1 it would need 0x0​x⁰/0, which is undefined.
  • But you already know a function with derivative x1​1/x: dxd​(lnx)=x1​d/dx(ln x) = 1/x.
  • Reverse it:
∫x1​dx=ln∣x∣+c∫ 1/x dx = ln |x| + c
  • The modulus is there because x1​1/x also exists for negative xx, where ln(−x)ln (−x) has derivative x1​1/x too.

Integration · standard integrals and areas

Area as the limit of a sum, and areas between curves

  • Split the area into thin strips of width δxδ x. Their total is close to the area, and the error vanishes as the strips get thinner:
∫ab​f(x)dx=δx→0lim​x=a∑b​f(x)δx∫_a^b f(x) dx = lim_(δ x → 0) Σ_(x = a)^b f(x) δ x
  • Between two curves, each strip has height top − bottom. So the area is ∫ab​(top−bottom)dx∫_a^b (top − bottom) dx.
  • The limits aa, bb are usually where the curves meet: solve their equations simultaneously.
  • Top − bottom works even where both curves are below the xx-axis.

Integration · substitution and parts

Substitution reverses the chain rule (A-level only)

  • Choose a new variable uu, usually the "inside" of a bracket or root, e.g. u=2x−1u = 2x − 1.
  • Differentiate it: dxdu​=2du/dx = 2, so replace dxdx by 21​du1/2 du.
  • Rewrite every xx in terms of uu, then integrate with respect to uu.
  • For a definite integral, change the limits to uu-values; then you never need to go back to xx.

Integration · substitution and parts

Integration by parts reverses the product rule

  • Integrate the product rule and rearrange:
∫udxdv​dx=uv−∫vdxdu​dx∫ udv/dx dx = uv − ∫ vdu/dx dx
  • It helps when the new integral ∫vdxdu​dx∫ vdu/dx dx is easier than the old one.
  • So choose uu to be the factor that gets simpler when differentiated (a power of xx), and dxdv​dv/dx one you can integrate (ekxe^kx, sinkxsin kx, coskxcos kx).
  • Exception: with lnxln x, make u=lnxu = ln x, since you cannot easily integrate it. Write lnxln x as 1×lnx1 × ln x if it is alone.
  • With x2x², apply parts twice.

Integration · differential equations

Turning words into a differential equation (A-level only)

  • "Rate of change of NN" means dtdN​dN/dt.
  • "Proportional to XX" means =kX= kX, with kk a positive constant. "Inversely proportional to XX" means =Xk​= k/X.
  • If the quantity is decreasing, put in a minus sign so that kk stays positive.
  • Example: a liquid cools at a rate proportional to how far it is above room temperature 20∘C20 °C: dtdθ​=−k(θ−20)dθ/dt = −k(θ − 20).

Integration · differential equations

Separating the variables

  • If dxdy​=f(x)g(y)dy/dx = f(x) g(y), move all the yy terms to one side and all the xx terms to the other:
∫g(y)1​dy=∫f(x)dx∫ 1/(g(y)) dy = ∫ f(x) dx
  • You may need to factorise first: 2xy+2x=2x(y+1)2xy + 2x = 2x(y + 1).
  • Integrate both sides, adding one constant cc. This is the general solution: a family of curves.
  • A given condition (e.g. y=0y = 0 when x=0x = 0) fixes cc and gives the particular solution.

Recap

Big ideas

  • ∫xndx=n+1xn+1​+c∫ xⁿ dx = (xⁿ⁺¹)/(n+1) + c (n=−1n ≠ −1): rewrite roots and fractions as powers first, and never forget +c+ c.
  • A point on the curve fixes cc; a definite integral F(b)−F(a)F(b) − F(a) needs no cc.
  • Area under a curve = definite integral, but parts below the xx-axis come out negative: split at the roots.
  • ∫x1​dx=ln∣x∣+c∫ 1/x dx = ln |x| + c; also ∫ekxdx=k1​ekx+c∫ e^kx dx = 1/ke^kx + c, ∫sinkxdx=−k1​coskx+c∫ sin kx dx = −1/kcos kx + c, ∫coskxdx=k1​sinkx+c∫ cos kx dx = 1/ksin kx + c.
  • ∫f(x)f′(x)​dx=ln∣f(x)∣+c∫ (f'(x))/(f(x)) dx = ln |f(x)| + c; use double-angle identities for sin2xsin²x and cos2xcos²x.
  • An integral is the limit of a sum of thin strips; between two curves, integrate top − bottom between their meeting points.
  • Substitution reverses the chain rule: replace xx, dxdx and the limits.
  • By parts reverses the product rule: ∫udxdv​dx=uv−∫vdxdu​dx∫ udv/dx dx = uv − ∫ vdu/dx dx, with uu the factor that simplifies (or u=lnxu = ln x).
  • Rational functions with linear factors: split into partial fractions, then each term gives a logarithm.
  • Translate words into a differential equation: rate → derivative, proportional → k×k×, decreasing → minus sign.
  • Separate the variables, integrate both sides with one constant (general solution), then use a condition to fix it (particular solution).
  • Read the solution in context: what happens for large tt, and where the model stops being realistic.
