Where it began

The Buddha — the Four Sights

Buddhism began with Siddhartha Gautama, a prince who grew up sheltered in a palace. On journeys outside, he saw four sights that changed his life — often called the Four Sights:

  • an old man — showing that everyone grows old;
  • a sick man — showing that everyone can suffer illness;
  • a dead man — showing that everyone dies;
  • a holy man (an ascetic) — calm despite the suffering around him.

What happened next: the first three sights showed Siddhartha that life is full of suffering; the holy man suggested a way to face it. He gave up his royal life (renunciation) to search for the truth.

Key vocabulary

Words you must be able to define

  • Enlightenment — waking up to the true nature of reality; the Buddha reached this under the Bodhi tree.
  • Dukkha — suffering, or the sense that life is unsatisfactory.
  • Dhamma (Sanskrit: Dharma) — the Buddha's teachings about the truth of things.
  • Nibbana (Sanskrit: nirvana) — liberation; the state of being free from suffering and rebirth.
  • Non-theism — a worldview that does not centre on belief in a creator God.

Note the two languages: Buddhist terms come in Pali and Sanskrit forms — e.g. nibbana (Pali) / nirvana (Sanskrit). Both name the same idea: enlightenment and freedom from suffering, not a creator-God.

Enlightenment

Awakening under the Bodhi tree

After leaving the palace, Siddhartha tried extreme asceticism (starving himself), but found it did not lead to the truth. He chose instead a Middle Way between luxury and self-denial. Sitting in deep meditation under the Bodhi tree, he reached enlightenment — from then on he was called the Buddha ("the awakened one").

Key idea: the Buddha was a human teacher who awoke to the truth — not a creator God. Buddhists follow his example and his teaching (the Dhamma) rather than worshipping him as God.
Watch out

The Buddha is not a creator God

A very common exam error is to say Buddhists worship the Buddha as a God who created the world. This is wrong. The Buddha was a human being who reached enlightenment; Buddhism is non-theistic — the path to nibbana does not depend on a creator God.

Say it like this: "Buddhism is non-theistic. The Buddha is a teacher and example, not a creator God — Buddhists follow his teaching (the Dhamma) to end suffering themselves." That single sentence protects you from the most common mistake in this topic.

The Noble Eightfold Path

The path that ends suffering

The Fourth Noble Truth (magga) is the Noble Eightfold Path — eight ways of living and thinking that lead to nibbana. They are usually grouped into three areas:

  • Wisdom (prajna) — right understanding and right intention.
  • Ethics (sila) — right speech, right action and right livelihood.
  • Meditation (samadhi) — right effort, right mindfulness and right concentration.
Not a straight line: the eight are practised together, not one after another — which is why the path is often shown as the eight spokes of a wheel (the Dhamma Wheel / Dharmachakra).

By developing wisdom, living ethically and training the mind, a Buddhist reduces craving and moves towards the end of suffering.

Karma, rebirth & samsara

The cycle of rebirth

Buddhists believe in samsara — the repeating cycle of birth, death and rebirth. What shapes each rebirth is karma:

  • Karma — the moral law that our actions have consequences: skilful (kind) actions lead to good results, unskilful (harmful) ones to bad results.
  • Rebirth — when a being dies, karmic energy leads on to a new life; the aim is to break free of the cycle.

Reaching nibbana means being freed from samsara — no longer being reborn, and free from suffering and craving.

Careful: Buddhist rebirth is not a fixed soul moving between bodies. Because of anatta (no permanent self), it is the flow of karmic energy that continues, not an unchanging soul.

Practice: meditation

Training the mind

Meditation is a central Buddhist practice — a way of calming and training the mind so it can see reality clearly. Two important types are:

  • Samatha — "calm abiding": focusing on the breath or an object to develop calm and concentration.
  • Vipassana — "insight": watching thoughts and feelings to gain insight into anicca, dukkha and anatta.

Through meditation Buddhists develop mindfulness and reduce craving, supporting the meditation (samadhi) section of the Eightfold Path.

Careful: Buddhist meditation is not praying to a God — it is a form of mental training and insight, in keeping with Buddhism's non-theistic path.

Practices: refuge, precepts & puja

Living as a Buddhist

Buddhists commit to the path by taking the Three Refuges (Three Jewels) — turning to the Buddha (the teacher), the Dhamma (his teaching) and the Sangha (the Buddhist community) for guidance.

Many Buddhists also try to keep the Five Precepts — guidelines for ethical living:

  • do not harm living beings; do not take what is not given;
  • avoid sexual misconduct; avoid false or harmful speech;
  • avoid drink and drugs that cloud the mind.
Worship (puja): Buddhists show respect at home or in the temple through puja — bowing, offering flowers, candles and incense before a Buddha image, and chanting. Festivals such as Wesak celebrate the Buddha's birth, enlightenment and death.

Two main traditions

Theravada and Mahayana

Buddhism has two main traditions — easy to muddle, so know the difference:

  • Theravada — "the teaching of the elders"; found mainly in Sri Lanka and SE Asia. It stresses following the Buddha's original teaching and the ideal of the arhat (one who reaches enlightenment through their own effort).
  • Mahayana — "the great vehicle"; found mainly in China, Japan and Tibet. It stresses the bodhisattva ideal — seeking enlightenment in order to help all beings.

Both share the core teachings — the Four Noble Truths, the Eightfold Path and the Three Marks — but emphasise them in different ways.

Memory hook: Theravada = the over (elders') original way; Mahayana = the many / great vehicle for all beings.

Why it matters

How these beliefs shape lives

Exam questions often ask about the impact of Buddhist beliefs. They are not just ideas — they shape how Buddhists live:

  • Believing in karma and rebirth encourages kind, skilful actions and avoiding harm.
  • Believing in anicca (impermanence) helps Buddhists let go and not cling to possessions or feelings.
  • Following the Five Precepts shapes honest, non-violent, mindful daily behaviour.
  • Practising meditation builds calm, mindfulness and insight, supporting the Eightfold Path.
  • A non-theistic view directs effort toward personal enlightenment (nibbana) and ethical living rather than worship of a creator God.

Recap

The ideas to know

The Buddha: Siddhartha Gautama; the Four Sights; renunciation; enlightenment under the Bodhi tree.

Three Marks: dukkha (suffering), anicca (impermanence), anatta (no fixed self).

Four Noble Truths: dukkha, samudaya (craving/tanha), nirodha, magga (the Eightfold Path).

Eightfold Path: wisdom (prajna), ethics (sila), meditation (samadhi) → nibbana.

Karma, rebirth, samsara: actions have consequences; the cycle of rebirth; nibbana frees you from it.

Non-theistic: the path does not depend on a creator God.

Practices: meditation (samatha & vipassana), puja, Three Refuges, Five Precepts, Wesak.
