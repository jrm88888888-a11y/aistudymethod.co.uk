Mission 1

Who was Siddhattha?

  • Buddhists call him the Buddha: "the awakened one". Before his awakening he was Siddhattha Gotama (Sanskrit: Siddhartha Gautama).
  • Ancestry: born into the Sakya (Shakya) clan, in the kshatriya class of rulers and warriors.
  • His father, Suddhodana, ruled the Sakyas from Kapilavatthu, in the foothills of the Himalayas (the border of modern Nepal and India).
  • His mother was Queen Maya (Mahamaya).
  • Dates: traditionally 6th–5th century BCE; many historians now prefer the 5th–4th century BCE.
  • Significance: Buddhists see the Buddha as a human being, not a god. They believe anyone can follow the path he found.
Mission 1

Before he was born

  • Buddhists believe Siddhattha had lived many lives before. The Jataka stories tell of them: in each he built up qualities such as generosity, patience and wisdom.
  • Ages earlier, as a hermit named Sumedha, he met an earlier Buddha, Dipankara, and vowed to become a Buddha himself (told in the Buddhavamsa).
  • In his last life before birth he lived in the Tusita heaven and, the stories say, chose his time, place and parents.
  • Queen Maya dreamed that a white elephant entered her side. Wise men said she would bear a great son.
Mission 1

The birth in Lumbini

  • Maya set out to give birth at her parents' home. On the way, in the Lumbini grove, she gave birth standing, holding the branch of a sal tree.
  • The stories say the baby took seven steps, a lotus springing up at each, and said this was his last birth.
  • Maya died seven days later. Her sister, Mahapajapati Gotami, raised the boy.
  • He was named Siddhattha: "one who achieves his aim".
  • Lumbini is today a place of pilgrimage. Many Buddhists celebrate the birth at Wesak (Vesak).
Mission 1

The prophecy

  • A sage, Asita, came to see the baby. He smiled, then wept: the child would become a Buddha, but Asita would die before hearing him teach.
  • At the naming, eight wise men (brahmins) examined the baby. Seven said: he will be either a great world ruler or, if he leaves home, a Buddha.
  • The youngest, Kondanna, said he would certainly become a Buddha. (Kondanna later became one of his first five followers.)
  • Suddhodana wanted a king, not a wandering holy man. So he decided to keep all signs of suffering away from his son.

  • The Buddhavamsa ("Chronicle of Buddhas") is a Pali text in the Sutta Pitaka. Chapter XXVI is about Gotama Buddha himself (paraphrase):

  • Having seen the four signs, he left on horseback and strove for six years.

Mission 1

A life of luxury

  • Suddhodana gave his son three palaces, one for each season: cold, hot and rainy.
  • Music, fine clothes, the best food; servants everywhere. Sick and old people were kept out of sight.
  • Siddhattha was trained in the skills of a prince.
  • At about 16 he married his cousin Yasodhara. Their son was Rahula.
  • Significance: he had everything the world can offer. So when he later left, Buddhists see it as proof that wealth and pleasure alone cannot end suffering.
Mission 1

The Four Sights

  • At 29, Siddhattha went out of the palace with his charioteer, Channa. On four trips he saw:
  • 1. an old man: everyone grows old;
  • 2. a sick man: everyone can fall ill;
  • 3. a corpse (a dead body): everyone dies;
  • 4. a holy man (a wandering ascetic), calm and content.
  • Each time he asked Channa: "Will this happen to me?" Channa: yes, to everyone.
  • The first three showed him suffering. The fourth showed a possible way out: leaving home to search for the truth.
Quick check

Mission 1

The Great Renunciation

  • That night Siddhattha looked at his sleeping wife and baby son, then left the palace.
  • He rode his horse Kanthaka, with Channa, to a river. There he cut off his hair and changed into simple robes.
  • He became a samana: a wandering seeker with no home.
  • Buddhists call this the Great Renunciation ("giving up").
  • Significance: Buddhists see it as the start of the search that led to the Dhamma. Monks and nuns who shave their heads and wear robes follow the same pattern.
Mission 1Diversity

How Buddhists read the stories

  • Theravada Buddhists stress that Siddhattha was a human being who reached Enlightenment by his own effort, after many lives of preparation.
  • Mahayana Buddhists (e.g. the Lotus Sutra) teach that the Buddha was enlightened long ages ago. His life on earth was a skilful means (upaya) to teach beings.
  • Some Buddhists take the wonders (seven steps, white elephant) literally. Others read them as symbols: the lotus for purity, seven steps for his greatness.
  • The oldest texts (e.g. Anguttara Nikaya 3.38) describe the Buddha reflecting on old age, sickness and death, with no story of four outings. Many Buddhists say the meaning matters more than the exact events.
  • All agree on the message: suffering touches everyone, and there is a way out.
Mission 1Worked answer

Belief, source, development

Question: Explain two reasons why the Four Sights are important to Buddhists today.

  • Reason 1: they show that suffering (old age, sickness, death) affects everyone, even a protected prince. Development: so Buddhists try to face suffering honestly rather than hide from it; this leads to the First Noble Truth.
  • Reason 2: the holy man shows a way out: Siddhattha left home to seek the truth. Source: Buddhavamsa XXVI says that, having seen the four signs, he left home and strove for six years. Development: many Buddhists see the monastic life, or regular meditation, as following his example.
  • (c) 5 marks: two developed reasons and a source of wisdom and authority, such as Buddhavamsa XXVI.

Mission 2

Learning from teachers

  • After leaving home at 29, Siddhattha became a samana: a wandering seeker.
  • He studied meditation with two famous teachers, Alara Kalama and Uddaka Ramaputta.
  • He quickly reached the deep, calm states of mind they taught.
  • But when he came out of meditation, old age, sickness and death were still there. These states did not end suffering.
  • So he left both teachers to search on his own.
Mission 2

Six years as an ascetic

  • Many seekers in India believed that punishing the body would free the mind. This is asceticism.
  • With five companions, Siddhattha tried the hardest practices: holding his breath, standing for hours, living on almost no food.
  • He grew so thin that, in his own later account, he could feel his spine through his stomach (Majjhima Nikaya 36, paraphrase).
  • He nearly died. He had pushed harder than anyone, and still had not found the answer.
  • His conclusion: extreme self-denial weakens the mind; it does not lead to awakening.
Mission 2

The Middle Way

  • He remembered a day in childhood: sitting under a rose-apple tree, he had slipped into calm, happy meditation, without any pain (Majjhima Nikaya 36).
  • Perhaps calm, clear meditation was the way.
  • He accepted a bowl of milk-rice from a young woman, Sujata, and grew strong again.
  • His five companions thought he had given up, and left him.
  • He had found the Middle Way: neither a life of luxury (the palace) nor harsh self-punishment (the forest).
  • The Middle Way runs through all Buddhist practice, from diet to meditation.

Mission 2

The night under the Bodhi tree

  • At Bodh Gaya (north-east India), at about 35, he sat beneath a fig tree, later called the Bodhi tree ("tree of awakening").
  • He vowed not to get up until he had found the truth.
  • Mara, the tempter (in Buddhism, the power of desire, fear and death), tried to stop him:
  • armies and storms to frighten him; Mara's daughters to tempt him; doubt: "who are you to claim this seat?"
  • Buddhist tradition tells that Siddhattha touched the ground. The earth bore witness to his many lives of good deeds, and Mara fled.
  • Many statues show this moment: the earth-touching gesture (bhumisparsha mudra).
Mission 2

Enlightenment

  • Buddhists believe that through the night, in deep meditation, he came to three knowledges (one in each watch of the night):
  • 1. he remembered his past lives;
  • 2. he saw beings dying and being reborn according to their kamma (karma: intentional actions);
  • 3. he understood the Four Noble Truths and was freed from craving and ignorance.
  • At dawn he was Enlightened: the Buddha, "the awakened one". Buddhists believe he was now free from suffering and would not be reborn.

Mission 2

The first sermon and the Sangha

  • At first the Buddha doubted anyone could understand. Buddhist texts say the god Brahma Sahampati begged him to teach (Samyutta Nikaya 6.1).
  • He went to the Deer Park at Sarnath, near Benares (Varanasi), and found his five old companions.
  • He taught them the Middle Way and the Four Noble Truths. This first sermon is called "Setting in motion the Wheel of the Dhamma" (Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta).
  • Kondanna understood first. The five became the first bhikkhus (monks): the start of the Sangha (community).
  • Soon the Buddha sent followers out to teach "for the welfare of many".
  • Later, his foster-mother Mahapajapati became the first bhikkhuni (nun).
  • Significance: the Sangha is one of the Three Jewels (see Mission 4) and still passes on the Dhamma today.
Mission 2Read the source

The first sermon

Samyutta Nikaya 56.11, Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta (paraphrase):

  • There are two extremes a seeker should not follow: chasing sense pleasures, which is low and unprofitable, and tormenting oneself, which is painful and unprofitable.
  • Avoiding both, the Buddha found the Middle Way. It gives vision and knowledge, and leads to peace, awakening and nibbana.
  • This Middle Way is the Noble Eightfold Path.

Mission 2Diversity

Different emphases

  • Theravada: the Buddha was a human being who found the path by his own effort; his example matters most. Followers aim to become arahants (fully freed ones) by the path he taught.
  • Mahayana: the Buddha shows what every being can become, since all have Buddha-nature. Many teach that he was enlightened long before his life on earth (Lotus Sutra) and that his death was a skilful lesson about impermanence.
  • Zen: stresses that he woke up by meditation, not by study; Zen practice centres on sitting meditation.
  • Pure Land: stresses the help of another Buddha, Amitabha, as well as Gotama's example.
  • All honour the Enlightenment as the moment the Dhamma began.
Mission 2Evaluate

"The Enlightenment is the most important event in the Buddha's life."

  • Reasons for: it made Siddhattha a Buddha; without it there would be no Dhamma to teach. Bodh Gaya is the most important pilgrimage place for many Buddhists. Wesak celebrates it.
  • Reasons against: without the Four Sights there would have been no search. Without the first sermon and the Sangha, no one else would have heard the Dhamma.
  • Another view: Buddhists honour three jewels: the Buddha, the Dhamma and the Sangha. Each event served all three.
  • Judge it by: Which event changed the most for other people? Which do Buddhists remember most in practice? Can one event be separated from the rest of the story?

Mission 3

What is the Sangha?

  • Sangha: "community". The third of the Three Jewels: Buddha, Dhamma, Sangha.
  • Narrow meaning: the monastic community of monks (bhikkhus) and nuns (bhikkhunis).
  • Wider meaning: the "fourfold assembly": monks, nuns, laymen and laywomen.
  • Deepest meaning: the noble Sangha: all who have reached at least the first stage of awakening.
  • Many Mahayana and Western Buddhists use "sangha" for any group practising together.
Mission 3

How the Sangha began

  • After his Enlightenment, the Buddha taught his first sermon to five ascetics at the Deer Park, Sarnath.
  • One, Kondanna, understood, and asked to join him: the first monk. The others soon followed.
  • Within months there were sixty arhats. The Buddha sent them out to teach "for the welfare of the many".
  • Later, his stepmother and aunt Mahapajapati led women who asked to be ordained: the first nuns.
  • Buddhavamsa XXVI (paraphrase) names Gotama's chief disciples: monks Sariputta and Moggallana; nuns Khema and Uppalavanna; his attendant Ananda.
Mission 3

Going for refuge

  • Buddhists recite the Three Refuges (Tisarana), often three times:
  • Buddham saranam gacchami: I go for refuge to the Buddha (the teacher).
  • Dhammam saranam gacchami: … to the Dhamma (the teaching).
  • Sangham saranam gacchami: … to the Sangha (the community).
  • A refuge is a safe place: a shelter from dukkha that does not change.
  • Saying the refuges marks someone as a Buddhist; they are chanted at the start of most ceremonies.
Quick check

Mission 3

Giving and receiving

  • Monks and nuns depend on lay people; lay people depend on them.
  • Lay people give (dana): food, robes, medicine, shelter.
  • The monastic Sangha gives: teaching, chanting, blessings, advice, and an example of the path.
  • The alms round: in Theravada countries, monks walk silently with bowls each morning; people place food in them.
  • Giving makes merit; the monk's silence lets the giver give freely.
  • Kathina festival, after the rains retreat: lay people offer new robe cloth.

Mission 4

What does "Dhamma" mean?

  • Dhamma is Pali; Dharma is Sanskrit. Buddhists use it in three main ways.
  • 1. The Buddha's teaching: the Four Noble Truths, the Eightfold Path and all he taught.
  • 2. The truth about how things are: a natural law the Buddha discovered, not one he invented. It would be true even if no Buddha taught it.
  • 3. dhammas (small d): the passing events of body and mind that make up experience.
  • The Buddha compared his teaching to a raft: something to cross the river with, not to carry on your head afterwards (Majjhima Nikaya 22, paraphrase).
Quick check

Mission 4

Dependent arising

  • Spelling: paticca-samuppada; also called dependent origination or conditionality.
  • The idea: everything arises from conditions, and ceases when those conditions cease.
  • The Buddha's short formula (e.g. Majjhima Nikaya 79, paraphrase): when this is, that is; when this arises, that arises. When this is not, that is not; when this ceases, that ceases.
  • Example: a flame burns only while there is fuel and air. Take one away and it goes out.
  • So nothing exists on its own, unchanging and independent.
Quick check
?Which statement best expresses the teaching of dependent arising?
Mission 4

Why it matters: samsara and kamma

  • Samsara: the endless cycle of birth, death and rebirth, driven by craving and ignorance.
  • Kamma (karma): intentional actions of body, speech and mind. They shape a being's circumstances in this life and future lives.
  • Rebirth is a chain of conditions, not a soul moving house: like one candle lighting another (Milinda Panha, paraphrase).
  • Suffering has conditions. Remove the conditions (craving, ignorance) and suffering ceases: nibbana.
  • So dependent arising explains both how suffering keeps going and how it can end.
  • The Buddha also set dependent arising out as a chain of twelve links (nidanas), from ignorance to ageing and death.
  • "Whoever sees dependent arising sees the Dhamma" (Majjhima Nikaya 28, paraphrase).
Mission 4

The Wheel of Life

  • The Wheel of Life (Sanskrit bhavacakra) is a picture of samsara, painted at the entrance of many Tibetan monasteries.
  • Held by Yama, a fearsome figure for death and impermanence: nothing in the wheel escapes change.
  • Hub: three animals for the Three Poisons (see Mission 7).
  • Second ring: beings rising (light half) and falling (dark half) according to their kamma.
  • Third ring: the six realms of rebirth: gods, demi-gods, humans, animals, hungry ghosts, hell beings.
  • Rim: twelve pictures of the twelve nidanas (e.g. a blind person for ignorance, a potter for formations).
  • Outside: the Buddha points to the moon: nibbana, the way out.

Mission 4Diversity

Different understandings

  • Theravada: the Dhamma is the teaching preserved in the Pali Canon. The twelve links are usually read over three lifetimes (past, present and future: Buddhaghosa, Visuddhimagga, 5th century CE).
  • Mahayana: Nagarjuna (c. 150–250 CE), Mulamadhyamakakarika 24.18: whatever arises dependently is empty (sunyata) of fixed existence (paraphrase).
  • Many Tibetan and modern teachers also read the twelve links moment by moment: one angry thought can run round the whole chain.
  • Zen: the Dhamma is realised directly in experience, "outside the scriptures", through meditation.
Mission 4Evaluate

"Dependent arising is the key to all the Buddha's teaching."

  • Reasons for: it explains suffering (conditions), rebirth (no soul needed) and impermanence (everything changes). Majjhima Nikaya 28 links seeing dependent arising with seeing the Dhamma. Nagarjuna builds Mahayana thought on it.
  • Reasons against: the Buddha's first sermon taught the Four Noble Truths, not the twelve links. The Dhamma is a raft for practice: meditation and ethics may matter more than theory. Pure Land Buddhists stress faith in Amitabha Buddha.
  • Another view: the Four Noble Truths are dependent arising applied to suffering, so the two cannot be separated.
  • Judge it by: Which teaching explains the most? Which changes how Buddhists live? Do ordinary lay Buddhists need the theory?

Mission 5

Three marks of everything

  • The Buddha taught that everything conditioned has three marks (Pali tilakkhana):
  • anicca: impermanence;
  • dukkha: unsatisfactoriness, suffering;
  • anatta: no fixed self.
  • They follow from dependent arising (see Mission 4): what depends on conditions must change, cannot fully satisfy, and has no independent core.
Mission 5

Anicca: everything changes

  • Anicca: nothing stays the same. Things arise, change and pass away.
  • Big changes: seasons, ageing, death, mountains wearing away.
  • Small changes: your body's cells, your thoughts and moods, moment by moment.
  • Even things that seem solid are processes.
Mission 5

Dukkha: nothing fully satisfies

  • Dukkha is often translated "suffering", but it is wider: unsatisfactoriness.
  • Pain is dukkha. But so is a happy moment, because it will end.
  • Because everything is impermanent (anicca), nothing can give lasting satisfaction.
  • This is not saying "life is miserable". It is saying: do not expect permanent happiness from things that change.
Mission 5

Anatta: no fixed self

  • Anatta: there is no fixed, permanent self or soul at the core of a person.
  • Edexcel: no fixed soul or essence.
  • It does not mean you do not exist. You exist as a changing process, like a river or a flame.
  • Many religions of the Buddha's time taught an eternal soul (atman). The Buddha rejected this.
  • Buddhists still believe in rebirth: what continues, they say, is a stream of causes and effects, not a soul (see Mission 4).

Mission 5

The five khandhas

  • If there is no fixed self, what is a person? The Buddha's answer: five khandhas ("heaps", "aggregates") working together.
  • 1. rupa: form, the body;
  • 2. vedana: feelings, pleasant, unpleasant or neutral;
  • 3. sanna: perception, recognising things;
  • 4. sankhara: mental formations, such as will and motivation;
  • 5. vinnana: consciousness, awareness.
  • All five are always changing. None of them is a fixed self.
Mission 5Read the source

Nagasena and the chariot

From the Milinda Panha ("Questions of King Milinda"), Book II, chapter 1 (paraphrase). King Milinda, a Greek ruler in north-west India, asks the monk Nagasena his name.

  • Nagasena: "I am called Nagasena, but that is only a name. No permanent person is found here."
  • He asks the king how he came. "By chariot."
  • "Is the pole the chariot? The axle? The wheels? The frame? The reins?" No, says the king, to each.
  • "Is the chariot something apart from these parts?" No.
  • The king explains: "chariot" is a name used for these parts put together.
  • Nagasena: just so, "Nagasena" is a name for the khandhas put together. Beyond them there is no fixed self.

Mission 5Diversity

Theravada and Mahayana

  • All Buddhists accept the three marks. They develop them differently.
  • Theravada: anatta is seen by analysing a person into the khandhas and seeing each one change. Meditation (vipassana, insight) looks at the marks directly.
  • Mahayana: extends no-self to everything: all things are empty (sunyata) of fixed existence.
  • Many Mahayana schools also teach Buddha-nature: every being can become a Buddha. Most Mahayana teachers say this is a potential, not a hidden self.
  • Both speak of two levels of truth: in everyday speech "I" and "you" are fine; in ultimate terms there is no fixed self.
Mission 5

Why the marks matter

  • Letting go: if everything changes, clinging only brings disappointment. Buddhists try to enjoy things without grasping.
  • Facing loss: at many Theravada funerals monks chant that all conditioned things are impermanent. Death is part of anicca.
  • Compassion: if there is no fixed self to protect, there is less reason for selfishness; others' suffering matters as much as one's own.
  • Practice: insight meditation contemplates the marks, to weaken craving.
Mission 5Evaluate

"If there is no fixed self, there is no reason to be good."

  • Reasons for: if no self continues, who receives the results of kamma? King Milinda himself asks this. Some argue moral responsibility needs a lasting person.
  • Reasons against: a process continues, like a flame passed between candles; kamma shapes that stream. Seeing no-self weakens selfishness, which supports kindness. Suffering is real whoever feels it, so it still matters to reduce it.
  • Another view: Buddhists use "self" in everyday truth: people are responsible for their actions, even if no self exists ultimately.
  • Judge it by: Does moral responsibility need an unchanging self? Does belief in no-self change behaviour? Do the two levels of truth answer the objection?

Mission 6

Four truths, like a doctor

  • The Buddha's first sermon, at Sarnath, taught the Four Noble Truths (Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta, Samyutta Nikaya 56.11):
  • 1. dukkha: there is suffering;
  • 2. samudaya: suffering has a cause (craving, tanha);
  • 3. nirodha: suffering can end;
  • 4. magga: there is a path to its end, the Eightfold Path.
  • Buddhists often compare the Buddha to a doctor: diagnosis (the illness), cause, prognosis (it can be cured), treatment.
Mission 6Read the source

The First Truth

Samyutta Nikaya 56.11, Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta (paraphrase):

  • Birth is dukkha; ageing is dukkha; sickness is dukkha; death is dukkha.
  • Sorrow, grief, pain and despair are dukkha.
  • Being with what you dislike is dukkha; being parted from what you love is dukkha.
  • Not getting what you want is dukkha.
  • In short, the five khandhas that we cling to are dukkha.

Q1 Which items on the list match the Four Sights?

Show answer

Ageing, sickness, death.

Q2 Which item covers even a happy life?

Show answer

The five khandhas we cling to: everything we are is impermanent.

Mission 6

What "dukkha" means

  • Dukkha has no single English word. Translations include suffering, unsatisfactoriness, stress, dis-ease.
  • It covers pain and grief, but also the restlessness of never being fully satisfied.
  • The First Truth is a fact to understand, not a feeling to wallow in.
  • Buddhists believe it applies to all beings in samsara, not just humans: animals, ghosts and even gods.
  • Edexcel's three types: pain, change, fabrication (next screen).
Mission 6

Three types of dukkha

  • 1. Dukkha-dukkha: ordinary pain, of body and mind: a broken arm, grief, fear.
  • 2. Viparinama-dukkha: the suffering of change: pleasant things end, so even happiness carries loss.
  • 3. Sankhara-dukkha: the suffering of conditioned existence: everything is made of changing parts (khandhas), so there is a deep, built-in unsatisfactoriness.
  • Edexcel calls type 3 suffering as fabrication: everything is "fabricated", put together from conditions.
Quick check
?Lily loves her summer holiday, but it has to end. Which type of dukkha does the fact that a happy holiday cannot last illustrate?
Mission 6Read the source

The mind and suffering

> "All that we are is the result of what we have thought: it is founded on our thoughts, it is made up of our thoughts. If a man speaks or acts with an evil thought, pain follows him, as the wheel follows the foot of the ox that draws the carriage." (Dhammapada 1, Müller)

  • The verse links suffering to the mind: harmful thoughts lead to harmful actions, and pain follows as surely as a cart follows the ox.

Q1 What does the wheel following the ox show?

Show answer

Pain follows evil thought as an inevitable result.

Q2 Which Noble Truth does this verse point towards?

Show answer

The second: suffering has a cause.

Mission 6

Realistic, not pessimistic

  • Buddhists say the First Truth is realistic: it names what everyone faces.
  • The Truths do not stop at suffering: the Third says it can end; the Fourth shows how.
  • Understanding dukkha is the first step to compassion (karuna): all beings suffer, just as I do.
  • Importance today: Buddhists meet suffering with honesty and kindness, e.g. hospice care and meditation for pain.
Mission 6Diversity

Can every being be freed?

  • All Buddhists agree that dukkha can end. They differ in emphasis.
  • Theravada: each person must walk the path; the goal is to become an arahant, freed from suffering. The Buddha shows the way; he cannot walk it for you.
  • Mahayana: all sentient beings have Buddha-nature and can be liberated. The Bodhisattva vows to work until all beings are freed.
  • Pure Land: the Buddha Amitabha vowed to bring all who call on him in faith to his Pure Land, where awakening is easier.
Quick check

Mission 6Evaluate

"Buddhism is a pessimistic religion."

  • Reasons for: the first teaching is about suffering. Even happiness is called dukkha. Birth itself is on the list.
  • Reasons against: the First Truth is a diagnosis; a doctor who names an illness is not pessimistic. The Third Truth says suffering can end; the Fourth gives a practical path. The Dhammapada calls nibbana "the highest happiness" (verse 204).
  • Another view: Buddhists call their outlook realistic: neither ignoring suffering nor despairing of it.
  • Judge it by: Does naming suffering make a view pessimistic? Do Buddhists live with hope? Which of the Four Truths is the "last word"?

For Buddhists, dukkha is not only pain. Even the best moments change, so nothing conditioned can give lasting satisfaction. The next truth asks why.

Mission 7

The Second Truth: samudaya

  • Samudaya means "arising" or "origin": suffering has a cause.
  • The Buddha named the cause tanha: literally "thirst", usually translated craving.
  • Craving is wanting things to be other than they are: holding on to what changes, pushing away what is unpleasant.
  • Because everything is impermanent (anicca), craving can never be fully satisfied. So it keeps producing dukkha.
  • Craving also keeps beings in samsara: it drives rebirth.
Mission 7

Three kinds of craving

  • 1. Kama-tanha: craving for sense pleasures: tastes, sights, sounds, comfort.
  • 2. Bhava-tanha: craving to be or become: to exist, to be someone, to be admired.
  • 3. Vibhava-tanha: craving not to be: to get rid of something, to escape, to disappear.
  • Even wanting something to stop can be craving.
Quick check
?Jay has a hard conversation coming up. He keeps wishing he could just disappear so he never has to face it. Which kind of craving is this?
Mission 7Read the source

The Sermon at Benares

The first sermon was given in the Deer Park near Benares (Varanasi): the Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta, Samyutta Nikaya 56.11. Its Second Truth (paraphrase):

  • The origin of suffering is craving, which leads to renewed existence (rebirth).
  • It comes with delight and passion, seeking pleasure now here, now there.
  • It is craving for sense pleasures, craving for existence, craving for non-existence.
  • Your spec's "Sermon at Benares" is this same first sermon: use it as your source for samudaya.

Q1 Name the three kinds of craving in the sutta.

Show answer

Sense pleasures; existence; non-existence.

Q2 What does craving lead to, beyond suffering in this life?

Show answer

Renewed existence: rebirth.

Mission 7

The Three Poisons

  • Craving grows from three roots of unskilful action, called the Three Poisons (or three fires):
  • lobha: greed, attachment, grasping;
  • dosa: hatred, aversion, anger;
  • moha: ignorance, delusion, confusion.
  • Ignorance is the deepest: not seeing the three marks; treating what changes as permanent, and a changing process as a fixed self.
  • From ignorance come greed (grabbing what we think will last) and hatred (pushing away what threatens "me").
Mission 7

The poisons in the Wheel of Life

  • At the very hub of the Wheel of Life (see Mission 4) are three animals:
  • a pig: ignorance (moha);
  • a rooster (cockerel): greed (lobha);
  • a snake: hatred (dosa).
  • In many paintings each animal bites the tail of the next: the poisons feed each other in an endless circle.
  • At the hub, they drive the whole wheel: kamma, the six realms and the twelve links all turn around them.
  • Message: to stop the wheel, deal with the poisons at the centre.
Mission 7Diversity

Reading the Wheel

  • The Wheel of Life picture belongs mainly to Tibetan (Mahayana/Vajrayana) Buddhism. Theravada shares the teaching but seldom uses the picture.
  • Theravada emphasis: the realms are mainly places of rebirth, shaped by kamma; the poisons are the roots of unskilful action, cut by the Eightfold Path.
  • Mahayana, especially Tibetan, emphasis: the realms are places of rebirth and also states of mind we pass through in this life: rage feels like hell; endless wanting like a hungry ghost.
  • Many modern Western teachers read the Wheel mainly psychologically.
Mission 7Read the source

Thirst and fire

> "Whomsoever this fierce thirst overcomes, full of poison, in this world, his sufferings increase like the abounding Birana grass." (Dhammapada 335, Müller)

> "There is no fire like passion, there is no shark like hatred, there is no snare like folly, there is no torrent like greed." (Dhammapada 251, Müller)

> "For hatred does not cease by hatred at any time: hatred ceases by love, this is an old rule." (Dhammapada 5, Müller)

Mission 7

Different readings, and the antidotes

  • Each poison has a positive opposite:
  • greed → generosity (dana) and letting go;
  • hatred → loving-kindness (metta) and compassion (karuna);
  • ignorance → wisdom (panna, Sanskrit prajna).
  • Buddhists train in these: giving to monks and charities, metta bhavana meditation, study and insight meditation.
Mission 7Worked answer

Belief, source, development

Question: Explain two Buddhist teachings about the causes of suffering.

  • Teaching 1: suffering is caused by tanha (craving). Source: the Buddha's first sermon (SN 56.11) says craving leads to renewed existence. Development: because everything is impermanent, craving can never be satisfied, so Buddhists try to let go.
  • Teaching 2: the Three Poisons, greed, hatred and ignorance, are the roots of unskilful action. Source: "hatred ceases by love" (Dhammapada 5). Development: so Buddhists practise metta to replace hatred with loving-kindness.
Mission check

?Priya buys new trainers every month. She gets angry when friends post better ones online, and is sure the next pair will finally make her happy. Which Buddhist analysis fits best?

Suffering has a cause inside the mind: craving, rooted in greed, hatred and ignorance. If the cause can be removed, can suffering end? That is the Third Truth.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Siddhattha Gotama was a Sakya prince, son of Suddhodana and Maya, born at Lumbini; Kondanna foretold he would become a Buddha.
  • He grew up in luxury, married Yasodhara and had a son, Rahula; at 29 the Four Sights (old age, sickness, death, a holy man) led to the Great Renunciation.
  • Buddhavamsa XXVI: Kapilavatthu, Suddhodana and Maya, 29 years in three palaces, wife and son, the four signs.

Mission 2

  • After his teachers and six years of harsh asceticism, Siddhattha chose the Middle Way and meditated under the Bodhi tree at Bodh Gaya.
  • Buddhists believe he defeated Mara, understood the Four Noble Truths and became the Buddha; at Sarnath he taught the first sermon (SN 56.11), and the five companions became the first Sangha.

Mission 3

  • The Sangha (community) is the third Jewel; Buddhists go for refuge to Buddha, Dhamma and Sangha.
  • The Buddha founded the Sangha after his first sermon; monks and nuns live simply and depend on lay people's dana, giving teaching in return.

Mission 4

  • Dhamma means the Buddha's teaching and the truth about reality; dependent arising teaches that everything arises from conditions and ceases when they cease.
  • Samsara is kept turning by craving and ignorance; kamma shapes rebirth; ending the conditions ends suffering (nibbana).

Mission 5

  • The three marks: anicca (impermanence), dukkha (unsatisfactoriness), anatta (no fixed self).
  • A person is five changing khandhas; Nagasena's chariot shows that "self" is a name for parts working together.
  • Theravada analyses the khandhas; Mahayana extends no-self to emptiness (sunyata); the marks lead to letting go and compassion.

Mission 6

  • The First Noble Truth: there is dukkha (suffering, unsatisfactoriness), taught in the first sermon (SN 56.11).
  • Three types: dukkha-dukkha (pain), viparinama-dukkha (change), sankhara-dukkha (conditioned existence; Edexcel "fabrication").
  • Buddhists see the truth as realistic, not pessimistic; Theravada and Mahayana differ on emphasis, e.g. the Bodhisattva vow to free all beings.

Mission 7

  • The Second Noble Truth (samudaya): suffering is caused by tanha (craving) for sense pleasures, for existence and for non-existence.
  • The Three Poisons: greed (lobha), hatred (dosa) and ignorance (moha); ignorance is the deepest root.
  • In the Wheel of Life the pig, rooster and snake at the hub drive samsara; Theravada and Mahayana read the realms with different emphases.
