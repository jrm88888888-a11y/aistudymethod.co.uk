Shakespeare: Close Analysis and Whole-Play Argument

Component 01 opens with Shakespeare, examined closed-text in two ways: a passage-based question demanding minute close analysis, and a discursive essay demanding a whole-play argument. This lesson teaches both skills so they work on any Shakespeare play your centre sets.

  • Two tasks: detailed analysis of a printed extract, and a sustained argument across the whole play.
  • AO2 close reading and AO5 interpretation carry serious weight here, alongside AO1 and AO3.
  • Genre is your framework: tragedy, comedy or history each supply their own vocabulary.

Any line referenced is treated as an illustrative example, never invented; set texts rotate, so the skills are the point. Press Start.

Task 1 · the passage question

Close analysis of an extract

The passage question rewards density: you must mine a short extract for how its language and form make meaning.

  • Place the extract in one sentence: where in the play’s arc does it fall, and what has just become possible or impossible?
  • Track shifts within the passage — a change of register, a switch from verse to prose, a turn of address.
  • Read the imagery cluster present in these particular lines, not imported from elsewhere.
  • Notice the metre and its disruptions — a broken pentameter can enact a broken mind.

Move across the whole extract, including its end. Weak answers exhaust the clock on the first few lines.

Task 2 · the whole-play essay

Building a sustained argument

The discursive essay ranges across the entire play and must argue a line, not survey the plot.

  • Answer the question’s concept — power, guilt, deception — with a thesis from the first sentence.
  • Trace a motif or idea across the play, choosing moments that develop your argument.
  • Weigh the ending against the opening — what has changed, and does the play endorse or question it?
  • Structure as a claim, not as a walk through the acts.

Selectivity is a skill: in a closed-text exam you cannot cover everything, so choose the three or four moments that best prove your case.

Genre · the tragic and comic frameworks

Vocabulary to read the play

Match your vocabulary to the play’s genre:

  • Tragedy — hamartia, hubris, peripeteia, anagnorisis, catharsis, nemesis (from Aristotle’s Poetics), used as analytical tools.
  • Comedy — the blocking figure, disguise and mistaken identity, the green world and the reconciling marriage (Northrop Frye), festive carnival (Bakhtin).
  • History — kingship, legitimacy, the providential or the Machiavellian view of power.

Name to analyse, not to decorate: the term must lead to a point about method or meaning.

AO5 · interpretation

Criticism and performance

OCR rewards awareness that a play sustains multiple readings (AO5), including in performance:

  • A.C. Bradley (Shakespearean Tragedy, 1904) — character-centred reading built on the hero and the flaw.
  • Jan Kott (Shakespeare Our Contemporary, 1964) — a political, absurdist reading for the modern age.
  • Performance criticism — meaning made in staging: a cut line, a doubled role, a director’s reading of an ending.

Discipline: a critical idea must produce a textual claim. Not “Kott is political”, but “the flat closing couplet lends weight to Kott’s sense that the state, not the soul, is the play’s real subject.”

AO3 · context

Production and reception

AO3 has two halves for Shakespeare:

  • Production — the ordered Elizabethan and Jacobean cosmos, kingship and succession, gender and marriage, damnation, the open-air playhouse and boy actors.
  • Reception — how the play’s meaning shifts for later audiences with different beliefs.

Weld it: if deleting the context leaves the reading unchanged, it was decoration. Context is a lens, not an appendix.

Exam craft · the closed text

Working from memory, accurately

Because the exam is closed-text, quotation must be accurate or replaced by precise reference.

  • Learn a small bank of exact, useful quotations rather than many half-remembered ones.
  • Where memory fails, describe the moment precisely instead of risking a misquotation.
  • Time discipline: the passage and the essay are worth comparable marks — do not lavish everything on one.

Honesty rule: a described effect with no quotation beats an invented line. Examiners mark what the text does.

The big ideas to take into the exam

Two tasks: close analysis of a printed passage; a sustained whole-play argument

Passage: place it, track shifts, read the imagery cluster, hear the metre break

Essay: thesis first, trace an idea across the play, weigh ending against opening, be selective

Genre: tragic and comic vocabulary (Aristotle, Frye) used as tools

AO5: Bradley vs Kott and performance criticism, each producing a textual claim

Closed text: accurate quotation or precise reference; never fabricate a line
