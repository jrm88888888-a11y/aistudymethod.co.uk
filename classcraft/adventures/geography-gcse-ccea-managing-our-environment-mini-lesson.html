Mission 1

Mission 1: In 1991 one volcano in the Philippines cooled the whole planet. How?

Make a guess: In 1991 one volcano in the Philippines cooled the whole planet. How?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA GCSE Geography

  • 2D.1 describe the greenhouse effect, define carbon footprint, and how both contribute to climate change
Mission 1

Volcanic eruptions: Pinatubo, 1991

  • In June 1991 Mount Pinatubo "injected about 15 million tons of sulfur dioxide into the stratosphere".
  • The gas formed tiny droplets (aerosols) that "scatter and absorb incoming sunlight".
  • High winds spread the haze around the world, so less sunlight reached the surface.
Mission 1

How big, and how long?

  • Global average temperature fell by about 0.6 °C over the next 15 months.
  • There was "a measurable cooling of the Earth's surface for a period of almost two years".
  • The droplets slowly fall out of the stratosphere, so the cooling fades.
  • Ash falls out within days; it is the sulfur haze that cools the whole planet.
Quick check
?Why did Pinatubo's cooling last only about two years?
Mission 1

The natural greenhouse effect

  • Sunlight (short-wave radiation) passes through the atmosphere and warms the ground.
  • The ground gives off heat (long-wave, infrared radiation).
  • Greenhouse gases absorb this heat and send some back down, warming the lower atmosphere.
  • "Water vapour is the most important greenhouse gas, and carbon dioxide (CO2) is the second-most important one".
  • Without it, the surface would be about −19 °C; the global mean is about 14 °C: a difference of 33 °C. It makes Earth habitable.
Mission 1

The enhanced greenhouse effect

  • Human activity adds greenhouse gases: burning fossil fuels, farming (cattle, rice, fertiliser) and deforestation.
  • 2024: CO₂, methane and nitrous oxide were 52%, 166% and 25% above pre-industrial levels.
  • CO₂ reached 423.9 ppm in 2024, with the largest yearly rise since measurements began in 1957.
  • More gas traps more heat: the enhanced greenhouse effect, which drives today's climate change.
Mission 1

Carbon footprint

  • A carbon footprint is the total greenhouse gas emitted to provide the goods and services a person, organisation or country uses.
  • The UK's footprint "includes emissions associated with the consumption of goods and services by UK or England residents", including "emissions that occur overseas in supply chains".
  • UK footprint, 2023: about 699 million tonnes CO₂ equivalent.
  • Bigger footprints add more gas, so they enhance the greenhouse effect.
Quick check
?Which statement describes the natural greenhouse effect?
Mission check

?Imagine a huge eruption injects sulfur gas high into the stratosphere. What would happen to global temperature?

Pinatubo "injected about 15 million tons of sulfur dioxide into the stratosphere". The haze scattered sunlight and cooled the planet by about 0.6 °C for almost two years.

Mission 2

Mission 2: The UK's emissions fell by 54% between 1990 and 2025. Has the UK really halved its part in climate change?

Make a guess: The UK's emissions fell by 54% between 1990 and 2025. Has the UK really halved its part in climate change?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA GCSE Geography

  • 2D.1 describe the greenhouse effect, define carbon footprint, and how both contribute to climate change
Mission 2

The natural greenhouse effect

  • Sunlight (short-wave) passes through the atmosphere and warms the ground.
  • The warm ground gives off heat (long-wave radiation).
  • Greenhouse gases (water vapour, carbon dioxide, methane, nitrous oxide) absorb some of it and give out heat in all directions, including back down.
  • Without them, Earth's surface would be about −19 °C; it averages about 14 °C.
Mission 2

The enhanced greenhouse effect

  • People add greenhouse gases faster than nature removes them.
  • More gas traps more long-wave heat, so the planet warms: the enhanced greenhouse effect.
  • 2024 levels: carbon dioxide 423.9 ppm, methane 1,942 ppb, nitrous oxide 338.0 ppb.
  • That is 52%, 166% and 25% above pre-industrial levels (before 1750).
Quick check
?What makes the greenhouse effect enhanced?
Mission 2

Cause 1: burning fossil fuels

  • Coal, oil and gas release carbon dioxide when burned.
  • In 2025 burning fossil fuels released about 38.1 billion tonnes of CO₂ (projection).
Mission 2

Cause 2: farming

  • Cattle and sheep release methane as they digest grass. Flooded rice fields and manure release methane too.
  • Fertilisers release nitrous oxide.
  • Farming and waste together released about 228 million tonnes of methane a year (2010–2019), against 115 million tonnes from fossil fuels.
  • Methane is the second most important human-influenced greenhouse gas, after CO₂.
Mission 2

Cause 3: deforestation

  • Trees store carbon as they grow. Cutting and burning them releases it as CO₂.
  • A cleared forest also stops absorbing CO₂: a lost carbon sink.
  • Land-use change, such as deforestation, released about 4.1 billion tonnes of CO₂ in 2025 (projection).
Quick check
?In 2025, fossil fuels released about 38.1 billion tonnes of CO₂ and land-use change (such as deforestation) about 4.1 billion tonnes. What percentage of this total came from fossil fuels, to the nearest whole number?
Mission 2

What is a carbon footprint?

  • A carbon footprint is the total greenhouse gas emitted to supply what a person, organisation or country uses, measured in CO₂-equivalent.
  • A country's footprint counts emissions from making its imports, wherever they happen.
  • Its territorial emissions count only what is released inside its borders.
Mission 2

The UK's footprint

  • The UK's carbon footprint was about 699 million tonnes CO₂-equivalent in 2023.
  • Emissions from making its imports rose from 31% of the footprint in 1996 to 53% in 2023.
  • Imports from China make up 25% of those import emissions.
Mission 2

Two measures, two stories

  • Since 1996, the UK's territorial emissions fell by 50%; its carbon footprint by only 15%.
  • Defra links this to "a shift from manufacturing towards a service-based economy, alongside an increased reliance on imported goods and services".
  • Some cuts were real: the UK's last coal power station closed in September 2024.
Quick check
?Why has the UK's carbon footprint fallen much less than its territorial emissions?
Mission 2

Your own carbon footprint

  • A person's footprint comes from travel, home heating and electricity, food and the things they buy.
  • Each tonne emitted adds to greenhouse gas levels, and so to the enhanced greenhouse effect.
  • Emissions per person (inside each country, 2025): United States 17.53 t, United Kingdom 5.45 t, India 3.11 t CO₂-equivalent.
  • Exam move: define carbon footprint, then link it in a chain: consumption → emissions → more greenhouse gas → more heat trapped → warming.
Mission check

?A country's territorial emissions fell by 40% over 20 years. In the same years it closed its steelworks and began importing most of its steel and electronics. Has its part in climate change fallen by 40%?

The UK's territorial emissions halved, but its carbon footprint fell by only 15% (1996–2023), because 53% of the footprint now comes from imports. Part of the cut is real; part has moved abroad.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Global sea level has risen about 10 cm since 1993, and the yearly rise has more than doubled. Where is the extra water coming from?

Make a guess: Global sea level has risen about 10 cm since 1993, and the yearly rise has more than doubled. Where is the extra water coming from?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA GCSE Geography

  • 2D.1 evaluate effects of climate change on the environment, people and the economy
Mission 3

The sea is rising faster

  • Global sea level rose 0.20 m between 1901 and 2018.
  • The yearly rise sped up: 1.3 mm a year (1901–1971), 1.9 mm (1971–2006), 3.7 mm (2006–2018).
  • Satellites: about 10 cm since 1993.
Mission 3

Where the extra water comes from

  • Thermal expansion: seawater takes up more space as it warms.
  • Melting land ice: water from glaciers and ice sheets runs into the sea.
  • Shares of the rise, 1971–2018: thermal expansion 50%, glaciers 22%, ice sheets 20%, other land water 8%.
  • Melting sea ice adds almost nothing: it is already floating, so it already displaces its own weight of water.
Quick check
?Which of these adds least to sea-level rise?
Mission 3

Retreating glaciers

  • Reference glaciers worldwide lost on average 214 mm of water-equivalent thickness per decade in the 1980s, and 896 mm per decade in the 2010s.
  • Glacier National Park, USA: about 80 glaciers larger than 0.1 km² around 1850; 32 in 2005.
  • People: in the upper Indus basin, glacier melt made up 40.6% of river runoff (1998–2007). Shrinking glaciers threaten that water supply in the long term.
Mission 3

Effects on the environment

  • About half of the species studied worldwide have moved towards the poles or uphill.
  • From early 2023 to mid-2025, heat stress able to bleach coral hit 84% of the world's reef area.
  • Climate zones are shifting: dry (arid) zones are expanding and polar zones shrinking.
Mission 3

Effects on people

  • Health: heat-related deaths average 546,000 a year, a rate 23% higher than in the 1990s.
  • Diseases carried by mosquitoes and ticks have spread to new areas.
  • Displacement: storms forced 17.9 million movements in 2025. Not every disaster is caused by climate change; scientists test each event.
  • About 3.3 to 3.6 billion people live in places highly vulnerable to climate change.
Quick check
?Which is an effect of climate change on people, rather than on the environment?
Mission 3

Food: changing crop yields

  • Farm output keeps growing, but climate change "has slowed this growth over the past 50 years globally".
  • Losers: yields of some crops, such as maize and wheat, have fallen in many lower-latitude (tropical) regions.
  • Winners: some crops (maize, wheat, sugar beet) have gained in many higher-latitude regions.
  • In Africa, livestock on pasture grow more slowly.
Mission 3

Effects on the economy, jobs and settlements

  • Jobs: heat cost 640 billion potential working hours in 2024, especially in outdoor work such as farming and building.
  • Food prices (projection): climate change could raise cereal prices by a median 7.6% by 2050 (range 1–23%).
  • Settlements: by 2050, many low-lying megacities and small islands are projected to face, at least once a year, extreme sea levels that used to come once a century.
Mission 3

Predicted sea level

  • By 2100, compared with 1995–2014: a likely rise of 0.28–0.55 m if emissions are very low, and 0.63–1.01 m if they are very high.
  • These are projections: the range depends on how much people emit.
Quick check
?Which statement about climate change and crop yields is best supported?
Mission 3

Weighing the effects

  • Who is hit hardest: in 2010–2020, deaths from floods, droughts and storms were 15 times as high in highly vulnerable regions as in the least vulnerable.
  • Time: some effects are already here (heat deaths); others grow over decades (sea level).
  • Gains: some higher-latitude farms gain, but losses fall mainly on poorer, hotter regions.
  • Exam move: to evaluate, sort effects into environment, people and economy, then judge which is most serious and for whom.
Mission check

?Imagine two summers. In summer 1, 100 billion tonnes of floating Arctic sea ice melts. In summer 2, 100 billion tonnes of ice melts from Greenland's ice sheet on land, and the upper ocean warms. Which summer raises sea level more?

The extra water comes from melting land ice (glaciers and ice sheets) and from thermal expansion of warming seawater, which explained half the rise in 1971–2018. Rising seas are one of many effects on people, places and the economy.

Mission 4

Mission 4: In 2016, China reported importing about 8.8 million tonnes of plastic waste. In 2018, it imported about 51,000 tonnes. What happened, and where did the waste go?

Make a guess: In 2016, China reported importing about 8.8 million tonnes of plastic waste. In 2018, it imported about 51,000 tonnes. What happened, and where did the waste go?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA GCSE Geography

  • 2D.2 the waste hierarchy and "reduce, reuse, recycle"
Mission 4

Where waste goes

  • Disposal options: landfill, incineration (burning), recycling, export, or dumping.
  • Only about 9% of all plastic waste had been recycled (estimate cited in 2018).
  • About 80% had been landfilled or had ended up in the environment.
Mission 4

The waste hierarchy

  • Five steps, "ranked according to environmental impact". Best first: 1. Prevention: use less material; keep products longer. 2. Preparing for re-use: check, clean, repair, refurbish. 3. Recycling: turn waste into a new product, including composting. 4. Other recovery: e.g. incineration with energy recovery. 5. Disposal: landfill, and incineration without energy recovery.
Mission 4

Reduce, reuse, recycle

  • Reduce: buy less, choose less packaging, refuse single-use items. No waste is made at all.
  • Reuse: refill bottles, repair phones, pass on clothes. The item keeps its value.
  • Recycle: sort waste so it can be made into something new. This still uses energy and transport.
  • The order matters: reduce first, recycle last of the three.
Quick check
?A council sends black-bag rubbish to a plant that burns it and uses the heat to generate electricity. Where is this on the waste hierarchy?
Mission 4

Exporting waste, and China's ban

  • "Upward of half of the plastic waste intended for recycling has been exported".
  • China had taken a cumulative 45% of the world's plastic waste imports since 1992.
  • In 2017 China announced a policy "permanently banning the import of nonindustrial plastic waste".
  • Imports reported (thousand tonnes), 2016 → 2018: China 8,801.6 → 51.4 (UN Comtrade, all plastic scrap, weight estimated; a study using the same UN database gives 7.35 million tonnes for 2016); Malaysia 287.7 → 872.5; Thailand 69.5 → 552.7; Türkiye 152.9 → 424.6.
Quick check
?Malaysia imported 287.7 thousand tonnes of plastic waste in 2016 and 872.5 thousand tonnes in 2018. About how many times as much did it import in 2018?
Mission 4

Moving up the hierarchy

  • Circular economy: "a system where materials never become waste and nature is regenerated".
  • International rules: Basel Convention controls on plastic waste took effect on 1 January 2021; from 1 January 2025 all e-waste needs "prior informed consent".
  • The EU bans plastic waste exports to non-OECD countries from 21 November 2026.
  • The aim: more reduce and reuse, less disposal.
Mission check

?A country stops accepting imports of used electronics. What is the most likely result for the countries that used to send them?

China banned most plastic waste imports (announced 2017), so its imports fell to 51.4 thousand tonnes while Malaysia's tripled. Waste moves unless it is reduced or reused: the top of the waste hierarchy.

Mission 5

Mission 5: If wind is free and never runs out, why don't we get all our electricity from it?

Make a guess: If wind is free and never runs out, why don't we get all our electricity from it?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA GCSE Geography

  • 2D.2 evaluate benefits and disadvantages of one renewable energy source as a sustainable solution (wind farms)
Mission 5

Two kinds of energy

  • Renewable: will not run out on a human timescale, because nature keeps renewing it.
  • Non-renewable: a fixed stock in the ground that is not replaced on a human timescale, so it runs out as we use it.
  • Renewable: solar, wind, hydro (HEP), tidal, wave, geothermal, biomass.
  • Non-renewable: fossil fuels (coal, oil, natural gas) and nuclear (uranium).
Mission 5

What powers the world?

  • In 2025, fossil fuels still supplied 86% of the world's total energy.
  • Solar grew by 30% that year, and renewables were the largest source of growth.
  • So renewables are growing fast, but from a small base.
Quick check
?Which energy source is non-renewable?
Mission 5

One renewable: wind

  • Growth: wind made 0.2% of UK electricity in 2000 and 29% in 2025.
  • It helped cut coal from about 40% of UK electricity in 2012 to 0% in 2024.
  • Benefits: no fuel cost, no CO₂ while generating, jobs in building and maintaining turbines.
  • Disadvantages: output varies with the wind; turbines change views and can kill birds.
  • Attitudes: in 2026, 79% of UK adults supported renewable energy, but only 36% would be happy with an onshore wind farm locally (14% unhappy).
Mission 5

Wind farms: a sustainable solution?

  • Environmental: cuts carbon; but turbines affect birds and landscapes.
  • Economic: no fuel to buy; but backup is needed on calm days, and that costs money.
  • Social: local jobs; but some residents object to noise and views.
  • Judgement: wind is sustainable as part of a mix, with backup or storage for calm spells.
  • Exam move: "Evaluate" needs benefits and disadvantages, then a judgement backed by evidence.
Quick check
?Most UK adults support renewables, yet only 36% would be happy with a local onshore wind farm. What does this show?
Mission 5

Why not all wind?

  • Wind is free, but it varies: on calm days turbines make little.
  • Demand does not stop, so the grid needs sources it can turn up on demand (gas, HEP, biomass), storage, or imports.
  • The UK government calls gas "flexible and reliable", easy to increase for short peaks.
  • So countries build an energy mix: wind is a big part, not the whole.
Mission check

?A small island has plenty of sunshine and plans to get all its electricity from solar panels. What is the biggest problem it must solve?

Free energy is not the same as reliable energy. Wind and solar vary, so a grid also needs storage, imports or sources it can turn up on demand: that is why countries use a mix.

Mission 6

Mission 6: The least developed countries emitted less than 0.4% of all historical fossil CO₂, yet face some of the worst impacts. Who should cut emissions, and who should pay?

Make a guess: The least developed countries emitted less than 0.4% of all historical fossil CO₂, yet face some of the worst impacts. Who should cut emissions, and who should pay?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA GCSE Geography

  • 2D.2 describe and evaluate the 2015 international climate change agreement (Paris)
Mission 6

Two kinds of response

  • Mitigation: reducing the causes of climate change, by cutting emissions or removing greenhouse gases (renewable energy, planting forests).
  • Adaptation: adjusting to the effects of climate change to reduce harm (sea walls, heat alerts, drought-tolerant crops).
  • Responses happen at international, national and local scales, and by individuals.
Mission 6

Before Paris: Kyoto

  • 1992: the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC) was adopted.
  • 1997: the Kyoto Protocol set binding cuts for 37 industrialised countries and economies in transition. It came into force in 2005.
  • Limits: the United States signed but never ratified it; Canada withdrew from 15 December 2012. Developing countries had no binding targets.
Mission 6

The Paris Agreement, 2015

  • Adopted on 12 December 2015; in force from 4 November 2016.
  • Aim: hold warming "well below 2°C above pre-industrial levels", and try to limit it to 1.5 °C.
  • Every country sets its own target, a nationally determined contribution (NDC), and updates it every five years.
  • 194 Parties. The United States' withdrawal took effect on 27 January 2026.
Mission 6

Who cuts, and who pays?

  • Principle: "common but differentiated responsibilities"; developed countries "should take the lead".
  • The least developed countries produced less than 0.4% of historical CO₂ from fossil fuels and industry (1850–2019).
  • Finance: rich countries' goal of US$100 billion a year was first met in 2022 (US$115.9 billion). In 2024 a new goal was set: US$300 billion a year by 2035.
  • A Fund for responding to Loss and Damage helps developing countries that are particularly vulnerable.
Quick check
?What is a nationally determined contribution (NDC)?
Mission 6

Is Paris working?

  • Strengths: almost every country has joined; every Party must set and update targets.
  • Limits: targets are self-chosen. UNEP (2025): if all NDCs are met, warming this century of 2.3–2.5 °C; current policies alone, up to 2.8 °C.
  • 2024 was likely the first calendar year above 1.5 °C (1.55 ± 0.13 °C). The Paris goal is judged over decades, not one year.
  • The remaining carbon budget for 1.5 °C is 170 billion tonnes of CO₂; 2025 emissions were about 38.1 billion tonnes from fossil fuels plus 4.1 billion from land-use change.
Quick check
?The remaining carbon budget for 1.5 °C is 170 billion tonnes of CO₂. In 2025, about 38.1 billion tonnes came from fossil fuels and 4.1 billion from land-use change. At 2025 levels, about how many years would the budget last?
Mission check

?Country X is a low-income country with tiny historical emissions and worsening coastal floods. Under the Paris Agreement, what is expected of it, and from others?

The agreements' answer is common but differentiated responsibilities: every Party cuts through its NDC, developed countries take the lead, and finance (US$300 billion a year by 2035; the Loss and Damage Fund) flows to the most vulnerable. Pledges still fall short: UNEP projects 2.3–2.8 °C.

Mission 7

Mission 7: Thailand closed one of its most famous beaches to visitors for about three and a half years. Why would a country shut a beach that tourists love?

Make a guess: Thailand closed one of its most famous beaches to visitors for about three and a half years. Why would a country shut a beach that tourists love?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA GCSE Geography

  • 2D.3 positive and negative cultural, economic and environmental impacts of mass tourism
Mission 7

Mass tourism and carrying capacity

  • Mass tourism: very large numbers of tourists visiting the same places, often on package holidays.
  • Carrying capacity: the most visitors a place can take before unacceptable damage occurs.
  • Damage can be environmental (coral, wildlife, litter), cultural (residents pushed out, traditions sold as shows) or felt as overcrowding.
Mission 7

Impacts of mass tourism

  • Economic +: jobs and income. The industry body WTTC estimates tourism supported 366 million jobs worldwide in 2025. Economic −: low-paid seasonal work; profits leak abroad; rising prices for residents.
  • Cultural +: money to restore heritage; interest in local crafts. Cultural −: in Venice, UNESCO reports homes replaced by tourist accommodation.
  • Environmental +: entry fees can fund protected areas. Environmental −: Maya Bay's coral was damaged by thousands of daily visitors.
  • The places here illustrate the impacts; you are not asked for a full case study.
Quick check
?In a historic city, flats are turned into holiday lets and long-term residents move away. What kind of impact is this?
Mission 7

Maya Bay, Thailand: a tropical coast

  • A bay on the uninhabited island of Phi Phi Leh, in a national park in Krabi province.
  • It became world-famous after the 1999 film The Beach. At its peak in 2017, almost 5,000 people a day visited.
  • It closed in June 2018, "when it was determined the area's coral and other marine life needed time to recover" from the damage caused by thousands of daily tourists.
  • Thailand is upper-middle income.
Mission 7

Managing Maya Bay

  • Closure: announced for four months from 1 June 2018, then extended; a plan to reopen on 1 January 2022 came after about three and a half years.
  • A new jetty on the far side of the island: boats must land there, not in the bay. Experts advised this was the only way for the coral to keep recovering.
  • Controlled daily numbers, a marked swimming area, and coral and beach trees replanted.
  • Dispersal: Thailand's tourism authority promoted Krabi's other islands while the bay was shut.
Mission 7

Venice, Italy: an urban lagoon

  • UNESCO: "The exceptionally high tourism pressure on the city of Venice has resulted in a partial functional transformation".
  • About 53,000 residents lived in the historic centre in 2018; on average 66,800 visitors and tourists were there each day (year not stated).
  • That is about 1.26 visitors per resident every day. Day-trippers alone: almost 22 million a year.
  • Italy is high income.
Mission 7

Managing Venice

  • Cruise ships: UNESCO's World Heritage Committee urged Italy in 2014 to ban large ships from the lagoon. In 2021 the Italian government banned large cruise ships from the Venice Lagoon.
  • Access fee: on set dates in 2026, day visitors to the historic city had to pay a fee unless exempt. The 2026 trial ended; from 27 July the fee no longer applied.
  • Both aim to cut pressure on the lagoon and the city without stopping tourism.
Quick check
?Which measure most directly protected Maya Bay's coral after it reopened?
Mission check

?A national park caps a famous mountain trail at a fixed number of walkers a day, even though many more want to come and would pay. Why would it turn paying visitors away?

Maya Bay passed its carrying capacity: almost 5,000 visitors a day in 2017 damaged its coral. Closing it, then reopening with a jetty and controlled daily numbers, let it recover. Venice faces the same pressure in a city, managed by a cruise-ship ban and an access fee.

Mission 8

Mission 8: In 2017 Rwanda doubled the price of a permit to see mountain gorillas, to US$1,500. Can making tourism more expensive make it more sustainable?

Make a guess: In 2017 Rwanda doubled the price of a permit to see mountain gorillas, to US$1,500. Can making tourism more expensive make it more sustainable?

Mission 8

Rwanda: a low-income country

  • The World Bank classes Rwanda as a low-income country. Income per person (GNI per capita) was US$1,150 in 2025.
  • Its mountain gorillas live in Volcanoes National Park, part of the Virunga Massif, shared with DR Congo and Uganda.
  • In CCEA's terms Rwanda is an LEDC.
Mission 8

Sustainable tourism and ecotourism

  • Sustainable tourism takes full account of its economic, social and environmental impacts, now and in the future, for visitors, businesses, the environment and host communities.
  • Ecotourism: "responsible travel to natural areas that conserves the environment, sustains the well-being of the local people, and involves interpretation and education".
  • One of its principles: provide direct money for conservation.
  • Not every nature holiday is ecotourism.
Quick check
?Which trip best fits the definition of ecotourism?
Mission 8

High value, low volume

  • On 6 May 2017 the Rwanda Development Board (RDB) raised the gorilla permit from US$750 to US$1,500.
  • RDB called this "high-value, low-volume": fewer visitors, each paying more.
  • Visits are limited: at most 8 visitors per group, and one hour with the gorillas.
  • Visitors who also spent 3 days in Rwanda's other national parks got a 30% discount.
Mission 8

Sharing the money

  • Since 2005, a revenue-sharing scheme has given part of park income to communities living next to the parks.
  • In 2017 the communities' share rose from 5% to 10% of park tourism revenue.
  • Since 2005 it has funded nearly 1,300 projects worth more than 23 billion Rwandan francs: schools, health centres and water supplies.
Quick check
?Communities receive 10% of park tourism revenue. How much of one US$1,500 permit is that?
Mission 8

Protecting the environment

  • Mountain gorillas in the Virunga Massif rose from as few as 242 in 1981 to 480 in 2010 and 604 in 2016.
  • The world total was about 1,063 in the 2019 census results.
  • Tourism pays for protection, and gives local people jobs as rangers, wardens and porters, so they gain from the forest standing.
  • Careful: these counts come before the 2017 price rise. They show recovery under long-term protection, not the effect of the new price.
Mission 8

Who makes tourism sustainable?

  • Government: RDB sets prices, limits group sizes and shares revenue with communities.
  • Organisations: groups such as the African Wildlife Foundation support the park and its neighbours; The International Ecotourism Society sets out ecotourism principles.
  • Individuals (responsible tourists):
    • keep to group sizes, time limits and the rangers' rules;
    • do not trek when ill: human diseases can infect gorillas;
    • hire local guides and porters, and buy local food and crafts;
    • respect local customs; take litter home.

Mission check

?A national park in another low-income country plans to double its entry fee. Which plan is most likely to make its tourism more sustainable?

Rwanda chose high value, low volume: a US$1,500 permit, small groups and 10% of park revenue shared with neighbouring communities. Gorillas were already recovering under protection.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Greenhouse gases keep Earth habitable; adding more enhances the effect.
  • Carbon footprint: emissions from everything we consume, wherever they arise.

Mission 2

  • Fossil fuels, farming and deforestation add CO₂, methane and nitrous oxide faster than nature removes them: the enhanced greenhouse effect.
  • A carbon footprint counts emissions from everything consumed, including imports. The UK's fell far less than its territorial emissions.

Mission 3

  • Sea level rises because seawater expands as it warms and land ice melts.
  • Effects reach the environment (species, coral, glaciers) and people (heat, disease, displacement).
  • Effects are uneven: the most vulnerable places suffer most.

Mission 4

  • The waste hierarchy ranks prevention, re-use, recycling, other recovery and disposal, best first.
  • Reduce, reuse, recycle, in that order.

Mission 5

  • Renewable sources (solar, wind, HEP, tidal, wave, geothermal, biomass) are renewed by nature; non-renewable fossil fuels and uranium are fixed stocks.
  • Every source has pros and cons; wind and solar are low-carbon but variable, so they work best in a mix.

Mission 6

  • Mitigation cuts causes; adaptation reduces harm.
  • Paris (2015): every Party sets an NDC; aim well below 2 °C, ideally 1.5 °C. Strength: nearly universal. Weakness: self-chosen targets that fall short.

Mission 7

  • Mass tourism brings jobs and income but can damage environments and push out residents: positive and negative economic, cultural and environmental impacts.
  • Maya Bay (Thailand, upper-middle income; tropical coast and coral): closure, jetty, daily limits. Venice (Italy, high income; urban lagoon): cruise-ship ban, access fee.

Mission 8

  • Ecotourism protects nature, benefits local people and educates visitors.
