Mission 1

Mission 1: Your DNA never leaves the nucleus. So how do its instructions reach the place where proteins are made?

Make a guess: Your DNA never leaves the nucleus. So how do its instructions reach the place where proteins are made?

Mission 1

Transcription comes first

  • Transcription: making an RNA copy of a gene, using one DNA strand as a template.
  • It is the first stage of gene expression. Translation comes next.
  • In eukaryotes, the mRNA leaves the nucleus through a nuclear pore. The DNA stays behind.
  • Not all genes are expressed at once. Transcription is a key stage at which a gene is switched on or off.
Mission 1

Transcription, step by step

  • RNA polymerase binds at the start of a gene. The DNA unwinds; hydrogen bonds between bases break.
  • Only one strand, the template strand, is read.
  • Free RNA nucleotides pair with exposed template bases: A–U, T–A, C–G, G–C. Hydrogen bonds hold each pair.
  • RNA polymerase joins adjacent RNA nucleotides with phosphodiester bonds, building the sugar–phosphate backbone.
  • The RNA separates from the template. The DNA rewinds.
  • Say: "RNA polymerase joins adjacent RNA nucleotides" — not: "RNA polymerase pairs the bases". Base pairs form by hydrogen bonding.
Quick check
?A section of the DNA template strand of a gene has the base sequence TACGCAT. What is the base sequence of the RNA transcribed from this section?
Mission 1

Worked example: three sequences

  • Template strand: T A C G G A T C
  • mRNA (complementary to the template): A U G C C U A G
  • Coding strand (paired with the template): A T G C C T A G
  • So mRNA = the coding strand, with U in place of T.
  • mRNA is a single strand. Its sugar is ribose. It is a copy of one gene, so it is short.
  • Its bases are read in triplets: codons.
Quick check
?During transcription, what holds each RNA nucleotide in position against the DNA template strand while it is being added to the growing RNA?
Mission 1

The template is not used up

  • Transcription reads the template. It does not change its base sequence.
  • One gene can be transcribed again and again.
  • Cells that never divide again (most neurons) keep the same DNA for their whole life.
  • So their DNA sequences must be conserved for decades.
Quick check
?A neuron formed before birth does not divide again, yet it transcribes the same genes throughout a lifetime of many decades. Which statement explains how this is possible?
Mission 1

Prokaryotes and eukaryotes

  • Eukaryotes: transcription in the nucleus; translation on ribosomes in the cytoplasm.
  • The nuclear membrane keeps them apart, so mRNA can be modified before it meets a ribosome.
  • Prokaryotes have no nucleus. mRNA can meet ribosomes at once, even while it is still being made.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Much of a gene's first RNA copy is cut out before it leaves the nucleus. What is kept, and why does it matter?

Make a guess: Much of a gene's first RNA copy is cut out before it leaves the nucleus. What is kept, and why does it matter?

Mission 2

Which way does transcription run?

  • RNA polymerase adds each nucleotide to the 3′ end of the growing RNA.
  • So RNA is made 5′ → 3′.
  • The template is antiparallel, so it is read 3′ → 5′.
  • The RNA has the same sequence and direction as the coding strand (U for T).
  • Translation is also 5′ → 3′: the ribosome moves from the mRNA's 5′ end towards its 3′ end.
  • Say: "made 5′ to 3′, template read 3′ to 5′" — not: "both run 5′ to 3′".
Quick check
?During transcription, RNA polymerase adds each new nucleotide to the free 3' end of the growing RNA strand. What does this mean for the direction of transcription?
Mission 2

Starting at the promoter

  • The promoter is a DNA sequence near the start of a gene. RNA polymerase binds there.
  • In eukaryotes, proteins called transcription factors bind to the promoter. Then RNA polymerase can bind and start.
  • Cell types differ in their transcription factors, so they switch on different genes.
  • The promoter is not transcribed into the mRNA.
  • Say: "promoters are DNA; transcription factors are proteins" — not: "a promoter is a protein".
Quick check
?A gene is transcribed in muscle cells but not in skin cells, although the DNA of the two cell types is identical. Which statement explains this in terms of initiation of transcription at the promoter?
Mission 2

From pre-mRNA to mature mRNA

  • In eukaryotes, the whole gene, introns included, is transcribed. The product is pre-mRNA.
  • Splicing: the introns are removed and the exons are joined.
  • A 5′ cap is added at one end and a 3′ poly-A tail at the other. Both stabilise the mRNA.
  • The mature mRNA leaves the nucleus.
  • Say: "introns are removed from the RNA" — not: "introns are cut out of the gene's DNA".
Quick check

Mission 2

One gene, several proteins

  • Exons from one pre-mRNA can be joined in different combinations: alternative splicing.
  • So one gene can code for several different polypeptides.
  • Different cell types can splice the same gene differently.
  • Example: exons 1 and 4 always kept; exons 2 and 3 each kept or not. That gives 2 × 2 = 4 different mRNAs.
  • Humans have about 20 000 protein-coding genes, yet make far more different polypeptides.
  • Most human genes with several exons are alternatively spliced.
Quick check

Mission 2

From gene to phenotype

  • Gene expression: transcription → translation → function of the protein.
  • The protein, often an enzyme, is what affects the phenotype.
  • Example: the tyrosinase gene → tyrosinase (an enzyme) → a step in making the pigment melanin.
  • Two non-functional alleles → no working enzyme → no melanin → albinism.
  • Say: "the gene codes for an enzyme that makes the pigment" — not: "the gene codes for the pigment".
Quick check
?A gene affects the colour of a flower's petals. Which sequence of stages is the mechanism by which the information in this gene has its effect on the phenotype?
Mission check

?A eukaryotic gene has four exons and three introns. In one family, a single-base change at the edge of intron 2 means intron 2 is no longer recognised for splicing. All other splicing is normal. Which prediction is correct?

Splicing decides which parts of the first RNA copy reach the ribosome. Keep one intron by mistake and the message changes.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why would a cell pump protons uphill across a membrane, only to let them flood straight back?

Make a guess: Why would a cell pump protons uphill across a membrane, only to let them flood straight back?

Mission 3

Handing on electrons

  • Reduced NAD and reduced FAD arrive from glycolysis, the link reaction and the Krebs cycle.
  • They deliver electrons (and protons) to a chain of carriers in the inner membrane, on the cristae.
  • Reduced NAD passes a pair of electrons to the first carrier. It becomes NAD again, free to collect more hydrogen.
  • Electrons pass from carrier to carrier in redox reactions: each carrier is reduced, then oxidised.
  • Each carrier is at a lower energy level than the last. Energy is released at each transfer.
Mission 3

Pumping protons

  • The energy released by electron flow pumps protons (H⁺) from the matrix into the intermembrane space.
  • The inner membrane is impermeable to H⁺, so they build up.
  • Result: a proton gradient across the inner membrane. It is electrochemical: a concentration difference and a charge difference.
  • More H⁺ = lower pH. The intermembrane space has a lower pH than the matrix.
  • Say: "protons are pumped across the membrane" — not: "electrons are pumped across".
Quick check
?How is the proton gradient across the inner mitochondrial membrane generated?
Mission 3

ATP synthase: chemiosmosis

  • H⁺ return to the matrix mainly through ATP synthase, a channel protein in the inner membrane.
  • H⁺ flow down their gradient. The flow turns part of the enzyme.
  • That energy joins ADP + Pᵢ → ATP, on the matrix side.
  • Making ATP using energy from a proton gradient = chemiosmosis.
  • The whole process, electron flow + chemiosmosis = oxidative phosphorylation. Most of the cell's ATP is made this way.
  • Say: "protons flow through ATP synthase" — not: "water moves by osmosis".
Quick check
?How does ATP synthase couple the proton gradient to the synthesis of ATP?
Mission 3

Oxygen: the last acceptor

  • Oxygen accepts electrons from the last carrier, and protons from the matrix.
  • Product: water (metabolic water).
  • This keeps the last carrier oxidised, so electrons keep flowing.
  • No oxygen → the last carrier stays reduced → every carrier backs up → flow and proton pumping stop.
  • Then reduced NAD cannot be re-oxidised, so the Krebs cycle and link reaction stop too.
  • Say: "oxygen becomes water" — not: "oxygen becomes carbon dioxide".
Quick check
?A student claims: 'Without oxygen the electron transport chain keeps running; only the formation of water stops.' Which statement correctly evaluates this claim?

?A chemical makes the inner mitochondrial membrane freely permeable to protons. Electron flow and oxygen consumption continue, but ATP synthesis stops. Which statement explains these observations?

Mission 4

Mission 4: Mitochondria, chloroplasts and bacteria all make ATP with the same machine. What does that tell us?

Make a guess: Mitochondria, chloroplasts and bacteria all make ATP with the same machine. What does that tell us?

Mission 4

Built for the job: the mitochondrion

  • Cristae: the folded inner membrane gives a large surface area for electron carriers and ATP synthase. More cristae → more ATP per second.
  • Very active cells (e.g. heart and flight muscle) have mitochondria packed with cristae.
  • Small intermembrane space: a few pumped protons raise the concentration fast → a steep gradient forms quickly.
  • Matrix: Krebs cycle enzymes and substrates are concentrated together → fast reactions; reduced NAD is made right beside the inner membrane.
  • Say: "cristae increase the area for ATP synthase and carriers" — not: "for absorbing oxygen or glucose".
Quick check
?Illustrative data.
Electron micrographs of mitochondria from two tissues were analysed. In tissue X the inner membrane had an area of about 40 µm² per µm³ of mitochondrial volume; in tissue Y the value was about 15 µm² per µm³. Which conclusion is best supported by these data?
Mission 4

The same machine in the chloroplast

  • In the thylakoid membrane, light excites electrons in chlorophyll (photosystems).
  • Excited electrons pass along a chain of carriers. The energy released pumps H⁺ from the stroma into the thylakoid space (lumen).
  • H⁺ flow back out through ATP synthase → ATP made in the stroma.
  • ATP made this way in light = photophosphorylation.
  • Electrons come from photosystem II (non-cyclic) or from photosystem I, returning to it (cyclic). Both pump protons.

Same machine, same weak point: in a chloroplast as in a mitochondrion, no gradient means no ATP.

Mission 5

Mission 5: A plant takes in carbon dioxide and water and gives out oxygen. Which of the two did the oxygen come from?

Make a guess: A plant takes in carbon dioxide and water and gives out oxygen. Which of the two did the oxygen come from?

Mission 5

Photosynthesis in one line

  • carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen
  • 6CO2 + 6H2O → C6H12O6 + 6O2 (light energy, absorbed by chlorophyll)
  • Light energy becomes chemical energy in the carbon compounds made.
  • Energy is transformed, not created.
  • This one transformation supplies most of the chemical energy used by life in ecosystems.
  • Say: "photosynthesis transforms light energy" — not: "plants make energy".
Mission 5

Water is split for its hydrogen

  • CO2 has no hydrogen. Glucose has 12 H atoms. They come from water.
  • Light energy is used to split water.
  • Its hydrogen (as protons and electrons) reduces CO2 to glucose.
  • The oxygen left over is a by-product: it is released.
  • CO2's carbon and oxygen end up in glucose and in some new water, not in the O2.
  • Plants, algae and cyanobacteria (prokaryotes) all split water and release O2.
  • Say: "the oxygen released comes from water" — not: "the plant keeps the carbon of CO2 and releases its oxygen".

Mission 5

Inside a chloroplast

  • Envelope: outer and inner membrane.
  • Thylakoids: flattened membrane sacs. A stack is a granum (plural grana).
  • Lamellae (intergranal thylakoids) link the grana.
  • Stroma: the fluid around the thylakoids, inside the inner membrane.
  • Thylakoid membranes hold chlorophyll and electron carriers.
  • The chlorophyll is organised into two photosystems.
  • Light-dependent stage: thylakoid membranes. Light-independent stage: stroma.
  • Stroma also holds circular DNA, 70S ribosomes, starch grains and lipid droplets.
Mission 5

Built for the job

  • Huge thylakoid membrane area: room for many photosystems, carriers and ATP synthase.
  • Small volume inside each thylakoid: protons pumped in build a steep gradient fast.
  • Stroma: Calvin cycle enzymes and substrates concentrated together, right next to the thylakoids that supply ATP and reduced NADP.
  • DNA and ribosomes: the chloroplast makes some of its own proteins.
  • Starch grains: sugar stored where it is made.
Quick check

Mission 6

Mission 6: A green leaf also hides orange and yellow pigments. How can you reveal them, and what are they for?

Make a guess: A green leaf also hides orange and yellow pigments. How can you reveal them, and what are they for?

Mission 6

One leaf, several pigments

  • Chloroplasts hold several photosynthetic pigments:
  • Chlorophyll a: blue-green.
  • Chlorophyll b: yellow-green.
  • Carotenoids: carotene (orange) and xanthophylls (yellow).
  • Chlorophyll a is the primary pigment. The others are accessory pigments.
  • Each pigment absorbs a different set of wavelengths, so together they use more of the spectrum.
Mission 6

Absorbing light

  • A pigment absorbs a photon only if its energy matches the gap to an excited state.
  • Then an electron is raised to a higher energy level. This energy is later turned into chemical energy.
  • Chlorophyll absorbs blue (about 430–450 nm) and red (about 640–680 nm) strongly.
  • It absorbs little green (about 500–600 nm): green is reflected or transmitted, so leaves look green.
  • Absorption spectrum: absorbance against wavelength (nm), for one pigment.
  • Label the x-axis with wavelengths and colours.
  • Say: "leaves look green because green light is reflected" — not: "chlorophyll absorbs green light".
Quick check
?The absorption spectrum of chlorophyll a has peaks at about 430 nm (blue) and 660 nm (red) but a trough at about 550 nm (green). Which statement best explains this pattern?
Mission 6

Action spectrum

  • Action spectrum: rate of photosynthesis against wavelength.
  • Rate = O2 produced, or CO2 taken in, per unit time.
  • Similar to the absorption spectra: peaks in blue and red, trough in green.
  • Different: the action spectrum is not zero in green. Accessory pigments absorb some green and pass the energy on.
  • Absorption spectrum: one pigment, light absorbed. Action spectrum: whole leaf, photosynthesis done.
  • Engelmann (1882) lit a filament of alga with a spectrum: oxygen-seeking bacteria crowded in the blue and red zones.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A leaf sealed in a chamber was illuminated with light of one wavelength at a time and the fall in carbon dioxide concentration in the chamber was recorded: at 450 nm the concentration fell by 80 ppm in 4 minutes; at 550 nm by 30 ppm in 5 minutes; at 650 nm by 90 ppm in 6 minutes; at 700 nm by 20 ppm in 2 minutes. Which statement about these results is correct?
Mission 6

Practical: separating the pigments

  • Paper or thin-layer chromatography; calculate Rf and identify pigments.
  • Grind leaf with sand and solvent (e.g. propanone). Spot the extract on a pencil origin line; dry; repeat to concentrate.
  • Stand in solvent below the origin, in a sealed container. Remove before the front reaches the top; mark the front at once.
  • Pigments separate by solubility in the solvent versus attraction to the stationary phase.
  • Rf = distance from origin to spot centre ÷ distance from origin to solvent front.
  • Identify by Rf and colour against reference values for the same solvent and stationary phase.
Mission 6

Worked example: Rf

Illustrative data. Distances from the origin line: solvent front 82 mm.

1. Orange spot: 77 mm. Rf = 77 ÷ 82 = 0.94.

2. Blue-green spot: 49 mm. Rf = 49 ÷ 82 = 0.60.

3. Yellow-green spot: 33 mm. Rf = 33 ÷ 82 = 0.40.

  • Rf has no units and is always less than 1.
  • Measure from the origin, not the paper edge; to the centre of the spot.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • RNA polymerase reads one DNA strand (the template) and joins complementary RNA nucleotides (A–U, C–G) to make mRNA.
  • mRNA has the coding strand's sequence with U for T; the DNA template is unchanged.
  • The nucleus separates transcription from translation, so mRNA can be modified first.

Mission 2

  • RNA is made 5′ → 3′ from a template read 3′ → 5′, starting where transcription factors and RNA polymerase bind the promoter.
  • Eukaryotic pre-mRNA is spliced: introns removed, exons joined.
  • A 5′ cap and 3′ poly-A tail stabilise it.
  • Alternative splicing lets one gene code for several polypeptides.

Mission 3

  • Electrons from reduced NAD and FAD flow along carriers in the inner membrane; the energy released pumps H⁺ into the intermembrane space; H⁺ flows back through ATP synthase, making ATP (chemiosmosis); oxygen accepts the electrons and forms water.

Mission 4

  • Chemiosmosis works the same way in mitochondria and chloroplasts: carriers pump H⁺ across a membrane and ATP synthase uses the flow back to make ATP; only the energy source, the compartments and the last acceptor differ.
  • Mitochondria are adapted for ATP production: large cristae area, small intermembrane space, Krebs enzymes concentrated in the matrix.

Mission 5

  • Photosynthesis transforms light energy into chemical energy: water is split, its hydrogen reduces CO2 to sugar, and its oxygen is released.
  • Light-dependent stage (light reactions): thylakoid membranes. Light-independent stage (Calvin cycle): stroma.

Mission 6

  • Chromatography separates leaf pigments; identify each by Rf (origin to spot ÷ origin to front) and colour, against references for the same solvent.
  • Pigments absorb only wavelengths whose energy matches an electron jump: mainly blue and red. Accessory pigments widen the range used.
  • Absorption spectrum: light absorbed by a pigment. Action spectrum: rate of photosynthesis at each wavelength.
