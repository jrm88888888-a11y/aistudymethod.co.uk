Mission 1

Mission 1: Your body has never met this year's flu virus, yet it makes antibodies that fit it. How?

Make a guess: Your body has never met this year's flu virus, yet it makes antibodies that fit it. How?

Mission 1

Self, non-self and antigens

  • Every cell carries surface molecules, mostly proteins and glycoproteins, that identify it.
  • The immune system ignores self molecules and responds to non-self ones.
  • An antigen is a molecule that triggers an immune response, including antibody production.
  • Say: "the antigen is recognised; the antibody is made against it" — not the other way round.
Quick check
?Which statement about antigens is correct?
Mission 1

One lymphocyte, one shape

  • Lymphocytes drive the adaptive (specific) response. They circulate in the blood and gather in lymph nodes.
  • Two kinds: B lymphocytes (B cells) and T lymphocytes (T cells).
  • Each lymphocyte has receptors of one shape. Each B cell can make one type of antibody.
  • You have millions of different lymphocytes, made before any infection.
  • An antigen binds only the few with a complementary receptor. It selects them: clonal selection.
  • Say: "the receptor is complementary to the antigen" — not: "the same shape as the antigen".
  • Say: "the antigen selects a cell that already fits" — not: "the cell designs an antibody to fit".
Quick check
?Which statement about B-lymphocytes and antibodies is correct?
Mission 1

Antigen presentation wakes T helper cells

  • A macrophage engulfs a pathogen and displays its antigens on its surface: an antigen-presenting cell (APC).
  • A T helper cell with a complementary receptor binds the presented antigen and is activated.
  • It divides by mitosis into a clone and releases chemical signals (cytokines).
Mission 1

T killer cells: the cell-mediated response

  • This is cell-mediated immunity.
  • Stimulated by T helper cells, T killer cells (cytotoxic T cells) divide into a clone.
  • They bind body cells that display the antigen: virus-infected cells, cancer cells, transplanted cells.
  • By direct contact, they release proteins that make pores in the target cell's membrane. The cell dies.
  • T memory cells remain after the infection.
Mission 1

B cells: the antibody response

  • A B cell's receptor binds its complementary antigen. The B cell takes it in and displays it.
  • A T helper cell activated by the same antigen binds the B cell. Only now is the B cell activated.
  • The activated B cell divides by mitosis into a clone: clonal expansion.
  • Most become plasma cells: they secrete antibody. Every cell in the clone makes the same antibody.
  • SNAB: B effector cells become plasma cells.
  • Some become memory cells: long-lived and ready for next time.
Quick check
?In an experiment, B-lymphocytes specific to an antigen were cultured with that antigen and no other cells. No antibody was produced. When helper T-lymphocytes were added to the culture, antibody production began. What does this show?
Mission 1

Why the first response takes days

  • Only a few B cells fit any one antigen.
  • They must be activated, then divide many times, before there are enough plasma cells.
  • This takes days. Meanwhile the pathogen multiplies, and you feel ill.
  • Memory cells survive for years. Next time they divide at once: the pathogen is usually cleared before symptoms.
Quick check
?After a person is exposed to a new pathogen for the first time, several days pass before antibodies against it can be detected in the blood. Which statement best explains this delay?
Mission check

?A child is born with no T lymphocytes. Her B lymphocytes are normal in number and type. Why does she make very little antibody against most pathogens?

The right cells already exist before infection. The antigen selects them, T helper cells switch them on, and mitosis builds the army.

Mission 2

What an antibody is

  • An antibody is a protein made by plasma cells.
  • It binds specifically to one antigen, because its binding site is complementary in shape.
  • Binding forms an antigen–antibody complex, which leads to the antigen's destruction.
Mission 2

How antibodies disable pathogens

  • Agglutination: each antibody has two binding sites, so it links pathogens together. Bacteria clump.
  • Clumped bacteria cannot spread, and phagocytes engulf many at once: phagocytosis of the complex.
  • Say: "antibodies label and clump pathogens" — not: "antibodies kill or digest bacteria".
Quick check
?Why does an antibody cause bacteria to clump together?
Mission 2

First time slow, second time fast

  • Primary response (first exposure): few specific B cells must be selected, activated and cloned. Days pass; antibody peaks low. You may become ill.
  • Memory cells form and survive for years.
  • Secondary response (same antigen again): memory cells divide at once into plasma cells. Antibody rises sooner, much higher, and stays high longer.
  • The pathogen is usually destroyed before symptoms: you are immune.
  • B memory cells and T memory cells both make the secondary response faster.
  • Say: "memory cells persist" — not: "antibodies stay in the blood for life".

Mission 2

Active and passive immunity

  • Active: your own lymphocytes make antibodies and memory cells. Takes days. Long-lasting.
  • Passive: you receive ready-made antibodies. Works at once. Short-term: the antibodies are broken down and no memory cells form.
  • Natural active: catching the infection. Artificial active: vaccination (see Mission 4).
  • Natural passive: antibodies across the placenta, or in colostrum (first breast milk).
  • Artificial passive: injected antibodies, e.g. anti-toxin against tetanus.
Quick check
?After a bite from a possibly rabid dog, a traveller is injected with rabies antibodies and also starts a course of rabies vaccine. Why are both given?
Mission check

?Illustrative data: a vaccine is given in two doses, one month apart. After dose 1, antibody rose slowly over two weeks to a low level. After dose 2, the same antibody rose within a few days to about ten times that level. What best explains the response to dose 2?

The second meeting is faster because memory cells are waiting, not antibodies. That is how a second infection, or a second vaccine dose, works.

Mission 3

Mission 3: A gene has already been copied into a message. Can the cell still stop its protein being made?

Make a guess: A gene has already been copied into a message. Can the cell still stop its protein being made?

Mission 3

Control after transcription

  • Transcription is not the last switch.
  • After it, the mRNA can be edited, blocked or broken down early.
  • Splicing can give one gene several proteins.
Mission 3

Splicing: introns out, exons joined

  • In eukaryotes, transcription makes primary mRNA (pre-mRNA): exons and introns.
  • Splicing removes the introns and joins the exons → mature mRNA.
  • Only mature mRNA leaves the nucleus to be translated.
  • Say: "introns are removed from the mRNA" — not: "introns are cut out of the gene".
Mission 3

Alternative splicing: one gene, several proteins

  • (1) Exons can be joined in different combinations.
  • (2) Different mature mRNAs → different polypeptides from one gene.
  • Example: one human gene gives calcitonin (a hormone) in thyroid cells and CGRP (a signalling peptide) in neurones. Different exons are kept in each.
  • So about 20 000 human genes code for many more proteins.
  • This is one way post-transcriptional changes to mRNA give more than one protein from one gene.
  • Say: "different exons are spliced together" — not: "the gene mutates to make each version".

Mission 4

Mission 4: Some vaccines protect for life, but the flu vaccine changes every year. Why the difference?

Make a guess: Some vaccines protect for life, but the flu vaccine changes every year. Why the difference?

Mission 4

How a vaccine works

  • A vaccine contains antigens from a pathogen: killed or weakened pathogens, purified antigen proteins, or inactivated toxins.
  • Some vaccines contain mRNA or DNA coding for an antigen. Body cells make the antigen for a short time.
  • The antigen triggers a primary response: plasma cells, antibodies and memory cells.
  • No disease develops. A later infection meets a fast secondary response.
  • Vaccination gives artificial active immunity.
  • Say: "a vaccine contains antigens" — not: "a vaccine contains antibodies".
Quick check
?An mRNA vaccine against COVID-19 contains RNA with the sequence coding for the spike protein of SARS-CoV-2, packaged in lipid nanoparticles. Which statement correctly explains how it stimulates immunity?
Mission 4

When antigens change

  • Memory cells bind only the antigen they were made against.
  • Antigen variability: mutations change a pathogen's surface antigens. Old memory cells no longer bind, so you can fall ill again.
  • Influenza antigens change often, so a new vaccine is made each year.
  • Evolutionary race: hosts evolve better defences; pathogens evolve ways to evade them.
  • Evasion includes changing antigens (influenza, HIV) and hiding inside host cells (TB). More in an earlier lesson.
Quick check
?A person had influenza last winter and recovered fully. This winter they catch influenza again. Which is the best explanation?
Mission check

?A measles vaccine gives long-lasting protection. Over 100 types of rhinovirus cause the common cold, each with different surface antigens. Why would a vaccine made from one rhinovirus type give little protection against colds?

Long-lasting protection needs an antigen that stays the same. When antigens change or come in many types, memory cells miss.

Mission 5

Mission 5: How can a drug that once cured an infection stop working against the very same germ?

Make a guess: How can a drug that once cured an infection stop working against the very same germ?

Mission 5

Hit what only bacteria have

  • An antibiotic: a chemical that kills bacteria or stops them growing.
  • It blocks a process found in bacteria but not in eukaryotic cells.
  • Penicillin: blocks the enzymes that cross-link peptidoglycan in the bacterial cell wall.
  • Tetracycline: binds bacterial 70S ribosomes; protein synthesis stops.
  • Human cells: no peptidoglycan wall; 80S ribosomes in the cytoplasm.
  • So the drug has a target in the bacterium and none in our cells: selective toxicity.
Mission 5

Why viruses escape antibiotics

  • A virus has no cell wall, no ribosomes, no metabolism of its own.
  • It replicates using the host cell's processes.
  • Antibiotics block none of these, at any dose.
  • Antibiotics for a cold or flu: no benefit, but still selection for resistant bacteria.
  • Say: "viruses have no bacterial processes for the drug to block" — not: "viruses are too small for the drug".
Quick check
?Why do antibiotics fail to control infections caused by viruses?
Mission 5

Kill or hold back?

  • Bactericidal: kills bacteria. E.g. penicillin: weakened wall, water enters by osmosis, the cell bursts (lysis).
  • Bacteriostatic: stops bacteria growing and dividing. E.g. tetracycline.
  • After a bacteriostatic drug, the immune system must clear the bacteria.
  • Viable count: bactericidal → falls; bacteriostatic → stays level.
  • Say: "bacteriostatic stops growth" — not: "bacteriostatic kills slowly".
Quick check
?Antibiotics P and Q were added to separate cultures of one bacterium, each starting with 1 × 10⁶ viable cells per cm³ (illustrative data). After 6 hours: culture P had 1 × 10⁶ viable cells per cm³; culture Q had 2 × 10³; a culture with no antibiotic had 6 × 10⁷. Which conclusion is supported?
Mission 5

How resistance spreads

  • A random mutation, or a gene gained from another bacterium on a plasmid, gives resistance before any drug is used.
  • The antibiotic kills susceptible bacteria. Resistant ones survive, reproduce, pass the allele on.
  • Resistance to each drug usually needs its own gene. Strains can collect several: multiresistant.
  • More antibiotic use, more selection: careful use slows resistance.
  • Say: "resistant bacteria were selected" — not: "the bacteria got used to the drug".
Quick check

After years of spraying with H, most plants in a field carry the allele. The farmer then stops using H. Which prediction about the resistance allele over the following generations is best supported?
Mission 5

Infections caught in hospital

  • Hospital-acquired infections (HAIs), e.g. MRSA, C. difficile.
  • Causes: heavy antibiotic use (strong selection); patients with weak immunity; wounds, catheters, surgery; staff and equipment moving between patients.
  • Prescribing codes: no antibiotics for viral or minor infections; identify the bacterium and the drugs it is sensitive to; narrow spectrum where possible; take exactly as prescribed; keep some antibiotics in reserve.
  • Infection-control codes: wash hands between patients; isolate infected patients; screen new patients (e.g. for MRSA); clean wards; sterilise equipment; bare below the elbows.
  • Alcohol gel does not kill C. difficile spores. Wash hands with soap and water.
Quick check
?Cases of C. difficile infection rise on a hospital ward. Staff already clean their hands with alcohol gel between patients. Which change to practice would best reduce the spread?
Mission 5Practical

Antibiotics on a bacterial lawn

  • Core practical 15: investigate the effect of different antibiotics on bacteria.
  • Spread a bacterial culture evenly over agar: a lawn.
  • Add paper discs soaked in different antibiotics. Control: a disc soaked in sterile water.
  • Tape the lid (not sealed all round). Incubate upside down at 25 °C for 24–48 h.
  • Measure the clear zone (zone of inhibition). Larger zone, more effective antibiotic, if concentration and disc size are the same (and the drugs diffuse through agar alike).
Mission 5

Aseptic technique

  • Aim: keep unwanted microbes out of the culture, and the culture away from people.
  • Disinfect the bench. Work beside a lit Bunsen burner: the updraft carries microbes away.
  • Flame the inoculating loop until red hot; let it cool.
  • Flame the neck of the culture bottle when opening and closing it.
  • Lift the Petri dish lid only slightly, at an angle.
  • Sterile pipettes, spreaders and forceps; dispose of cultures by autoclaving.

Mission check

?Tuberculosis is treated with four antibiotics at once for the first two months. Each drug acts on a different target in the bacterium. Why is a combination used rather than a single drug?

The drug never changed. The population of bacteria did, because the drug selected the few that could already survive it.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Antigens select the few lymphocytes with complementary receptors; activated B cells form clones of plasma cells that all make one antibody, plus memory cells.
  • T helper cells, activated by antigen-presenting cells, switch on B cells and T killer cells, which destroy infected and abnormal cells.

Mission 2

  • Antibodies are Y-shaped proteins with two complementary binding sites; they clump pathogens for phagocytes. Active immunity makes memory cells and lasts; passive immunity is borrowed antibody and fades.
  • Memory cells make the secondary response sooner, larger and longer: that is immunity.

Mission 3

  • Alternative splicing joins different exons, so one gene can give several polypeptides.

Mission 4

  • Vaccines (antigens, or nucleic acids coding for them) produce memory cells without disease.
  • Changing antigens defeat old memory cells: why the flu vaccine changes yearly.

Mission 5

  • Antibiotics block processes found only in bacteria, so they do not harm our cells and cannot touch viruses.
  • Resistance evolves by selection: random variants that survive the drug reproduce, so every use of an antibiotic must be justified.
