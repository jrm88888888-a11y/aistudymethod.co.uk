Mission 1

Mission 1: Why can a few minutes of weak ultraviolet damage skin cells' DNA when hours of bright red light can't?

Make a guess: Why can a few minutes of weak ultraviolet damage skin cells' DNA when hours of bright red light can't?

Mission 1

Light comes in packets: photons

  • Electromagnetic radiation is emitted and absorbed in discrete packets of energy: photons.
  • A photon is a quantum of electromagnetic energy.
  • Energy of one photon: E = hf = hc/λ.
  • h = Planck constant = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s.
  • Higher frequency (shorter wavelength) → more energy per photon.
  • A brighter beam of the same colour has more photons, not bigger ones.
Mission 1

The electronvolt

  • Photon energies in joules are tiny. Use the electronvolt.
  • 1 eV: the energy gained by an electron accelerated through a potential difference of 1 V.
  • 1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. J → eV: divide by 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹.
  • Example, blue light, λ = 450 nm:
    • E = hc/λ = (6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.00 × 10⁸)/(450 × 10⁻⁹) = 4.42 × 10⁻¹⁹ J
    • = 4.42 × 10⁻¹⁹/1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 2.76 eV.
Quick check
?Light with a wavelength of 500 nm in air passes from the air into water (n = 1.33). What is the energy of each photon while it travels through the water? Use h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s and c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹, and treat the speed of light in air as c.
Quick check
?Predict first. Which observation from photoelectric experiments cannot be explained by the classical wave theory of light?
Mission 1

The photoelectric effect

Shine electromagnetic radiation on a clean metal surface. Electrons may be emitted: photoelectrons.

  • Below a threshold frequency f0: no emission, however intense the light.
  • Above f0: emission immediately, even in very dim light.
  • Maximum kinetic energy of the electrons rises with frequency; it does not depend on intensity.
  • Number emitted per second is proportional to intensity (above f0).
  • f0 depends on the metal (e.g. zinc needs ultraviolet).
Mission 1

Einstein's explanation

  • One photon gives all its energy to one electron: a one-to-one interaction.
  • Work function φ: the minimum energy needed to remove an electron from the metal's surface.
  • If hf < φ: no electron can escape. Threshold: hf0 = φ.
  • If hf is bigger than the work function, the extra energy becomes kinetic energy. Electrons from just below the surface lose some on the way out, so this is a maximum.
  • hf = φ + KEmax
Mission 1

Why waves can't explain it

Wave theory: energy arrives spread over the wavefront and builds up with intensity and time. It predicts:

  • Any frequency should work, given bright enough light. ✗ Observed: a sharp threshold f0.
  • Dim light should cause a delay while energy builds up. ✗ Observed: instant emission.
  • Brighter light should give faster electrons. ✗ Observed: the maximum kinetic energy is set by f alone.
  • Photon model explains all three: energy comes in lumps of hf, one lump per electron.
  • So the photoelectric effect is evidence for the particle (photon) nature of light.
Mission 1

Worked example

Light of wavelength 400 nm falls on potassium, work function 2.30 eV.

  • Photon energy: hf = hc/λ = (6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.00 × 10⁸)/(400 × 10⁻⁹) = 4.97 × 10⁻¹⁹ J = 3.11 eV.
  • Maximum kinetic energy = 3.11 − 2.30 = 0.81 eV = 1.29 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.
  • Threshold frequency: f0 = (work function)/h = (2.30 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹)/(6.63 × 10⁻³⁴) = 5.55 × 10¹⁴ Hz.
Quick check
?Light whose photons each have energy 4.0 eV shines on three metals: X, with work function 2.0 eV; Y, with work function 3.0 eV; and Z, with work function 4.5 eV. Which statement about the maximum kinetic energy of the electrons emitted from each metal is correct?
Mission check

?Ultraviolet light above the threshold frequency shines on a metal plate and electrons are emitted. The ultraviolet is replaced by ultraviolet of a shorter wavelength, with the same intensity (the same power per square metre). What happens?

Energy per photon is set by the frequency; the number of photons by the power. The same holds in skin: each ultraviolet photon can damage a molecule on its own; red photons cannot, however many arrive.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Experiments in the early 1800s convinced physicists light is a wave. Which experiment made them think again?

Make a guess: Experiments in the early 1800s convinced physicists light is a wave. Which experiment made them think again?

Mission 2

Demonstration: electroscope and zinc plate

  • Clean zinc plate on a gold-leaf electroscope, charged negatively: the leaf rises (repelled).
  • Shine ultraviolet on the zinc: the leaf falls. Electrons are leaving the zinc.
  • Shine a very bright visible lamp instead: no change. Below the threshold frequency.
  • Charge it positively and shine UV: no change. Emitted electrons are pulled back.
  • Put a sheet of glass (absorbs UV) in the beam: the leaf stops falling.
Quick check
?A clean zinc plate is isolated and initially uncharged, and it is not connected to any circuit or battery. In a vacuum, ultraviolet light with a frequency above the threshold frequency for zinc shines on the plate. Which claim is correct?
Mission check

?The zinc on a negatively charged electroscope is replaced by a clean plate of a metal that emits electrons only for light of wavelength shorter than 540 nm. A bright red lamp (650 nm) shines on it, then a dim blue lamp (450 nm). What does the leaf do?

Whether electrons leave depends on the energy of each photon, set by its frequency; brightness changes only how many leave. A sharp threshold like this fits photons, not waves.

Mission 3

Mission 3: An LED stays dark until the voltage reaches a set value, and that value depends on its colour. Why?

Make a guess: An LED stays dark until the voltage reaches a set value, and that value depends on its colour. Why?

Mission 3

From electron energy to photon energy

  • An electron accelerated through a p.d. V gains energy eV.
  • If all of it goes into one photon: eV = hf = hc/λ.
  • That photon has the highest frequency and shortest wavelength possible.
  • Example: 2.0 V → 2.0 eV → λ = hc/eV = (6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.00 × 10⁸)/(1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 2.0) = 622 nm (orange-red).
Mission 3

Planck constant from LEDs

  • An LED emits light of (nearly) one wavelength λ, given by the maker or measured with a grating.
  • It begins to emit only when the p.d. across it reaches a threshold value V0.
  • Model: each electron crossing the LED gives one photon: eV0 = hc/λ.
  • Circuit: variable supply, protective resistor in series, voltmeter across the LED, ammeter (or watch for the first glow through a tube).
  • Repeat for several colours. Plot V0 against 1/λ: straight line, gradient = hc/e, so h = e × gradient/c.
  • No semiconductor theory needed.
Mission 3

Worked example

Red LED, λ = 630 nm, threshold p.d. V0 = 1.96 V.

  • eV0 = hc/λ → h = eV0λ/c
  • h = (1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 1.96 × 630 × 10⁻⁹)/(3.00 × 10⁸) = 6.59 × 10⁻³⁴ J s.
  • Close to 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s. Judging the exact moment the LED "starts" is the main uncertainty: use the graph of several LEDs.
Quick check
?A student is given the frequency f of each LED's light, not its wavelength. She plots threshold p.d. V0 against f. The gradient of the straight line is G. Which expression gives the Planck constant? (e = electron charge, c = speed of light)
Mission check

?An LED of wavelength λ starts to emit at a threshold p.d. V0. A second LED starts to emit at 2V0. What is its wavelength?

eV0 = hc/λ: the higher the threshold voltage, the shorter the wavelength. Each colour's photons need a different energy.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Electrons fired at a thin carbon film make rings, just as light does. How can particles diffract?

Make a guess: Electrons fired at a thin carbon film make rings, just as light does. How can particles diffract?

Mission 4

Wave or particle?

  • Light: diffraction and interference → wave. Photoelectric effect → photons (particles).
  • Electrons: definite mass and charge, single impacts → particle. Electron diffraction → wave.
  • Wave–particle duality: light and matter both need both models. The experiment decides which shows.
Mission 4

The de Broglie wavelength

  • Every moving particle has a wavelength.
  • λ = h/p, where p is the momentum.
  • More momentum → shorter wavelength.
  • h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, so λ is tiny unless p is tiny.
Mission 4

Worked example

  • An electron is accelerated from rest through 150 V. Find λ.
  • Kinetic energy: Eₖ = eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 150 = 2.40 × 10⁻¹⁷ J.
  • Momentum: Eₖ = p²/2m, so p = √(2mEₖ) = √(2 × 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ × 2.40 × 10⁻¹⁷) = 6.61 × 10⁻²⁴ kg m s⁻¹.
  • λ = h/p = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ ÷ 6.61 × 10⁻²⁴ = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁰ m.
  • About the spacing of atoms in a crystal.
Quick check
?An electron is accelerated from rest through a potential difference of 2.0 kV. Take h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, me = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg and c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹, and treat the electron as non-relativistic. What is the de Broglie wavelength of the electron?
Quick check
?Predict first. In an electron diffraction tube, electrons accelerated through a potential difference of a few kilovolts pass through a thin film of graphite and strike a fluorescent screen, where bright concentric rings are seen. How does this experiment provide evidence for the wave nature of matter?
Mission 4

Electron diffraction

  • Electron gun: a hot filament releases electrons; a few kV accelerates them.
  • They pass through a thin film of polycrystalline graphite, then hit a fluorescent screen.
  • The screen shows bright concentric rings: a diffraction pattern.
  • Electron λ (about 10⁻¹¹ m) is a few times smaller than the spacing between carbon atoms (about 10⁻¹⁰ m): close enough for the atomic layers to diffract them.
  • Many tiny crystals at random angles → rings, not spots.
  • Ring angles fit λ = h/p. Only a wave model explains this.
Mission 4

More momentum, less diffraction

  • Raise the accelerating potential difference → electrons faster → more momentum.
  • More momentum → shorter λ → smaller diffraction angles → smaller rings.
  • Lower potential difference → longer λ → larger rings.
  • From eV = p²/2m: λ ∝ 1/√V. Four times the p.d. halves λ.
Mission 4

When does the wave nature show?

  • Diffraction is noticeable only when λ is comparable to the gap or object size.
  • A cricket ball at 30 m s⁻¹: λ ≈ 10⁻³⁴ m. No gap is that small, so no diffraction is seen.
Mission check

?A beam of electrons and a beam of protons are each accelerated from rest through 100 V and aimed at the same crystal, whose rows of atoms are 2.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ m apart. Which beam shows clear diffraction?

Electrons have a wavelength λ = h/p. They diffract when it is close to the size of the gap, as with the spacing of carbon atoms.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Photon energy E = hf = hc/λ; 1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. Intensity sets the number of photons, frequency the energy of each.
  • One photon frees at most one electron; threshold hf0 = φ; maximum kinetic energy = hf − φ, independent of intensity. Evidence for photons.

Mission 2

  • Negatively charged zinc on an electroscope discharges under UV, not under bright visible light: a threshold frequency.

Mission 3

  • One electron's energy eV into one photon: eV = hc/λ.
  • LEDs: eV0 = hc/λ; plot V0 against 1/λ, h = e × gradient/c.

Mission 4

  • Particles have a de Broglie wavelength λ = h/p; electron diffraction is the evidence.
  • More momentum → shorter λ → less diffraction (smaller rings).
