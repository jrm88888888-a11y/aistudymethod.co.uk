Mission 1

Mission 1: Why do Christians all over the world still say words first written down over 1,600 years ago?

Make a guess: Why do Christians all over the world still say words first written down over 1,600 years ago?

Mission 1

What a creed is

  • A creed is a short statement of shared belief. From Latin credo: "I believe".
  • It sums up what a church teaches about God, Jesus and salvation.
  • Said aloud together, it shows who belongs: "this is what we believe".
  • The two most used: the Apostles' Creed and the Nicene Creed.
  • Both are built in three parts: Father, Son, Holy Spirit.
Mission 1

Where the Apostles' Creed came from

  • It grew from the questions asked of new Christians at baptism in Rome: "Do you believe in God the Father…?"
  • An early Roman form was written down by about 340.
  • Its final form was settled in Western Europe by about the 8th century.
  • A legend says each of the twelve apostles wrote one line. Historians see it as a summary of the apostles' teaching, not their words.
  • It is used mainly in Western churches: Catholic, Anglican, Lutheran, Methodist, Reformed.
Mission 1Read the source

The Apostles' Creed

"I believe in God the Father Almighty, Maker of heaven and earth:

And in Jesus Christ his only Son our Lord, Who was conceived by the Holy Ghost, Born of the Virgin Mary, Suffered under Pontius Pilate, Was crucified, dead, and buried, He descended into hell; The third day he rose again from the dead, He ascended into heaven, And sitteth on the right hand of God the Father Almighty; From thence he shall come to judge the quick and the dead.

I believe in the Holy Ghost; The holy catholic Church; The Communion of Saints; The Forgiveness of sins; The Resurrection of the body, And the life everlasting. Amen." (Book of Common Prayer, 1662)

  • Old words: Ghost = Spirit; quick = living; sitteth = sits.

Q1 Find the three parts. Which part is the longest?

Show answer

The Son's.

Q2 Which line ties the Creed to a real moment in history?

Show answer

"Suffered under Pontius Pilate".

Mission 1

What the Creed affirms

  • Father: Almighty Creator of everything.
  • Son: Jesus is God's Son and Lord; born as a human (Incarnation); died, rose, ascended; will return as judge.
  • "Descended into hell": went to the place of the dead, between his death and resurrection.
  • Holy Spirit: God at work now.
  • "Holy catholic Church": catholic means universal: all Christians everywhere, not one denomination.
  • "Communion of saints": all believers, living and dead, united in Christ.
  • Ends with hope: forgiveness, resurrection of the body, life everlasting.
Quick check
?In the Apostles' Creed, what does "the holy catholic Church" mean?
Mission 1

The Nicene Creed

  • Agreed at the Councils of Nicaea (325) and Constantinople (381).
  • Said at the Eucharist or Mass on Sundays (Catholic, Anglican, Lutheran) and at the Orthodox Divine Liturgy.
  • Same three parts, but more detail on who the Son is: "God of God, Light of Light, Very God of very God, Begotten, not made, Being of one substance with the Father".
  • More on the Spirit: "the Lord and giver of life", worshipped and glorified with the Father and the Son.
  • So it states the Trinity more fully: three Persons, each fully God.
Quick check
?Which of these is found in the Nicene Creed but not in the Apostles' Creed?
Mission 1

Creeds in Christian life

  • Baptism and confirmation: candidates (or parents and godparents) answer questions drawn from the Apostles' Creed.
  • Sunday worship: the Nicene Creed at Mass or Eucharist; the Apostles' Creed at Anglican Morning and Evening Prayer.
  • Unity: Christians of different churches can say the same words together.
  • Daily life: "forgiveness of sins" → forgive others; "Maker of heaven and earth" → care for creation; "he shall come to judge" → live responsibly; "resurrection of the body" → hope at funerals.
Mission check

?At a joint service, Catholics, Anglicans and Methodists stand and say the same creed together. Which reason best explains why Christians value doing this?

A creed is a shared, three-part map of belief; that is why the same old words still unite Christians today.

Mission 2: In less than a day Jesus went from supper with his friends to a cross. What happened, and why does it matter?

Make a guess: In less than a day Jesus went from supper with his friends to a cross. What happened, and why does it matter?

Mission 2

The last days

  • It is Passover in Jerusalem: the Jewish festival remembering freedom from slavery in Egypt.
  • Thursday evening: the Last Supper with the Twelve; then prayer in Gethsemane; Jesus is arrested after Judas betrays him.
  • Night and early morning: questioned by the Jewish council (Sanhedrin), then sent to Pontius Pilate, the Roman governor.
  • Friday: sentenced, crucified at Golgotha outside the city, dies in the afternoon, and is buried before the Sabbath.
  • Christians remember these days in Holy Week: Maundy Thursday, Good Friday, then Easter.
Mission 2Read the source

The Last Supper (Luke 22)

He took bread, and when he had given thanks, he broke and gave it to them, saying, "This is my body which is given for you. Do this in memory of me." Likewise, he took the cup after supper, saying, "This cup is the new covenant in my blood, which is poured out for you." (Luke 22:19–20)

  • Jesus says one at the table will betray him (22:21), and that Peter will deny him three times (22:34).
  • The disciples argue over who is greatest; Jesus: "I am amongst you as one who serves" (22:27).

Q2 What is the cup called?

Mission 2

Gethsemane and the arrest

  • On the Mount of Olives Jesus prays in anguish; he finds the disciples asleep (Luke 22:41–46).
  • "Father, if you are willing, remove this cup from me. Nevertheless, not my will, but yours, be done." (Luke 22:42)
  • Judas arrives with an armed crowd and identifies Jesus with a kiss.
  • A disciple strikes the high priest's servant with a sword.
  • Jesus heals the servant's ear (Luke 22:51).
  • Significance for Christians: fully human anguish; full obedience to the Father.

?At the Last Supper Jesus links the cup with a "new covenant" in his blood. What do Christians understand by "new covenant"?
Mission 2

The trials

  • Before the Jewish council (led by the high priest Caiaphas): false witnesses; the high priest asks whether he is the Christ, the Son of God. His answer is judged blasphemy (Matthew 26:57–68).
  • Meanwhile Peter denies knowing Jesus three times, then weeps (26:69–75).
  • Before Pilate: the charge becomes political: claiming to be "King of the Jews", a threat to Rome.
  • Luke: Pilate sends him to Herod Antipas, who mocks him (23:6–12); Pilate says three times he finds no guilt (23:4, 14, 22).
  • Pilate offers to release a prisoner; the crowd chooses Barabbas. Pilate hands Jesus over to be crucified.
  • Churches today teach that the blame cannot be laid on Jews as a people, then or now (Catholic Church, Nostra Aetate, 1965).
Mission 2Read the source

The crucifixion (Luke 23)

  • Simon of Cyrene carries the cross; women of Jerusalem weep for Jesus (23:26–31).
  • As he is crucified: "Father, forgive them, for they don't know what they are doing." (23:34)
  • To a criminal who asks to be remembered: "today you will be with me in Paradise." (23:43)
  • Darkness; the Temple curtain is torn in two (23:44–45).
  • "Father, into your hands I commit my spirit!" (23:46) The centurion: "Certainly this was a righteous man." (23:47)

Q1 What do Jesus' words from the cross show about him?

Show answer

Forgiveness, mercy and trust in the Father to the end.

Mission 2

The burial

  • Joseph of Arimathea, a respected disciple (Luke: a council member who had not agreed with the decision), asks Pilate for the body.
  • He wraps it in linen and lays it in a tomb cut in rock; a large stone is rolled across the entrance.
  • Women who had followed Jesus from Galilee watch where he is laid.
  • Significance: Jesus truly died. The empty tomb on Sunday depends on it.
Quick check
?When Jesus died, the Gospels say the Temple curtain was torn in two. What do many Christians take this to mean?
Mission 2

Why the cross matters

  • Christians believe the crucifixion was not a defeat but the centre of God's plan.
  • Salvation: Jesus' death brings forgiveness and restores the relationship with God (atonement; full in an earlier lesson).
  • Who Jesus is: innocent, obedient, forgiving; the King whose throne is a cross.
  • A righteous man (centurion, Luke 23:47).
  • God shares suffering: Christians believe God knows human pain from the inside.
  • Example: love that gives itself for others (John 15:13).
Quick check
?Why do Christians call the day of Jesus' crucifixion "Good Friday"?
Mission 2Diversity

Remembering the cross

  • Catholic: the crucifix (Christ's body on the cross); Stations of the Cross; no Mass on Good Friday, but veneration of the cross.
  • Orthodox: Holy Friday services; the Epitaphios, an embroidered burial cloth, is carried in procession.
  • Protestant: often an empty cross, stressing the resurrection; Good Friday services and ecumenical "walks of witness".
  • Eucharist views: Catholic and Orthodox believe Christ is truly present in bread and wine (Catholic: transubstantiation); many Protestants see a memorial; Quakers and the Salvation Army hold no outward sacraments.
Mission 2Evaluate

"The crucifixion was the most important event of Holy Week."

  • Reasons for: Christians believe salvation comes through Jesus' death (atonement). The cross is the central Christian symbol. Paul: "we preach Christ crucified" (1 Corinthians 1:23).
  • Reasons against: without the resurrection, the cross would be a defeat (1 Corinthians 15:14). The Last Supper gave the Eucharist, the centre of most Christian worship. Gethsemane shows the obedience that makes the cross meaningful.
  • Another view: the events form one story, the Paschal Mystery: none can be ranked alone.
  • Judge it by: Which event do the others depend on? Which shapes worship most? What do the Gospels give most space to?
Mission check

?A hospital chaplain sits with a dying patient who says, "Does God even know what this is like?" The chaplain points to a cross on the wall. What belief about the crucifixion is the chaplain relying on?

Christians see the cross both as the act that reconciles people to God and as God's presence in human suffering.

Mission 3: One bite of fruit in a garden: why do many Christians say it affects every human being since?

Make a guess: One bite of fruit in a garden: why do many Christians say it affects every human being since?

Mission 3

The Fall

  • In Eden, God allows the first humans to eat from every tree but one: the tree of the knowledge of good and evil (Genesis 2:16–17).
  • The serpent says that if they eat, they will be "like God" (Genesis 3:5, paraphrase).
  • The woman eats, and gives some to the man (3:6). They feel shame and hide from God (3:7–8).
  • Each blames another: the man the woman, the woman the serpent.
  • Consequences: pain, hard work, death; they are sent out of the garden (3:16–24).
  • Christians call this the Fall: humans fall away from their close relationship with God.
  • Genesis says "fruit". The apple comes from later art.
Mission 3Read the source

Genesis 3

"When the woman saw that the tree was good for food, and that it was a delight to the eyes, and that the tree was to be desired to make one wise, she took some of its fruit, and ate. Then she gave some to her husband with her, and he ate it, too." (Genesis 3:6)

"You will eat bread by the sweat of your face until you return to the ground, for you were taken out of it. For you are dust, and you shall return to dust." (Genesis 3:19)

Q1 Find three reasons the tree attracted the woman.

Show answer

Q2 What two results does God announce in 3:19?

Show answer

Mission 3

What sin is

  • Sin: any thought, word or action that goes against God's will.
  • Sin damages relationships: with God, with others, with oneself, with creation. (In Genesis 3, all four break.)
  • Paul: "all have sinned, and fall short of the glory of God" (Romans 3:23).
  • God's law (the commandments) shows what sin is, but cannot by itself stop people sinning (Romans 3:20, paraphrase).
Mission 3Diversity

Original sin: different views

  • Original sin: the sinful state, or tendency, that all humans inherit from the first sin. Not the same as each person's own sins.
  • Augustine (354–430): through Adam, all humans inherit a fallen nature and share his guilt (Romans 5:12).
  • Catholic: every human is born lacking the original holiness God intended; original sin is "contracted", not committed. Baptism removes it, but a pull towards sin (concupiscence) remains.
  • Protestant Reformers (Luther, Calvin): human nature is corrupted in every part; only God's grace can save.
  • Orthodox: "ancestral sin": humans inherit death and a weakened nature, but not Adam's guilt.
  • Many liberal Christians: the Fall is a symbolic story about every human's tendency to selfishness.
Quick check
?Which statement best matches Catholic teaching on original sin?
Mission 3

Sin: a barrier to salvation

  • "For the wages of sin is death, but the free gift of God is eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord." (Romans 6:23)
  • Salvation: being saved from sin and restored to friendship with God.
  • Christians believe sin separates people from God, and most say humans cannot remove it by their own efforts.
  • Jesus says people "loved the darkness rather than the light" (John 3:19, paraphrase), yet God sent the Son to save the world (John 3:17).
  • Christians believe the barrier is removed by God's grace through Jesus.
Quick check
?In Romans 6:23, Paul contrasts "wages" with a "free gift". What point is he making?
Mission 3Evaluate

"The Fall must be literally true for original sin to make sense."

  • Reasons for: Paul treats Adam as one man through whom sin entered the world (Romans 5:12). Without a first sin, why would every human share a fallen nature? Catholic teaching says Genesis uses figurative language but describes a real event at the start of human history.
  • Reasons against: adam means "human": the story can describe every person. Selfishness can be seen everywhere without a history lesson. Orthodox teaching stresses inherited death, not inherited guilt. Many Christians accept evolution and still believe in original sin.
  • Judge it by: Does a doctrine need a historical event, or only a true picture of human nature? How should Paul's use of Adam be read?
Mission check

?A toddler snatches a toy from another child, though nobody taught her to. Christians who accept the doctrine of original sin would most likely say this:

The Fall explains, for many Christians, why selfishness comes so naturally: a wounded nature passed to every human.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Why do earthquakes and wars make some people doubt that an all-powerful, loving God exists?

Make a guess: Why do earthquakes and wars make some people doubt that an all-powerful, loving God exists?

Mission 4

Two kinds of evil

  • Evil: what is morally wrong or causes harm. Suffering: the pain or distress that results.
  • Moral evil: caused by human choices: murder, war, abuse, bullying.
  • Natural evil: caused by nature, not human choice: earthquakes, floods, disease.
  • Also called moral suffering and natural suffering.
  • Some suffering mixes both: a famine made worse by war; a flood made worse by poor building.
Quick check
?A dam bursts after its builders cut corners to save money, and a village is flooded. Which kind of evil is this?
Mission 4

The inconsistent triad

  • Christians hold three beliefs:
  • 1. God is omnipotent: able to stop evil.
  • 2. God is benevolent: wants to stop evil.
  • 3. Evil and suffering exist.
  • Together they seem to clash: if 1 and 2 are true, why is 3 true? This is the problem of evil.
  • So the problem is how a loving and righteous God can allow suffering.
Mission 4Read the source

Epicurus' questions

Epicurus (341–270 BCE), a Greek philosopher, is credited with these questions. David Hume (1711–1776) gives them in Dialogues Concerning Natural Religion (1779):

"Is he willing to prevent evil, but not able? then is he impotent. Is he able, but not willing? then is he malevolent. Is he both able and willing? whence then is evil?"

  • impotent = powerless · malevolent = wishing harm · whence = from where

Q1 Which question challenges God's omnipotence?

Show answer

The first.

Q2 Which challenges God's benevolence?

Show answer

The second.

Q3 What does the third question ask?

Show answer

If God is both, where does evil come from?

Mission 4

When suffering shakes faith

  • Believers ask: Why me? Why do the innocent suffer? Why doesn't God act?
  • The Bible itself asks: "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Psalm 22:1). Jesus prays these words on the cross (Mark 15:34).
  • Some believers doubt or lose faith; some become agnostic or atheist.
  • Many people see the problem of evil as the strongest argument against God's existence.
  • Other believers say their faith grew: they found God with them in suffering, and point to Jesus, who suffered too.
Quick check
?A flood kills thousands of people. Why might this lead a Christian to question God?
Mission 4Evaluate

"Evil and suffering prove that God does not exist."

  • Reasons for: the inconsistent triad; Epicurus' questions seem unanswered. Natural evil is not caused by human choice. There is so much suffering of the innocent, including children.
  • Reasons against: "prove" is a strong word: God may have reasons humans cannot see, as the book of Job suggests (Job, a blameless man, suffers without being told why). Free will may need the possibility of evil (a later mission). Believers experience God's compassion even in suffering (psalms such as Psalm 103 praise God's compassion). Christians believe God shares suffering in Jesus.
  • Judge it by: Is it a contradiction (a proof) or a challenge (evidence against)? Could God have reasons we cannot know? Do natural and moral evil need different answers?
Mission 4Worked answer

Why the problem troubles believers

Question: Explain two reasons why evil and suffering may cause some Christians to question their faith.

  • Reason 1: Natural suffering, such as an earthquake, is not caused by human choice. Development: as Creator of nature, God seems responsible, which is hard to square with a benevolent God.
  • Reason 2: Christians believe God is loving and powerful: "His kingdom rules over all" (Psalm 103:19). Development: when prayers for a suffering loved one seem unanswered, God's power and love can feel absent.
  • (b) 4-mark tip: two reasons, each one developed.
Mission check

?A newborn baby has a serious, painful illness. Why do many people find this kind of suffering especially hard to square with belief in an omnipotent, benevolent God?

Power, love and suffering together form the problem; a later mission looks at how Christians respond to it.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Christians still trust God when disaster strikes. What answers do they give, and do any of them work?

Make a guess: Christians still trust God when disaster strikes. What answers do they give, and do any of them work?

Mission 5

Three kinds of answer

  • Christians do not usually deny the problem. They respond in three ways:
  • Theoretical: reasons why a good God might allow evil (free will, soul-making).
  • Biblical: what scripture says about suffering (Job, a good man who suffers terribly without being told why; the Psalms; the cross).
  • Practical: what to do about suffering (prayer, charity, care for the sufferer).
  • A reasoned defence of God's goodness in the face of evil is a theodicy.
  • Edexcel calls these solutions: biblical, theoretical and practical.
Mission 5

Free will (Augustine)

  • St Augustine of Hippo (354–430), Confessions (c. 400) and City of God (413–426).
  • God made everything good (Genesis 1:31). Evil is not a thing God made: it is a lack or loss of good (a privation).
  • God gave angels and humans free will. They turned from God: the Fall (Genesis 3).
  • Moral evil comes from misused free will. Augustine links natural evil to a creation disordered by the Fall.
  • Why allow it? Love and goodness must be freely chosen; a world of robots could not love.
Mission 5

The vale of soul-making (Irenaeus, Hick)

  • St Irenaeus (c. 130–c. 202): humans are made in God's image but must grow into his likeness.
  • John Hick (1922–2012), Evil and the God of Love (1966), develops this idea.
  • Hick borrows the poet John Keats' phrase (1819): the world is a "vale of soul-making".
  • A world with real dangers and pain lets people develop courage, compassion, patience and trust.
  • In a world with no risk, nothing would be at stake: no one could be brave or kind.
  • Hick believed all people will finally reach that goal (heaven), so suffering is not wasted.
Quick check
?After a flood, neighbours who had never spoken risk their lives to rescue strangers. Which response to the problem of evil uses events like this as evidence?
Mission 5Read the source

Job's answer

  • Job is a good man who loses his wealth, his children and his health (Job 1–2).
  • Job's friends insist he must have sinned. Job, who is innocent, demands an answer from God.
  • God answers out of a whirlwind: "Where were you when I laid the foundations of the earth? Declare, if you have understanding." (Job 38:4)
  • Job replies: "I know that you can do all things, and that no purpose of yours can be restrained." (Job 42:2)
  • "I had heard of you by the hearing of the ear, but now my eye sees you. Therefore I abhor myself, and repent in dust and ashes." (Job 42:5–6)
  • God says the friends were wrong about him (42:7, paraphrase) and restores Job (42:10).

Show answer

No: God shows how far his wisdom exceeds Job's.

Q2 What changes for Job?

Show answer

  • "This is my comfort in my affliction, for your word has revived me." (Psalm 119:50)
  • "It is good for me that I have been afflicted, that I may learn your statutes." (Psalm 119:71)

Q2 Which theodicy does verse 71 sound like?

Show answer

Mission 5

Practical responses: prayer and charity

  • Prayer: Christians bring suffering to God, for others and for themselves. It brings comfort, strength and a sense of God's presence.
  • Charity (from Latin caritas, love): Christians act to reduce suffering: Jesus healed the sick and taught care for the hungry and sick (Matthew 25:31–46).
  • Examples: Christian Aid and CAFOD (disaster relief); hospices; foodbanks run by churches.
  • Many Christians say the practical answer matters most: evil is something to fight, not just explain.
Mission 5

The God who suffers with us

  • Christians believe that in the Incarnation God became human in Jesus, and that Jesus suffered and died on the cross.
  • So God does not watch suffering from far away: God has entered it.
  • Christians also hope that suffering is not the end: the resurrection points to a future without it (Revelation 21:4).
Mission 5Diversity

Different emphases

  • Catholic: suffering joined to Christ's can be redemptive; many Catholics "offer up" pain in prayer.
  • Orthodox: the Fall disordered all creation; Christ's resurrection begins its healing.
  • Reformed Protestants: God's providence: "all things work together for good for those who love God" (Romans 8:28).
  • Liberal Protestants: often stress practical action and God's suffering with people over a full explanation.
  • Some Christians see suffering as punishment or a test of faith (see Mission 4); many reject the punishment idea (John 9:1–3).
Quick check
?How does belief in the Incarnation shape many Christians' response to suffering?
Mission 5Evaluate

"Christian responses solve the problem of evil."

  • Reasons for: free will explains moral evil and makes real love possible. Soul-making gives natural evil a purpose. Job shows that limited humans may not see God's reasons. Prayer and charity actually reduce suffering.
  • Reasons against: natural evil (disease, earthquakes) is hard to blame on human choice. Could God not make free beings who always choose good? (J. L. Mackie, "Evil and Omnipotence", 1955.) The amount of suffering, of children especially, seems more than soul-making needs. Non-religious thinkers, including humanists, say no theodicy works.
  • Another view: many Christians say the problem is not "solved" but lived with: trust, as Job learned, and action, as Jesus showed.
  • Judge it by: Does it explain natural as well as moral evil? Does it fit the amount of suffering? Is a practical answer enough without a theoretical one?
Mission 5Worked answer

Two solutions, one source

Question: Explain two ways Christians respond to the problem of evil and suffering. Refer to a source of wisdom and authority.

  • Way 1: Free will. Development: God gave humans the freedom to choose, so love can be real; moral evil comes from misusing that freedom, not from God.
  • Way 2: Trust, as Job did. Source: "I know that you can do all things" (Job 42:2). Development: Christians accept that God's purposes are greater than they can see, so they keep faith without a full answer.
  • (c) 5-mark tip: two ways, each developed, plus one source of wisdom and authority.
Mission check

?A volcano erupts millions of years before the first humans exist. A Christian wants to explain why God would allow natural destruction like this. Which explanation is hardest to use here?

Different evils fit different answers: free will suits moral evil; soul-making and Job's trust reach further; prayer and charity act on what remains.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • A creed is a short statement of shared belief; the Apostles' Creed grew from baptism questions in Rome and has three parts: Father, Son, Holy Spirit.
  • The Nicene Creed (325, 381) adds that the Son is "of one substance with the Father", stating the Trinity more fully.

Mission 2

  • Last Supper (bread and cup, new covenant) → Gethsemane → arrest → trials (council, Pilate) → crucifixion → burial.
  • Christians believe Jesus' death was the centre of God's plan: forgiveness, a new covenant, God sharing human suffering.
  • Denominations remember the cross differently (crucifix or empty cross; views of the Eucharist).

Mission 3

  • The Fall (Genesis 3): humans disobey God and their relationships with God, each other and creation break.
  • Original sin is the fallen state all humans inherit; Catholic, Protestant, Orthodox and liberal Christians understand it differently.
  • Sin is a barrier to salvation: "the wages of sin is death, but the free gift of God is eternal life" (Romans 6:23).

Mission 4

  • Moral evil comes from human choices; natural evil does not.
  • The inconsistent triad: an omnipotent, benevolent God and the existence of evil seem to clash (Epicurus' questions).
  • Suffering can make believers question their faith or God's existence.
  • Psalm 103 shows the loving, powerful God that makes the question sharp.

Mission 5

  • Christians respond to evil with theoretical answers (free will, soul-making), biblical answers (Job) and practical action (prayer, charity).
  • Many add that in Jesus God shares human suffering.
  • Psalm 119: comfort and learning through God's word in affliction.
