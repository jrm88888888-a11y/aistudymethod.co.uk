Poetry · "The Charge of the Light Brigade"Screen 3

A galloping beat

  • Dactylic dimeter: two beats a line, each a dactyl (DUM-da-da): HALF a league | HALF a league. Hooves at full gallop.
  • Short lines and repetition ("Cannon to right of them, / Cannon to left of them") drive the men on.

  • The order is direct speech, but the commander is never named.

Mission checkScreen 9

?600 men ride into a deadly blunder. Why does Tennyson say 'Honour'?

Tennyson honours the soldiers' courage and their duty to obey, even into "the valley of Death". But by telling us that "Some one" had blundered, he lets readers see the waste too. Many readers hear celebration; others hear a quiet criticism of those in command.

Mission 2

A cricket match and a desert battle. Why the same words?

Make a guess: A cricket match and a desert battle. Why the same words?

Conflict · "Vitaï Lampada"Screen 1

A school, a game and an empire

  • Henry Newbolt (1862–1938) wrote "Vitaï Lampada" in 1892; it was published in his collection Admirals All (1897).
  • The Close is the cricket ground of Clifton College, the public school Newbolt attended.
  • The title is Latin, from Lucretius: the torch of life.
  • Stanza 2 is based on the Battle of Abu Klea in Sudan (1885), a fight of the British Empire. Public schools like Clifton trained many future army officers.

Rhythm, refrain and the torch

  • Eight-line stanzas rhymed ABABCDCD, with four strong beats a line: a brisk, ballad-like beat, easy to chant.
  • Stanza 2 crowds disaster into short clauses: "The Gatling's jammed and the Colonel dead". Then one voice restores order.

  • Stanza 3 explains the title: each boy carries the code "like a torch in flame" and passes it on.

?A cricket match and a desert battle. Why the same words?

The refrain carries the captain's call from the Close to the desert, showing the public-school code of selfless duty at work in war. Newbolt presents this sincerely. The strongest answers explain that view and then weigh how readers after 1914–18 have questioned the idea that war is a game.

Mission 3

He shot a man he'd have shared a drink with. Why can't he say why?

Make a guess: He shot a man he'd have shared a drink with. Why can't he say why?

Conflict poetry · "The Man He Killed"Screen 1

Who is speaking

  • Hardy wrote the poem in 1902, during the Second Boer War (1899–1902) in South Africa, and collected it in 1909.
  • The whole poem sits inside speech marks: a dramatic monologue by an ordinary ex-soldier, talking in a pub.
  • He remembers shooting an enemy soldier "face to face".
  • Words to know: nipperkin, a small measure of beer; 'list, enlist; traps, belongings; half-a-crown, a coin (2s 6d), a loan to a friend in need.

A simple song for a hard thought

  • Five quatrains, rhymed ABAB, with short lines of three beats; each third line stretches to four.
  • The tune is plain and regular, like a ballad or a pub song, which fits a working man's voice.
  • Hardy uses colloquial speech: "Off-hand like", "Just so", "you'd treat".
  • The calm form clashes with the subject: killing a man is told in a jingle.

How the reason falls apart

  • Repetition with a dash: "because— / Because" shows him hunting for words.
  • Caesuras in stanza 3 ("Just so:", "That's clear enough;") sound like someone trying to convince himself.
  • Enjambment: stanza 3 runs into stanza 4, the poem's only run-on between stanzas.
  • Stanza 4 finds the real reason: like the speaker, the enemy was poor and "out of work".
  • "Quaint and curious" is understatement: mild words for a killing.

?He shot a man he'd have shared a drink with. Why can't he say why?

The speaker reaches for the official answer, "he was my foe", but stumbles over it, because the man he killed was a working man like himself who might have been a friend. Hardy uses an ordinary voice and a simple song form to question why ordinary people are sent to kill each other. How bitter or how gentle the ending is ("quaint and curious") is open to debate.

Mission 4

A recruiting poem calls war a game. What does that hide?

Make a guess: A recruiting poem calls war a game. What does that hide?

Conflict · "Who's for the Game?"Screen 1

A poem with a job to do

  • Jessie Pope (1868–1941) was a journalist and writer of light, popular verse.
  • "Who's for the Game?" first appeared in a British newspaper in 1915 and was collected in her More War Poems.
  • In 1915 the army still relied on volunteers. Conscription began only with the Military Service Act of 1916. This helps explain why the poem works so hard to persuade.
  • Wilfred Owen later attacked such poems: an early draft of "Dulce et Decorum Est" was dedicated to Jessie Pope.
Extract Conflict · "Who's for the Game?"Screen 2

"Who's for the Game?" (the whole poem)

Conflict · "Who's for the Game?"Screen 3

How it persuades

  • The poem is built from rhetorical questions: "Who's for…?", "Who'll…?". Each one dares the reader to answer "Me!"
  • An extended metaphor turns war into sport: "tackle", "toe the line", "a turn to himself in the show".
  • Contrast: players against spectators. The volunteer plays; the man who stays home wants "a seat in the stand".
  • It ends with personification: "Your country is up to her neck in a fight, / And she's looking and calling for you."

Conflict · "Who's for the Game?"Screen 5

A cheerful tune, read critically

  • Lines alternate four and three beats and rhyme ABAB: a bouncy, chant-like rhythm, like a song.
  • Understatement: "it won't be a picnic, not much" makes danger sound like mild discomfort.
  • "red crashing game" is the only hint of blood, and it is made exciting.
  • Words of shame target men who stay home: "sit tight", "lie low".
  • Read critically: the poem never mentions death. Many readers see that silence as its most important feature.

?A recruiting poem calls war a game. What does that hide?

Calling war "the game" turns fighting into sport and hides its cost: death is never mentioned, and a crutch is better than being "out of the fun". The questions, shame words and jaunty rhythm all serve recruitment. Read it critically, alongside Owen's poems that answer it.

Mission 5

No bells, no prayers, no coffins. How are these dead mourned?

Make a guess: No bells, no prayers, no coffins. How are these dead mourned?

Conflict · "Anthem for Doomed Youth"Screen 1

A soldier writes an anthem

  • Wilfred Owen (1893–1918) was an officer on the Western Front. He wrote this sonnet between September and October 1917 at Craiglockhart War Hospital, Edinburgh, where he was recovering.
  • There he met the poet Siegfried Sassoon, who suggested changes, including "doomed" for "dead".
  • Owen was killed on 4 November 1918, a week before the war ended. His Poems were published in 1920.
  • An anthem is a solemn song of praise, often sung in church.

Conflict · "Anthem for Doomed Youth"Screen 5

Sounds of war, silence of grief

  • Personification: "the monstrous anger of the guns".
  • Alliteration and onomatopoeia: "stuttering rifles' rapid rattle" imitates gunfire.
  • Metaphor: shells become "demented choirs", a mad mockery of church singing.
  • In the sestet the sounds soften. "pallor" and "pall" sound alike: pale faces become the coffin cloth.
  • The last line recalls a mourning custom: blinds drawn in a house where someone has died.

Mission checkScreen 9

?No bells, no prayers, no coffins. How are these dead mourned?

At the front, only the guns, rifles and shells "mourn" them: a harsh mockery of a funeral. At home, the rituals become private grief: the glimmer of tears in boys' eyes, pale faces, "patient minds" and blinds drawn at dusk. Owen shows how war strips death of dignity, while love at home keeps it human.

Mission 6

He expects to die in the war. Why doesn't he care who wins?

Make a guess: He expects to die in the war. Why doesn't he care who wins?

Conflict · "An Irish Airman Foresees His Death"Screen 1

An Irish pilot in a British war

  • W. B. Yeats (1865–1939) wrote the poem in 1918; it was published in 1919 in The Wild Swans at Coole.
  • The airman is widely believed to be Major Robert Gregory, Yeats's friend, the only child of Lady Gregory of Coole Park in the west of Ireland.
  • Gregory joined the Royal Flying Corps in 1916 and was killed in Italy on 23 January 1918.
  • Yeats held the poem back during the war, avoiding political poems.
Extract Conflict · "An Irish Airman Foresees His Death"Screen 2

"An Irish Airman Foresees His Death" (the whole poem)

Conflict · "An Irish Airman Foresees His Death"Screen 3

Every reason but one

  • The airman speaks in the first person and calmly predicts his own death: "I know that I shall meet my fate".
  • He feels no hatred for the enemy and no love for those he guards.
  • His "country" is a small Irish district: the war's end will change nothing for "Kiltartan's poor".
  • He rejects every usual reason to fight: "law", "duty", politicians ("public man") and "cheering crowds".
  • Only one thing drove him to the sky.

Conflict · "An Irish Airman Foresees His Death"Screen 5

A poem in balance

  • Sixteen lines in one stanza, rhymed as four ABAB quatrains, in iambic tetrameter (four beats a line): steady and controlled.
  • Parallelism sets opposites side by side: "Those that I fight I do not hate / Those that I guard I do not love".
  • The ending mirrors itself (a chiasmus): "The years to come seemed waste of breath, / A waste of breath the years behind / In balance with this life, this death."
  • The calm form matches his calm choice: "I balanced all".

Mission checkScreen 9

?He expects to die in the war. Why doesn't he care who wins?

The war's outcome will bring his people, "Kiltartan's poor", neither loss nor gain, so he does not fight for a side. He flies for "A lonely impulse of delight", and having weighed everything, he accepts that this life and this death are worth more than the empty years. Readers debate whether this is heroic freedom or a sad detachment.

Poetry · "What lips my lips have kissed"Screen 1

Millay's sonnet in brief

  • Edna St. Vincent Millay (1892–1950) was an American poet; she won the Pulitzer Prize for Poetry in 1923.
  • This sonnet first appeared in Vanity Fair in November 1920, then in The Harp-Weaver and Other Poems (1923). Your anthology adds the title "Sonnet XLIII".
  • The speaker, alone on a rainy night, thinks about the people she loved when she was young. She can't remember them, but she feels their absence.
  • A sonnet has 14 lines and is traditionally a love poem.

Poetry · "What lips my lips have kissed"Screen 4

Form: an octave, then a turn

  • A Petrarchan sonnet: an octave (lines 1–8, rhymed abbaabba) and a sestet (lines 9–14, rhymed cdedce).
  • Iambic pentameter: ten syllables, five beats: what LIPS my LIPS have KISSED, and WHERE, and WHY.
  • The volta (turn) comes at line 9, "Thus": from the ghostly rain to a calm extended simile of the tree.
  • Enjambment lets thoughts spill over line ends ("the rain / Is full of ghosts"), as memories spill into the present.

Poetry · "What lips my lips have kissed"Screen 6

Why is it in a Conflict anthology?

  • Conflict can be inner: a struggle inside one person.
  • Forgetting against feeling: "I have forgotten", yet "a quiet pain" stirs.
  • Past against present: summer "sang in me"; now it "sings no more". Youth gives way to age, summer to winter.
  • Its subject is loss: people who "not again / Will turn to me". That links it to poems about those who don't come back.
  • It was published in 1920, two years after the First World War, but it never mentions war.

Recap

Big ideas

  • The poem (1854) responds to news of the Light Brigade's charge at Balaclava, caused by a confused order.
  • Galloping dactyls, repetition and a changing refrain carry the men forward; personification makes the valley a devouring monster.
  • It honours the soldiers' courage while showing the blunder: strong answers weigh both.
  • In Unit 2 you can bring an unannotated anthology, so know where the key lines are.
  • "Vitaï Lampada" (1892, published 1897) links a school cricket match to a desert battle through one refrain: "Play up! play up! and play the game!"
  • A brisk ballad beat and the torch image present a selfless code passed from one generation to the next.
  • Pair it with "Who's for the Game?" or contrast it with "Anthem for Doomed Youth"; weigh the poem's view against later readings.
  • A dramatic monologue by an ex-soldier, written in 1902 during the Boer War: he killed a man he would have treated to a drink, and he can't find a reason that holds.
  • Mirror-image lines, the stumbling "because—", the one run-on stanza and the understatement "quaint and curious" carry Hardy's doubt about war.
  • Your anthology is open in the exam, so spend your effort on what methods do, and compare both poems throughout.
  • "Who's for the Game?" (1915) was a newspaper poem written to persuade men to volunteer before conscription began in 1916.
  • Rhetorical questions, a sporting metaphor, understatement and a bouncy rhythm make war sound like a game and hide its dangers.
  • Read it critically and compare it with poems that show war's real cost, such as "Anthem for Doomed Youth".
  • "Anthem for Doomed Youth" (written 1917, published 1920) is a sonnet that asks how soldiers who "die as cattle" can be mourned.
  • The octave replaces funeral rites with the noise of war; the sestet turns to quiet grief at home.
  • Contrast it with "Who's for the Game?" or compare it with other poems about death in war.
  • "An Irish Airman Foresees His Death" (written 1918, published 1919) is spoken by an Irish pilot, widely believed to be Robert Gregory, who calmly expects to die.
  • He rejects hatred, love, law, duty and crowds; he flies for "A lonely impulse of delight".
  • Balanced quatrains, parallelism and chiasmus make it a strong contrast with poems about duty or anger.
  • A Petrarchan sonnet (1920; The Harp-Weaver, 1923): an octave of ghostly rain and quiet pain, then a turn to the winter tree.
  • Personification ("ghosts" that "tap and sigh") and the extended simile of the tree show loss felt as emptiness.
  • Its conflict is inner (forgetting against feeling, youth against age): compare it with poems of grief and absence.

  • Thus in the winter stands the lonely tree, (line 9) The volta: the extended simile begins.
  • Yet knows its boughs more silent than before: (line 11) Loss felt as absence, not memory.
  • I only know that summer sang in me / A little while, that in me sings no more. (lines 13–14) Past against present: youth has gone.
