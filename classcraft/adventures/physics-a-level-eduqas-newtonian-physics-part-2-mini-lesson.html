Mission 1

Mission 1: A runner finishes a lap exactly where she started. What was her average velocity?

Make a guess: A runner finishes a lap exactly where she started. What was her average velocity?

Mission 1

Position, displacement, distance

  • Position: where it is, measured from an origin along an axis. The sign shows which side.
  • Displacement: the change in position. A vector.
  • Symbol x.
  • Distance: total length of the path. A scalar, never negative.
  • 40 m out, 10 m back: distance 50 m; displacement 30 m outward.
Quick check
?A runner moves along a straight track. Her position x is measured from a marker on the track. She starts at x = +30 m, runs to x = +70 m, then turns around and runs to x = −20 m, where she stops. What is her displacement?
Mission 1

Speed and velocity

  • Average speed = total distance ÷ total time. A scalar.
  • Average velocity = displacement ÷ time. A vector, in the direction of the displacement.
  • v = Δx/Δt
  • Unit: m s⁻¹.
Mission 1

Acceleration

  • Acceleration = rate of change of velocity. Unit: m s⁻².
  • a = Δv/Δt
  • Velocity changes if the speed or the direction changes. A ball that bounces straight back at the same speed has accelerated.
  • a in the same direction as v: speeding up. Opposite: slowing down.
  • A negative acceleration does not always mean slowing down.
Quick check
?An object moves along a straight line, with positive taken to the right. At one instant its velocity is −3.0 m s⁻¹ and its acceleration is +2.0 m s⁻². The acceleration stays constant. Which describes the motion of the object?
Mission 1

Average and instantaneous

  • Average value: over a time interval.
  • Average velocity and average acceleration use only the initial and final states.
  • Average speed uses the whole path: total distance ÷ total time.
  • Instantaneous value: at one instant.
  • The average over a very short interval approximates the instantaneous value.
  • A speedometer shows instantaneous speed.
  • A card of length L passes a light gate in time Δt: v ≈ L ÷ Δt.
  • Uniform acceleration: velocity changes by the same amount every second. Non-uniform: the acceleration itself changes.
Quick check
?The table shows the position x of an object moving along a straight line at four instants. Which value is the best estimate of the object's instantaneous velocity at t = 2.00 s?
Mission 1

Worked example

  • A cyclist rides 600 m north in 120 s, then 200 m south in 40 s.
  • Distance = 600 + 200 = 800 m. Displacement = 600 − 200 = 400 m north.
  • Time = 160 s.
  • Average speed = 800 ÷ 160 = 5.0 m s⁻¹.
  • Average velocity = 400 ÷ 160 = 2.5 m s⁻¹ north.
  • Both legs are at 5.0 m s⁻¹, but the velocity changes: north, then south.
Mission check

?A car travels 3.0 km along a straight road at a constant speed of 10 m s⁻¹, then returns along the same road to its starting point at a constant speed of 15 m s⁻¹. What are its average speed and its average velocity for the whole journey?

Back at the start means zero displacement, so zero average velocity, for the car and for the runner on her lap. Average speed uses total distance ÷ total time: 6000 m ÷ 500 s, not the mean of the two speeds.

Mission 2

Mission 2: On a velocity–time graph, where is the distance covered hiding?

Make a guess: On a velocity–time graph, where is the distance covered hiding?

Mission 2

Displacement–time graphs

  • Displacement–time (x–t) graph.
  • Gradient = velocity.
  • Straight line: constant velocity. Steeper: faster. Horizontal: at rest.
  • Negative gradient: moving in the negative direction.
  • Curve: velocity changing. Gradient of the tangent = instantaneous velocity.
Quick check
?A displacement–time graph for a body moving along a straight line has three straight sections: from (0, 0) to (3.0 s, 12 m); horizontal from 3.0 s to 5.0 s; then from (5.0 s, 12 m) down to (8.0 s, 0). What is the velocity of the body at t = 6.0 s?
Mission 2

Velocity–time graphs

  • Gradient = acceleration. Straight line: uniform acceleration. Curve: non-uniform.
  • Area between the line and the time axis = displacement.
  • Area below the axis: negative displacement (moving backwards).
  • Distance = total area, every part counted as positive.
  • Line crosses the time axis: velocity zero, the object reverses.
  • Its acceleration need not be zero.
Mission 2

Worked example

  • A car: 0 to 12 m s⁻¹ in 4.0 s; steady 12 m s⁻¹ for 6.0 s; then to rest in 3.0 s.
  • Accelerations: 12 ÷ 4.0 = 3.0 m s⁻²; 0; −12 ÷ 3.0 = −4.0 m s⁻².
  • Displacement = area: ½ × 4.0 × 12 + 6.0 × 12 + ½ × 3.0 × 12
  • = 24 + 72 + 18 = 114 m.
  • Average velocity = 114 ÷ 13.0 = 8.8 m s⁻¹.
Quick check
?A car starts from rest at t = 0 and x = 0 and moves along a straight road. A graph of its position x against time t is a curve that gets steeper with time. At t = 3.0 s, x = 16.5 m, and the tangent to the curve at this point also passes through the point (5.0 s, 40.5 m). What is the instantaneous velocity of the car at t = 3.0 s?
Mission 2

Curved velocity–time graphs

  • Area under a curve: count squares. One square = (velocity per square) × (time per square).
  • Count part-squares as halves, or split the area into strips (trapezia).
  • Grid of 1 m s⁻¹ × 1 s squares: 38 squares → about 38 m.
  • Acceleration at a point: draw a long tangent, take its gradient.
Quick check
?A graph of velocity against time for a sprinter starts at the origin, rises steeply, becomes gradually less steep, and is a horizontal line at 11 m s⁻¹ from t = 6.0 s until the end of the race. Which describes the sprinter's motion?
Mission 2

Acceleration–time graphs

  • Area under an a–t graph = change in velocity.
  • Horizontal line: uniform acceleration. Line on the time axis: constant velocity.
  • Final velocity = starting velocity + area. You need the starting velocity.
  • Area below the axis: a negative change in velocity.
Quick check
?The graph shows how the acceleration of a dragster moving along a straight track varies with time. The dragster starts from rest at t = 0. What is the velocity of the dragster at t = 15 s?
Mission check

?A cyclist's velocity–time graph is a straight line from (0, 4.0 m s⁻¹) to (5.0 s, 10 m s⁻¹), then a horizontal line at 10 m s⁻¹ until t = 8.0 s. What is the cyclist's displacement during these 8.0 s?

Distance and displacement hide in the area under a velocity–time graph. Area below the time axis counts as negative displacement but still adds to distance.

Mission 3

Mission 3: A dropped stone falls a distance d in its first second. How far does it fall in the next second?

Make a guess: A dropped stone falls a distance d in its first second. How far does it fall in the next second?

Mission 3

Five quantities, one condition

  • Use these equations only when the acceleration is constant: same size, same direction, along a straight line.
  • x displacement · u initial velocity · v final velocity · a acceleration · t time.
  • Every quantity except t has a direction along the line: pick a positive direction and give each one a sign.
Mission 3

The equations

  • v = u + at (no x)
  • x = ut + ½at² (no v)
  • v² = u² + 2ax (no t)
  • x = ½(u + v)t (no a)
  • Choose the equation that leaves out the quantity you neither know nor want.
Mission 3

Where they come from

  • v–t graph: a straight line from u to v over time t.
  • Gradient = a, so a = (v − u)/t → v = u + at.
  • Area under the line (a trapezium) = displacement → x = ½(u + v)t.
  • Put v = u + at into it → x = ut + ½at².
  • Put t = (v − u)/a into it → v² = u² + 2ax.
Mission 3

Worked example

  • A cyclist moving at 4.0 m s⁻¹ accelerates uniformly at 1.5 m s⁻² for 6.0 s. Find the final velocity and the displacement.
  • Known: u = 4.0 m s⁻¹, a = 1.5 m s⁻², t = 6.0 s. Want: v, x.
  • v = u + at = 4.0 + 1.5 × 6.0 = 13 m s⁻¹
  • x = ut + ½at² = 4.0 × 6.0 + ½ × 1.5 × 6.0² = 24 + 27 = 51 m
  • Check with the third equation: 13² = 4.0² + 2 × 1.5 × 51 → 169 = 169 ✓
Quick check
?A car travelling at 16 m s⁻¹ brakes with uniform deceleration and comes to rest in 4.0 s. What distance does the car travel while braking?
Mission 3

Free fall

  • Free fall: gravity is the only force acting (air resistance negligible).
  • Acceleration = g, downwards, the same for every mass.
  • Take g = 9.81 m s⁻².
  • A ball thrown upwards has acceleration g downwards while rising, at the top and while falling.
  • At the top its velocity is zero; its acceleration is not.
Quick check
?A small wheeled vehicle moves up a straight ramp, slowing down at a steady rate. It turns around at its highest point and then speeds up at the same steady rate as it rolls back down. The direction up the ramp is positive. Which description matches the vehicle's velocity–time graph?
Mission 3

Worked example: thrown upwards

  • A ball is thrown straight up at 12.0 m s⁻¹. How high does it rise, and when does it reach the top?
  • Take up as positive: u = +12.0 m s⁻¹, a = −g. At the top, v = 0.
  • Height: 0 = u² − 2gx → x = u²/2g
  • Time: 0 = u − gt → t = u/g
  • x = 12.0² ÷ (2 × 9.81) = 7.34 m · t = 12.0 ÷ 9.81 = 1.22 s
Mission 3

Measuring g: timing a fall

  • An electromagnet holds a steel ball. Switching it off releases the ball and starts the timer.
  • The ball hits a trapdoor, which stops the timer.
  • Measure the drop height h and the time t. Repeat for 5–6 heights.
  • From rest: h = ½gt². Plot h against t²: a straight line through the origin.
  • Gradient = g/2, so g = 2 × gradient.
Mission 3

Measuring g: light gates

  • Drop a card of known length L from rest, a height h above a light gate.
  • The data logger (computer software) times how long the card blocks the beam, Δt.
  • Speed at the gate: v = L/Δt.
  • From rest: v² = 2gh. Plot v² against h: gradient = 2g.
  • Electronic timing removes human reaction time.
Mission 3

Errors in measuring g

  • Delayed release (the magnet holds the ball briefly): every t is too long → g too small. Systematic.
  • h measured to the wrong point of the ball: the same offset in every h → shifts the intercept, not the gradient. g unaffected.
  • Air resistance: g too small. Use a small, dense ball.
  • Random scatter in t: repeat each height and average; the line of best fit averages the rest.
Quick check
?A student drops a ball from rest from several heights h and times each fall, t. She plots t² (y-axis) against h (x-axis): a straight line through the origin with gradient 0.204 s² m⁻¹. What value of g do the results give?
Mission check

?A toy car is released from rest on a straight slope and speeds up with constant acceleration. It travels 0.60 m in the first 2.0 s. How far does it travel between t = 4.0 s and t = 6.0 s?

A dropped stone does the same. From rest, distance fallen ∝ t², so after T, 2T, 3T it has fallen d, 4d, 9d: the distances in successive equal times go 1 : 3 : 5.

With g = 9.81 m s⁻²: 4.91 m in the first second, then 14.7 m in the next.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Fire a bullet horizontally and drop another at the same instant. Which hits the ground first?

Make a guess: Fire a bullet horizontally and drop another at the same instant. Which hits the ground first?

Mission 4

Two motions, one clock

  • No air resistance: the only force is weight, straight down.
  • Horizontally: no force → constant velocity.
  • Vertically: acceleration g downwards → free fall.
  • The two motions are independent. They share only the time, t.
Mission 4

Launched horizontally

  • A ball rolls off a table 0.80 m high at 2.5 m s⁻¹. Where does it land?
  • Vertical first: it starts with zero vertical velocity. 0.80 = ½gt².
  • t = √(2 × 0.80 ÷ 9.81) = 0.40 s
  • Then horizontal: x = 2.5 × 0.40 = 1.0 m from the table.
  • Recipe: find t from the vertical motion; use it in the horizontal motion.
Quick check
?A ball is kicked horizontally at 12 m s⁻¹ from the top of a vertical cliff 20 m high and lands on level ground below. Fluid resistance is negligible. Take g = 9.81 m s⁻². How far from the foot of the cliff does the ball land?
Mission 4

Launched at an angle

  • Launch at a given speed and angle θ to the horizontal. Resolve at the start:
  • horizontal ux = u cos θ · vertical uy = u sin θ
  • Horizontal component: never changes.
  • Vertical component: changes by g every second, as in free fall.
  • Below the horizontal: the vertical component starts downwards. Watch the sign.
Mission 4

The top and the landing

  • At the highest point: vertical velocity = 0. Velocity = the horizontal component, not zero.
  • Acceleration at the top: still g downwards.
  • Over level ground (no air resistance): the path is symmetrical. Time up = time down.
  • Range = horizontal velocity × total time of flight.
Mission 4

Worked example: a kicked ball

  • A ball is kicked at 18.0 m s⁻¹, 35.0° above level ground. Find the maximum height and the range.
  • Horizontal: 18.0 cos 35.0° = 14.7 m s⁻¹. Vertical: 18.0 sin 35.0° = 10.3 m s⁻¹.
  • Top: vertical velocity 0 → time to top = 10.3/g; height = 10.3²/2g.
  • Landing (level ground): time of flight = 2 × time to top. Range = 14.7 × time of flight.
  • g = 9.81 m s⁻²: top at 1.05 s · height 5.43 m · flight 2.10 s · range 31.0 m
  • Unrounded values carried through; round only at the end.
Quick check
?A ball is launched from level ground at 20 m s⁻¹ at 60° above the horizontal. Fluid resistance is negligible. What is the speed of the ball at its highest point?
Quick check
?A ball is kicked from level ground at 18.0 m s⁻¹ at 50.0° above the horizontal. Fluid resistance is negligible. Take g = 9.81 m s⁻². How long is the ball in the air before it lands on the ground?
Quick check
?Predict first. A small plastic ball is launched without spin from level ground at 30° above the horizontal. The motion is compared with an identical launch in which fluid resistance is negligible. Which describes the effect of fluid resistance on the ball's motion?
Mission 4

With air resistance

  • Air resistance (drag) acts opposite to the velocity and increases with speed.
  • The horizontal velocity component now decreases during the flight.
  • Lower highest point · shorter range · steeper, longer descent: the path is no longer symmetrical.
  • Falling far enough, the body nears its terminal speed: drag balances weight.
  • Balls in sport, such as shuttlecocks and footballs, follow drag-shortened paths.
Mission check

?Ball X is thrown horizontally at 8.0 m s⁻¹ from a height of 5.0 m above level ground. At the same instant, ball Y is dropped from rest from a height of 20 m. Air resistance is negligible. How does the time Y takes to reach the ground compare with the time X takes?

The bullets are the same. Fired horizontally or dropped, each starts with zero vertical velocity and falls with acceleration g, so both hit level ground together (ignoring air resistance). The fired one just lands much farther away.

Mission 5: Push a car and a wheelbarrow equally hard. How much more slowly does the car speed up?

Make a guess: Push a car and a wheelbarrow equally hard. How much more slowly does the car speed up?

Mission 5

Newton's second law

  • F = ma: resultant force = mass × acceleration (constant mass).
  • The acceleration is in the direction of the resultant force, not necessarily the velocity.
  • 1 newton: the resultant force that gives 1 kg an acceleration of 1 m s⁻². 1 N = 1 kg m s⁻².
  • Same force: a ∝ 1/m. Same mass: a ∝ F.
Quick check
?A crate slides to the left across a level floor with negligible friction. At one instant, two students push on it horizontally: one pushes to the left with a force of 20 N, and the other pushes to the right with a force of 30 N. Which statement about the crate at this instant is correct?
Mission 5

Worked example

  • A 1200 kg car: driving force 3000 N forwards, resistive forces 600 N backwards.
  • Resultant force = 3000 − 600 = 2400 N forwards.
  • a = F/m = 2400 ÷ 1200 = 2.0 m s⁻² forwards.
  • From rest, after 5.0 s: v = 0 + 2.0 × 5.0 = 10 m s⁻¹.
  • Always use the resultant force, never just the driving force.
Mission 5

Testing F = ma

  • Trolley on a runway, tilted just enough to compensate for friction. Pulled by a string over a pulley to a hanging mass.
  • Light gates (or a motion sensor) and software give the acceleration.
  • a ∝ F: move masses from the trolley to the hanger. The total mass (trolley + hanger) stays constant; the resultant force on the system, the hanger's weight, changes. a against hanger weight: straight line through the origin.
  • a ∝ 1/m: keep the hanger's weight fixed; add mass to the trolley. Plot a against 1/(total mass of trolley + hanger): straight line through the origin.
Quick check
?A loaded trolley of mass 8.0 kg moves along a straight, level track. The graph shows the trolley's velocity v as a function of time t. What is the magnitude of the resultant force exerted on the trolley between t = 0 and t = 2.0 s?
Mission check

?A student exerts different constant resultant forces on a trolley on a level track and measures the trolley's acceleration each time. The graph shows the results. A block is then fixed to the trolley so that the mass of the trolley–block system is twice the mass of the trolley, and the experiment is repeated with the same resultant forces. Which describes the new graph of acceleration against resultant force?

Same force, a ∝ 1/m. A 1200 kg car has 40 times the mass of a 30 kg wheelbarrow, so the same push (ignoring friction) gives it 1/40 of the acceleration.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Raindrops fall from high clouds. Why don't they hit the ground like bullets?

Make a guess: Raindrops fall from high clouds. Why don't they hit the ground like bullets?

Mission 6

Drag

  • Drag: the resistive force on an object moving through a fluid (a liquid or a gas). Air resistance is drag in air.
  • It acts opposite to the velocity relative to the fluid.
  • It increases with speed.
  • It also grows with cross-sectional area, a less streamlined shape, and the fluid's density.
Mission 6

Falling with drag

  • At release: v = 0, so drag = 0.
  • In air (upthrust negligible), the only force is the weight.
  • Speed rises → drag rises → resultant force (weight − drag) falls.
  • When drag = weight: resultant force zero.
  • The object then falls at a constant terminal velocity.
  • v–t graph: starts as steeply as the no-drag line, then curves and levels off at vT.
Quick check
?A small steel ball is released from rest in air and falls until it reaches terminal velocity. How does its acceleration change during this time?
Mission 6

Worked example: a falling ball

  • A 0.20 kg ball falls. At one instant the drag on it is 0.80 N.
  • Weight = 0.20 × 9.81 = 1.96 N. Resultant = 1.96 − 0.80 = 1.16 N downwards.
  • a = 1.16 ÷ 0.20 = 5.8 m s⁻²: less than g.
  • It keeps speeding up until drag = weight, about 2.0 N. Then a = 0: terminal velocity.
Mission check

?Two paper cones have the same size and shape. A second cone is pushed inside one of them, doubling its mass without changing its outside shape. Both are dropped from a high balcony. Which statement about their terminal velocities is correct?

Drag grows with speed until it balances the weight. Falling raindrops reach this balance at under 10 m s⁻¹, not the 140 m s⁻¹ they would reach falling 1 km without air.

Mission 7: When a truck hits a small car, which one feels the bigger force?

Mission 7

Forces come in pairs

  • If body A exerts a force on body B, then B exerts a force on A that is equal in size and opposite in direction.
  • The two forces in a pair are always:
    • the same type (both gravitational, both contact, …)
    • on different bodies
    • along the same line, at the same time.
Quick check
?A hand pushes block L to the right, and L pushes a smaller block S ahead of it. Both blocks speed up to the right along a level surface with negligible friction. The diagram shows a student's representation of the forces that L and S exert on each other: FL on S is the force exerted on S by L, and FS on L is the force exerted on L by S. Which statement about the student's diagram is correct?
Mission 7

Finding the partner

  • Name the force: "on X by Y". Its partner is "on Y by X".
  • Book on a table. Weight = force on book by Earth → partner: force on Earth by book, upwards.
  • Normal force on book by table → partner: push on table by book, downwards.
  • Weight and normal force on the book are not a pair: both act on the book, and they are different types.
Quick check
?A lamp hangs at rest from a cord fixed to the ceiling. The cord exerts an upward force on the lamp. Which force forms a Newton's third law pair with this force?
Quick check
?A horse pulls a cart along a level road, and both speed up from rest. A student argues: "The cart pulls back on the horse with a force equal in magnitude to the horse's pull on the cart, so these forces cancel and the cart cannot speed up." Which statement correctly identifies the flaw in the argument?
Mission 7

Pairs never cancel

  • The two forces act on different bodies, so they never add to zero on either one.
  • Horse pulls cart; cart pulls back on horse, equally. The cart still speeds up:
    • on the cart: horse's pull > friction on the cart.
    • on the horse: ground's forward push on its hooves > cart's pull back.
  • Equal forces, different masses → different accelerations (a = F/m).
Quick check
?A steel paper clip lies at rest on a wooden table. A magnet held near it pulls it with a small force, but friction from the table stops it moving. Which force forms a Newton's third law pair with the magnet's pull on the clip?
Mission check

?Trolley 1 rolls along a level track and collides with trolley 2. Trolley 1 has three times the mass of trolley 2. Force sensors record the force exerted on trolley 1 by trolley 2 and the force exerted on trolley 2 by trolley 1; the graph shows the data, with the direction of trolley 1's initial motion as positive. Friction is negligible. How does the change in velocity of trolley 2 during the collision compare with that of trolley 1?

Truck and car feel forces of the same size at every instant. The car and its occupants get a much larger acceleration from the same size of force, because the car's mass is much smaller. That is why the car comes off worse.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Distance and speed are scalars; displacement, velocity and acceleration are vectors.
  • Average velocity = displacement ÷ time; average speed = distance ÷ time. Return to the start: average velocity zero.
  • Acceleration = Δv/Δt: a change of speed or of direction. Instantaneous = average over a very short interval.

Mission 2

  • Displacement–time graph: gradient = velocity. v–t graph: gradient = acceleration, area = displacement.
  • a–t graph: area = change in velocity.
  • Curves: tangent for instantaneous values.
  • Curves: count squares for the area.

Mission 3

  • Constant acceleration only: choose the constant-acceleration equation that leaves out the quantity you neither know nor want; give every vector a sign.
  • Free fall: a = g = 9.81 m s⁻², downwards; measure g from the gradient of h against t² (gradient g/2) or v² against h (gradient 2g).

Mission 4

  • Projectile, no air resistance: horizontal velocity constant, vertical acceleration g; the two share only the time t.
  • Resolve the launch velocity; at the top the vertical velocity is zero but the speed is not.
  • Air resistance shortens the range and makes the path asymmetrical.

Mission 5

  • F = ma (constant mass): use the resultant force; the acceleration is in its direction.

Mission 6

  • Drag grows with speed. Falling, the resultant force and acceleration fall to zero when drag = weight: terminal velocity.

Mission 7

  • Third-law pair: same type, equal size, opposite direction, on two different bodies; so the pair never cancels.
