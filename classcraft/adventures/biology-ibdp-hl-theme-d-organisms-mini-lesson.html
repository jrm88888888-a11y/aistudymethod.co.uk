Mission 1

Mission 1: Most flowers have both male and female parts. Why do so many work hard to avoid pollinating themselves?

Make a guess: Most flowers have both male and female parts. Why do so many work hard to avoid pollinating themselves?

Mission 1

Flowers are for sexual reproduction

  • Flowering plants reproduce sexually: meiosis makes haploid cells, these give rise to gametes, and gametes fuse at fertilisation.
  • Male gametes are made inside pollen grains, in the anthers.
  • Female gametes are made inside ovules, in the ovary.
  • A pollen grain is not a gamete: it carries the male gametes.
  • Pollination: transfer of pollen from an anther to a stigma.
  • Pollination is not fertilisation. Fertilisation comes later, inside the ovule.
  • A flower with both male and female parts is still reproducing sexually.
Mission 1

An insect-pollinated flower

  • Sepal: protects the flower in bud.
  • Petal: large, coloured, scented → attracts insects.
  • Nectary: makes sugary nectar, the insect's reward.
  • Stamen (male) = anther (makes pollen) + filament (holds the anther up).
  • Carpel (female) = stigma + style + ovary.
  • Stigma: sticky; pollen lands and germinates here.
  • Style: holds up the stigma; the pollen tube grows down it.
  • Ovary: contains the ovules.
  • Draw it: clear single lines, labels with functions, no shading.
  • Say: "the stigma receives pollen" — not: "the stigma makes pollen".
Quick check
?Which structure of an insect-pollinated flower is correctly paired with its function?
Mission 1

Self- or cross-pollination?

  • Self-pollination: pollen reaches a stigma on the same plant (same flower or another flower).
  • Cross-pollination: pollen reaches a stigma on another plant of the same species.
  • Pollen is carried by animals (insects, birds, bats) or by wind.
  • Self-pollination → self-fertilisation → inbreeding.
  • Inbreeding: offspring more homozygous → harmful recessive alleles already present are expressed more often.
  • Result: lower genetic diversity and lower vigour.
  • Say: "inbreeding exposes harmful recessive alleles" — not: "inbreeding causes mutations".
Mission 1

Ways to promote cross-pollination

  • Different maturation times: anthers shed pollen before the stigma is ready, or the other way round.
  • Separate male and female flowers on one plant (e.g. maize, hazel): no flower can pollinate itself.
  • Separate male and female plants (e.g. holly): every pollination is a cross.
  • Self-incompatibility: genetic; the plant's own pollen fails (next screen).
  • Animals and wind carry pollen between plants.
Quick check
?In one flower species the anthers shed their pollen and wither several days before the stigma of the same flower becomes receptive. How does this promote cross-pollination?
Mission 1

Self-incompatibility: rejecting your own pollen

  • Controlled by a gene with many alleles (S alleles).
  • Pollen lands on a stigma of the same plant.
  • Its S allele matches one in the stigma and style → it is recognised.
  • The pollen fails to germinate, or its pollen tube stops growing.
  • Pollen with a different S allele grows normally.
  • So the gametes that fuse come from different plants → more genetic variation.
  • Say: "genetic recognition after pollen lands" — not: "a barrier that keeps pollen off".
Quick check
?In a self-incompatible plant species, pollen from a flower lands on the stigma of the same flower and germinates, but no seed is set. Which statement about self-incompatibility explains this?
Mission 1

Crossing plants by hand

  • Pea flowers normally self-pollinate before they open.
  • So to cross two plants (the P generation):
  • Remove the anthers from a flower of one plant before they shed pollen.
  • Cover the flower with a bag.
  • Brush pollen from the other plant onto its stigma. Bag and label it.
  • Seeds from this flower → the F1 generation.
  • Let F1 plants self-pollinate → the F2 generation. Predict ratios with a Punnett grid (see Mission 5).
  • Breeders use crosses like this to make new crop and garden varieties.
Quick check
?A breeder wants to cross a tall pea plant with a dwarf pea plant to obtain an F1 generation. Which procedure is required, and why?
Mission check

?A rare wildflower survives as a few plants in one valley. They are self-compatible and mostly self-pollinate. Seeds from self-pollination give many more weak seedlings than seeds from crosses with plants from another valley. What is the best explanation?

That is why so many flowers avoid their own pollen: crossing keeps diversity and vigour high.

Mission 2

Mission 2: A dry bean can sit in a jar for years, then sprout within days of getting wet. What powers that first growth?

Make a guess: A dry bean can sit in a jar for years, then sprout within days of getting wet. What powers that first growth?

Mission 2

From stigma to embryo

  • Inside the anther, meiosis makes haploid pollen grains. Each makes male gametes by mitosis.
  • A compatible pollen grain on the stigma germinates.
  • A pollen tube grows down the style, secreting enzymes to digest a path.
  • It enters the ovule through the micropyle, a small gap in the ovule's coat.
  • It delivers the male gametes.
  • A male gamete fuses with the egg cell (female gamete): fertilisation.
  • The diploid zygote divides by mitosis → embryo.
Mission 2

Ovule → seed; ovary → fruit

  • Zygote → embryo: radicle (young root), plumule (young shoot), cotyledons (seed leaves).
  • Integuments → testa (seed coat). The micropyle stays as a tiny pore.
  • Ovule → seed.
  • Ovary wall → fruit wall (pericarp). Ovary → fruit.
  • The seed dries out and becomes dormant.
  • Say: "the ovule becomes the seed" — not: "the ovary already contains seeds".
Mission 2

Dispersal is not pollination

  • Pollination moves pollen, before fertilisation.
  • Seed dispersal moves seeds, after fertilisation.
  • Why disperse? Less competition with the parent and with each other; new areas colonised.
  • Wind: wings or plumes (e.g. sycamore, dandelion).
  • Animals: fleshy fruits eaten, seeds pass out in faeces; or hooks that catch on fur.
  • Water (e.g. coconut) or explosive pods.
Quick check
?What is the difference between pollination and seed dispersal?
Mission 2

Germination: living on stored food

  • Needs water, oxygen and a suitable temperature.
  • Seed absorbs water through the micropyle; testa softens and splits. Enzymes become active.
  • Embryo releases gibberellin → amylase made.
  • Food reserves mobilised: starch → maltose → glucose; proteins → amino acids; lipids → fatty acids and glycerol.
  • Glucose → aerobic respiration → ATP. Glucose and amino acids → new cells (cellulose, proteins).
  • Radicle emerges first (anchors, absorbs water), then the plumule.
  • Dry mass falls until the first leaves photosynthesise.
Quick check
?A barley grain germinating in dark, moist soil grows a root and a shoot for several days. Where do the energy and materials for this growth come from?
Mission check

?Pea seeds were soaked and grown in complete darkness for ten days. Each day some seedlings were weighed fresh, then dried and weighed again. Fresh mass rose every day; dry mass fell every day. What explains this?

A seed carries its own packed lunch: until its leaves reach the light, every new cell is built from stored food.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Every month the lining of the uterus is shed. What stops that happening once a pregnancy begins?

Make a guess: Every month the lining of the uterus is shed. What stops that happening once a pregnancy begins?

Mission 3

The embryo sends a signal

  • Without pregnancy: the corpus luteum degenerates → progesterone falls → the endometrium is shed.
  • After implantation, the embryo's outer cells secrete hCG.
  • hCG: human chorionic gonadotropin.
  • hCG keeps the corpus luteum alive and secreting progesterone (and oestrogen).
  • Progesterone maintains the endometrium → no menstruation.
  • Say: "hCG is secreted by the embryo" — not: "by the mother's pituitary gland".
Mission 3

Detecting hCG: the pregnancy test

  • Only an embryo or developing placenta makes hCG. Some reaches the urine.
  • The test strip carries monoclonal antibodies that bind specifically to hCG.
  • hCG present → antibodies bind it → a coloured test line. So: pregnant.
  • The full strip mechanism, with its control line, is in Mission 4.
  • Say: "manufactured monoclonal antibodies bind hCG" — not: "the test detects progesterone".
Quick check
?Which statement explains why hCG is needed early in pregnancy?
Mission 3

The hand-over

  • Weeks 1 to about 8: progesterone comes mainly from the corpus luteum, kept alive by hCG.
  • By about weeks 8 to 12: the placenta secretes enough progesterone and oestrogen itself.
  • hCG then falls; the corpus luteum degenerates; the pregnancy continues.
  • Progesterone: maintains the endometrium; keeps the uterus muscle relaxed (inhibits contractions).
  • Say: "progesterone from the corpus luteum, then the placenta" — not: "from the pituitary".
Mission 3Read the data

Hormones across pregnancy

Illustrative data: the shapes follow typical patterns; the levels are relative, not measurements.

  • hCG rises from implantation, peaks at about week 10, then falls to a low, steady level.
  • Progesterone rises all through pregnancy.
  • Its source changes: the corpus luteum share falls after about week 8 as the placenta share rises.
  • Oestrogen also rises to a peak near birth.
  • Read across: the hCG peak comes as the placenta takes over.
Quick check
?On the graph, hCG falls sharply after about week 10, but progesterone keeps rising. Which conclusion is best supported?
Mission 3

The placenta: an exchange organ

  • Villi of foetal tissue project into pools of maternal blood → large surface area.
  • Foetal capillaries run inside each villus. The two bloods are separated by a thin barrier: they do not mix.
  • To the foetus: oxygen, glucose, amino acids, water, ions, some antibodies.
  • To the mother: carbon dioxide, urea.
  • By diffusion (oxygen, carbon dioxide), facilitated diffusion (glucose), active transport (amino acids), endocytosis (antibodies).
  • Not a perfect filter: alcohol, nicotine, some drugs and some viruses cross.
  • The placenta lets the foetus stay in the uterus to a later stage of development. Compare marsupials: a brief, simple placenta, so the young are born tiny and finish developing in a pouch.
Quick check
?A foetus in the uterus has fluid-filled, collapsed lungs, yet its blood is oxygenated. How does oxygen reach the blood of the foetus?
Mission 3

Birth: a positive feedback loop

  • (1) Late in pregnancy, progesterone falls. Its inhibition of contractions is removed.
  • Newer research: in humans, blood progesterone stays high until birth. What falls is its effect: the uterus responds to it less.
  • (2) The posterior pituitary releases oxytocin → the uterus muscle contracts.
  • (3) Contractions push the baby against the cervix. Stretch receptors send impulses to the hypothalamus → more oxytocin.
  • (4) Stronger contractions → more stretch → more oxytocin. Positive feedback.
  • The loop stops when the stimulus stops: the baby is born.
  • Say: "positive feedback amplifies the change" — not: "negative feedback keeps contractions steady".
Quick check
?Which sequence correctly describes the hormonal changes that bring about childbirth?
Mission check

?In a mammal, removing the ovaries (and with them the corpus luteum) early in pregnancy ended the pregnancy, but the same operation late in pregnancy did not. What does this show?

The lining stays because progesterone stays high: first from the corpus luteum, kept alive by the embryo's hCG, then from the placenta.

Mission 4

Mission 4: How can a paper strip pick out one hormone from the thousands of chemicals in a urine sample?

Make a guess: How can a paper strip pick out one hormone from the thousands of chemicals in a urine sample?

Mission 4

One clone, one target

  • An antibody binds an antigen with a complementary shape.
  • Monoclonal antibodies: identical antibodies made by one clone of cells.
  • So every molecule binds the same antigen, and nothing else.
  • Made in the laboratory, in large amounts.
  • They can carry a dye, an enzyme or a drug to one target.
Mission 4

The pregnancy test

  • After implantation, the embryo, then the placenta, secretes hCG (human chorionic gonadotropin). Some reaches the urine.
  • (1) Urine soaks along the strip. Mobile monoclonal antibodies, complementary to hCG and carrying coloured particles, bind any hCG.
  • (2) Test line (T): fixed antibodies to hCG catch the hCG–antibody complexes. A coloured line forms.
  • (3) Control line (C): fixed antibodies that bind the mobile antibodies themselves. A line forms whether or not hCG is present.
  • C and T: pregnant. C only: not pregnant. No C line: the test has failed.
  • Say: "antibodies complementary to hCG" — not: "antibodies that detect pregnancy".
Quick check
?A home pregnancy test strip contains monoclonal antibodies. Which statement explains how the test indicates pregnancy?
Mission check

?A rapid test for virus X has monoclonal antibodies complementary to protein N of virus X, on mobile coloured particles and fixed at the test line. The control line works as in a pregnancy test. A person infected with virus Y, whose protein N has a different shape, uses the test correctly. What result is expected?

One clone, one shape, one target. That is how a strip finds a single hormone in a sample full of other molecules.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Two tall pea plants can produce a short one. Where was the short plant hiding?

Make a guess: Two tall pea plants can produce a short one. Where was the short plant hiding?

Mission 5

Genes and alleles

  • A gene is a length of DNA coding for a polypeptide (or a functional RNA). Its position on a chromosome is its locus.
  • Alleles are different forms of the same gene, at the same locus.
  • Body cells are diploid: two copies of each autosomal gene, one on each homologous chromosome.
  • Genotype: the alleles an organism has, e.g. Tt.
  • Homozygous: two identical alleles (TT or tt). Heterozygous: two different alleles (Tt).
  • Phenotype: the observable traits. It results from the genotype and the environment.
  • Gametes are haploid: one allele of each gene. Fertilisation makes a diploid zygote with one allele from each parent.
  • Say: "two alleles of the gene for height" — not: "a gene for tall and a gene for short".
Mission 5

Dominant and recessive

  • Dominant allele: expressed in the phenotype even when only one copy is present. Capital letter: T.
  • Recessive allele: expressed only when homozygous (tt). Lower-case letter: t.
  • So TT and Tt plants are equally tall.
  • Why? T codes for a functional protein; one copy makes enough of it.
  • t usually codes for a non-functional or absent product. It is still there, and still passed on.
  • Say: "the recessive allele has no effect in the heterozygote" — not: "the dominant allele switches it off".
Quick check
?In peas the allele T for tall stems is dominant to the allele t for dwarf stems, and TT and Tt plants are equally tall. What is the reason that the heterozygote is not shorter than the homozygous dominant plant?
Mission 5

The monohybrid cross

  • P: TT (tall) × tt (dwarf). Gametes: all T; all t. F1: all Tt, all tall.
  • F2: Tt × Tt. Each parent makes T and t gametes in equal numbers.
  • A Punnett grid gives 1 TT : 2 Tt : 1 tt, so 3 tall : 1 dwarf.
  • The two alleles separate into different gametes. That is the law of segregation.
  • The hook: both tall parents were Tt. The short plant is the ¼ that got t from each.
Mission 5

Making a cross in flowering plants

  • Pollen contains the male gametes. The female gametes are in the ovary.
  • Peas make both on the same flower, so they normally self-pollinate and self-fertilise.
  • To cross two plants: remove the anthers from an unopened flower of one plant.
  • Brush pollen from the other plant onto its stigma. Cover the flower with a bag.
  • Plant the seeds: that is the F1. Let F1 plants self-pollinate to get the F2.
  • Breeders use crosses like this to produce new crop and ornamental varieties.

Mission 5

A recessive disorder in a family

  • Some human disorders are caused by a recessive allele of an autosomal gene.
  • Heterozygotes are unaffected carriers. Two carriers can have an affected child.
  • Phenylketonuria (PKU): the gene codes for the enzyme that converts phenylalanine to tyrosine.
  • In pp people the enzyme is missing or does not work, so phenylalanine builds up. Treatment: a low-phenylalanine diet.
  • Carrier × carrier: each child has a ¼ chance of being affected.
  • Each fertilisation is independent. Earlier children do not change the odds.
Quick check
?A man and a woman are both unaffected carriers of the PKU allele. They already have one child with PKU. What is the probability that their next child will have PKU?
Mission 5

The test cross

  • A tall plant could be TT or Tt. You cannot tell by looking.
  • Test cross: cross it with a homozygous recessive (tt) plant.
  • tt passes on only t, which masks nothing. So each offspring shows the allele it got from the tall plant.
  • All tall offspring: the plant is probably TT.
  • Any dwarf offspring: the plant must be Tt. Expect about 1 tall : 1 dwarf.
Quick check
?To find out whether a purple-flowered pea plant is homozygous (PP) or heterozygous (Pp), a breeder crosses it with a white-flowered plant (pp). Why is a white-flowered plant used for this test cross?
Mission check

?In mice, the allele B for black fur is dominant to the allele b for brown fur. A black mouse of unknown genotype is crossed with a brown mouse. Of the 9 offspring, 5 are black and 4 are brown. Which conclusion is correct?

Like the short pea plant in the hook, the brown pups reveal a recessive allele that was there all along, hidden in a heterozygote.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Gametes are made inside pollen grains and ovules; pollination carries pollen to a stigma, and fertilisation follows later.
  • Cross-pollination is promoted by different maturation times, separate male and female flowers or plants, and self-incompatibility; inbreeding exposes harmful recessive alleles and lowers diversity and vigour.

Mission 2

  • A pollen tube delivers male gametes to the ovule; fertilisation gives a zygote that grows into the embryo; the ovule becomes the seed and the ovary the fruit.
  • Pollination moves pollen before fertilisation; dispersal moves seeds after it.
  • Germination mobilises stored food: starch → sugars for respiration and growth, so dry mass falls until leaves photosynthesise.

Mission 3

  • The embryo's hCG keeps the corpus luteum secreting progesterone; the placenta takes over by about weeks 8 to 12, so the endometrium is kept.
  • The placenta's villi give a large surface area for exchange between separate maternal and foetal bloods.
  • Birth: progesterone falls; oxytocin rises by positive feedback as the cervix stretches.

Mission 4

  • Monoclonal antibodies are identical and bind one complementary antigen, so they can find, report or deliver to a single target.

Mission 5

  • Alleles are forms of one gene; genotype + environment → phenotype; one dominant allele is enough for the dominant phenotype.
  • Heterozygote × heterozygote → 3 : 1; a test cross with the homozygous recessive reveals a hidden recessive allele.
