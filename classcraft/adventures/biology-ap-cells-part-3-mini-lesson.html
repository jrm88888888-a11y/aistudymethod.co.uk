Mission 1

Mission 1: Why would a drip of pure water into a patient's vein do harm?

Make a guess: Why would a drip of pure water into a patient's vein do harm?

Mission 1

Osmosis

  • Osmosis: the net movement of water across a partially permeable membrane.
  • The membrane lets water through but not the solute.
  • Water molecules cross randomly both ways; more cross toward the more concentrated solution.
  • Compared with another solution, one is hypotonic (lower solute concentration), hypertonic (higher) or isotonic (equal).
  • Water moves from hypotonic to hypertonic.
Mission 1

Water potential

  • Water potential (Ψ): the tendency of water to move out of a solution.
  • Pure water has Ψ = 0: the highest value.
  • Solutes lower Ψ. Solutions have negative Ψ; more solute, more negative.
  • Units: bars.
  • Water moves from higher (less negative) Ψ to lower (more negative) Ψ.
  • Say: "−3 bars is higher than −9 bars" — not: "−9 bars is the bigger water potential".
  • Same direction: water moves from low to high osmolarity (solute concentration).
Quick check
?A U-tube is divided at the bottom by a membrane that is permeable to water but not to sucrose. Side A contains 0.1 M sucrose and side B contains 0.5 M sucrose. At the start, the liquid levels on the two sides are equal and the temperature is the same throughout. Which statement best describes the net movement of water in the model?
Mission 1

Cells without a wall

  • Outside more dilute than the cytoplasm: water enters. The cell swells and can burst: lysis.
  • Outside more concentrated: water leaves. The cell shrinks and its surface puckers: crenation.
  • These are hypotonic and hypertonic surroundings.
  • Equal concentrations: water still crosses both ways, with no net movement.
  • Freshwater single-celled organisms, such as Paramecium, gain water all the time. A contractile vacuole expels it.
  • Osmoregulation keeps an organism's water balance and controls its internal solute composition.
Mission 1

Cells with a wall

  • Outside more dilute: water enters. The cell contents push on the wall: turgor pressure. The cell is turgid.
  • The wall resists, so the cell does not burst.
  • Outside more concentrated: water leaves; the contents shrink; the membrane pulls away from the wall: plasmolysis.
  • The wall is freely permeable: the gap fills with the outside solution.
  • No turgor pressure: the cell is flaccid.
  • Cell walls of bacteria, archaea, fungi and plants give a structural boundary, block some substances and protect against osmotic lysis.
  • Say: "the wall is freely permeable; the membrane is partially permeable" — not: "the wall keeps water out".

Solution 2 contains sucrose at a concentration that makes its total solute concentration equal to that of the bacterial cytoplasm; sucrose does not cross the plasma membrane of these bacteria. Mission 1

Aquaporins

  • Water crosses the bilayer, but slowly.
  • Aquaporins are channel proteins for water. They let large amounts cross quickly.
  • Evidence: Preston, Agre and colleagues (1992) made frog egg cells produce an aquaporin. In dilute solution these eggs swelled fast and burst; ordinary eggs barely swelled.
  • Cells with many aquaporins: kidney collecting-duct cells, root cells.
Mission 1

Isotonic fluids in medicine

  • A common drip fluid has about the same solute concentration as blood plasma: 0.9% sodium chloride.
  • It is isotonic with plasma.
  • Pure water would enter red blood cells by osmosis. They would swell and burst.
  • A concentrated solution would draw water out of them. They would shrink.
Quick check
?A freshwater protist lives in pond water that is hypotonic to its cytoplasm. The protist has a contractile vacuole that repeatedly fills and then empties to the outside of the cell. Which statement best explains why the protist needs its contractile vacuole?
Mission check

?Jam contains so much sugar that most bacteria landing on it cannot grow. Which statement explains this?

Water follows solute, whether it is entering red blood cells from pure water or leaving bacteria for the jam.

In water potential terms: the jam's Ψ is far lower than the cytoplasm's.

Mission 2: Potato pieces gain mass in some sugar solutions and lose it in others. What does the point in between reveal?

Make a guess: Potato pieces gain mass in some sugar solutions and lose it in others. What does the point in between reveal?

Mission 2

Two parts of water potential

  • Ψ = ΨP + ΨS
  • Solute potential: the effect of dissolved solutes. Zero for pure water; negative when solutes are present.
  • Pressure potential: the effect of pressure. In a plant cell, the wall pressing back on the contents makes it positive.
  • A plasmolyzed cell: pressure potential = 0, so water potential = solute potential.
  • A solution in an open container: ΨP = 0, so Ψ = ΨS.
Mission 2

Worked example: solute potential from concentration

ΨS = −iCRT

  • i = ionization constant (1.0 for sucrose, which does not ionize).
  • C = molar concentration (M); R = 0.0831 L·bar/(mol·K); T = °C + 273.
  • Example: 0.30 M sucrose at 22 °C, in an open beaker.
  • Step 1: T = 22 + 273 = 295 K.
  • Step 2: ΨS = −(1.0)(0.30)(0.0831)(295) = −7.35 ≈ −7.4 bars.
  • Step 3: open beaker, so ΨP = 0 and Ψ = −7.4 bars.
  • More solute, more negative ΨS.
Quick check
?A cell from a hypothetical plant has sucrose as the solute in its cell sap, at a concentration of 0.20 M, at a temperature of 27 °C. Its pressure potential is +2.0 bars. Using Ψ = ΨP + ΨS and ΨS = −iCRT, where R = 0.0831 liter bars/mole K, what is the water potential of the cell?
Mission 2Practical

Water potential of plant tissue

  • Make a dilution series of sucrose from a stock solution: water and 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8.
  • Concentrations in M.
  • Cut equal cylinders with a cork borer; blot; weigh each (or measure its length).
  • Leave in each solution for the same time at the same temperature. Blot the same way; reweigh.
  • % change = (final − initial) ÷ initial × 100. This allows for different starting masses.
  • Repeat at each concentration; use the mean.

The percent change in mass of each bag is measured after 30 minutes. Mission check

Change the solutes in a cell and its water potential, and so the direction of osmosis, changes with them.

Mission 3

Mission 3: A cell keeps taking in a substance it already holds more of than its surroundings. How is that possible?

Make a guess: A cell keeps taking in a substance it already holds more of than its surroundings. How is that possible?

Mission 3

Active transport

  • It needs energy from ATP, made in respiration.
  • It needs a membrane protein that binds the particle.
  • Active transport needs a direct input of energy. In some cases it moves molecules from low to high concentration.
Quick check
?Cells of a hypothetical seaweed accumulate iodide ions (I⁻) to a concentration many times higher than that in the seawater around them. Which statement best explains why the cells must use metabolic energy, such as energy from ATP, to do this?
Mission 3

How do you know it is active?

  • Sign 1: the substance builds up above the outside concentration.
  • Sign 2: uptake falls sharply when the cell's ATP supply falls: no oxygen, or a respiratory inhibitor.
  • Diffusion is not affected by respiratory inhibitors. That is the test.
  • Ions leak back down their gradient. So a gradient needs a continuing energy supply, not a one-off.
  • Metabolic energy is needed to establish and maintain electrochemical gradients.

  • A steady level is not zero movement: uptake was balancing leakage.

?At 30 minutes, a drug that stops the cells from making ATP was added. Which claim is best supported by the data in the table?
Mission 3

The sodium–potassium pump

  • One carrier, two ions, both moved against their gradients.
  • Each cycle, one ATP: 3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in.
  • Called the Na⁺/K⁺ pump or Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase.
  • Result: Na⁺ is low inside the cell; K⁺ is high.
  • One more positive charge leaves than enters, so the inside stays negative: the pump helps generate the membrane potential.
  • The pump keeps up the Na⁺ and K⁺ gradients that the rest of the potential depends on.
  • Say: "3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in" — not: "Na⁺ in, K⁺ out", nor "one for one".
Quick check
?In a hypothetical animal cell, a drug blocks the Na⁺/K⁺ pump (Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase) but has no direct effect on any ion channel. Which prediction about the cell during the following hour is best supported?
Mission 3

Bulk transport: vesicles

  • Large molecules and particles cannot cross the bilayer. They move in vesicles.
  • Endocytosis (in): the membrane folds inward around the material and pinches off a vesicle.
  • Exocytosis (out): a vesicle moves to the membrane, fuses with it and releases its contents outside. E.g. digestive enzymes, hormones, neurotransmitters.
  • Both need ATP: to reshape membrane and move vesicles.
  • Both use energy to move large substances or large amounts of substances.
  • Say: "endocytosis and exocytosis need ATP" — not: "they are passive because no pump is involved".
Quick check
?Cells of a hypothetical gland make a digestive enzyme, a large protein, and package it into vesicles inside the cell. A chemical is added that blocks exocytosis in these cells but does not affect how the enzyme is made or packaged. Which prediction about the cells is best supported?
Mission check

?Red blood cells hold K⁺ at a much higher concentration than the plasma around them. In donated blood stored at 4 °C, the cells slowly lose K⁺. When the cells are warmed to 37 °C with glucose, they take K⁺ back up to the original high level. Which explanation fits these observations?

A gradient against the odds is never free. Stop the energy supply and it runs down; restore it and the pumps rebuild it.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Earth began hot and lifeless. What had to come together before the very first cell could exist?

Make a guess: Earth began hot and lifeless. What had to come together before the very first cell could exist?

Mission 4

Early Earth

  • Earth formed about 4.6 billion years ago (bya).
  • No free oxygen in the atmosphere.
  • No oxygen → no ozone layer → intense ultraviolet light reached the surface.
  • Volcanoes and lightning supplied energy. Carbon compounds could form spontaneously, by reactions that do not happen today.
  • Geological evidence gives the window:
  • Too hostile for life until about 3.9 bya.
  • Earliest fossil evidence of life: about 3.5 bya.
  • So life arose between about 3.9 and 3.5 bya.
Quick check
?Earth formed about 4.6 billion years ago (bya), and O2 began to accumulate in its atmosphere about 2.4 bya. According to the geological and fossil evidence, about when did Earth's environment first become able to support life?
Mission 4

The RNA world

  • Puzzle: today, DNA needs protein enzymes to copy it, and proteins need DNA to code for them. Which came first?
  • Proposal: RNA came first, before DNA and before protein enzymes.
  • RNA can store information in its base sequence and be copied by base pairing.
  • Some RNA molecules are catalysts: ribozymes.
  • So RNA could have been both the genes and the enzymes of the earliest cells.
  • Still seen today: in the ribosome, rRNA catalyzes peptide bond formation.
  • The RNA world hypothesis makes three assumptions:
  • (1) At some point, genetic continuity was assured by the replication of RNA.
  • (2) Base pairing is necessary for replication.
  • (3) Genetically encoded proteins were not involved as catalysts.
Quick check
?The RNA world hypothesis proposes that RNA could have been the earliest genetic material. Which property of RNA best explains why RNA, rather than another molecule, is proposed?
Mission check

?In a hypothetical early organism of the kind the RNA world hypothesis proposes, one RNA molecule both carries the organism's genetic information and acts as a catalyst, and copies of it are made by base pairing. A copying error changes one base in this RNA. Which prediction is best supported?

A molecule that both carries information and catalyzes could start life's chain of copying, with changes passed on; that is the case for an RNA world after about 3.9 bya.

Mission 5: Your cells hold the descendants of bacteria taken in long ago. How can anyone know that?

Mission 5

How do we date the first cells?

  • Radiometric dating of rocks: long-lived isotopes (e.g. uranium-238) decay at known rates.
  • Fossils in dated rock: microfossils and stromatolites (layered mats made by bacteria).
  • A fossil gives a minimum age: life is at least that old.
  • Not carbon-14: it is useless beyond about 50 000 years.

Mission 5

Bacteria made the oxygen

  • Photosynthesis: CO2 + H2O + light energy → carbohydrates + O2.
  • It first evolved in prokaryotes.
  • Rock evidence: free O2 first builds up about 2.4 bya, when cyanobacteria existed but no eukaryotes did.
  • So cyanobacterial photosynthesis produced Earth's oxygenated atmosphere.
  • Prokaryotic photosynthetic pathways became the foundation of photosynthesis in eukaryotes (next screen).
Quick check
?A student claims that photosynthesis by cyanobacteria, which are prokaryotes, produced the oxygenated atmosphere of early Earth. Which of the following provides the best support for this claim?
Mission 5

Endosymbiosis

  • Start: an early cell with internal membranes.
  • (1) It took in an aerobic prokaryote by endocytosis. Not digested: it lived on inside.
  • (2) Over generations it became the mitochondrion. All eukaryotes descend from this cell.
  • (3) Later, in some lineages only, a cell that already had mitochondria took in a photosynthetic cyanobacterium: the chloroplast.
  • So plants and algae have both; animals and fungi have mitochondria only.
  • Endosymbiosis: one organism living inside another, to mutual benefit.
Mission 5

The evidence inside the organelles

Mitochondria and chloroplasts are like bacteria, not like the rest of the cell:

  • Their own circular, naked DNA.
  • Their own ribosomes, which resemble bacterial ribosomes.
  • They divide by splitting in two; a cell cannot make new ones from scratch.
  • A double membrane: the inner one was the prokaryote's own membrane.
  • Their rRNA gene sequences are most similar to those of bacteria (mitochondria) and of cyanobacteria (chloroplasts).
  • A theory is strong when it explains many observations and makes predictions that are confirmed. Endosymbiosis does both.
  • Say: "shared features show shared ancestry" — not: "they do the same job as bacteria, so they came from bacteria".
Quick check
?Which observation provides the strongest evidence for the claim that mitochondria evolved from once free-living prokaryotic cells?
Mission 5

One ancestor for all eukaryotes

  • Evidence that all eukaryotes share a common ancestor:
  • Membrane-bound organelles (nucleus, mitochondria, ER, Golgi).
  • Linear chromosomes.
  • Genes that contain introns.
  • Most bacteria have none of these three.
  • A feature shared by every eukaryote is best explained by inheritance from that ancestor.
Quick check
?Biologists discover a single-celled organism whose cells contain a nucleus, mitochondria and an endoplasmic reticulum, each enclosed by membranes. Which conclusion is best supported?
Mission check

?A researcher hypothesizes that the ribosomes inside mitochondria resemble bacterial ribosomes, whereas the ribosomes in the cytosol of the same eukaryotic cell do not. An antibiotic blocks protein synthesis by bacterial ribosomes and does not affect ribosomes that differ from them. If the hypothesis is correct, which result is predicted when the antibiotic is added to eukaryotic cells?

No one watched it happen, but the theory makes predictions like this one, and they hold: the organelles still behave like the bacteria they came from.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Osmosis: net movement of water across a partially permeable membrane into the more concentrated solution; cells without walls burst or shrink, cells with walls become turgid or plasmolyzed.
  • Water moves from higher to lower water potential; pure water has Ψ = 0 and solutes make Ψ negative.

Mission 2

  • Water potential = solute potential + pressure potential; solute potential is zero or negative, pressure potential in a turgid cell is positive.
  • ΨS = −iCRT: more solute, more negative.

Mission 3

  • Active transport uses carrier (pump) proteins and ATP to move specific particles against their concentration gradient; it stops when the ATP supply stops.
  • Endocytosis and exocytosis move large substances in vesicles and need energy.

Mission 4

  • RNA can carry information and catalyze, so it is the presumed first genetic material.
  • Earth formed about 4.6 bya; hostile until about 3.9 bya; earliest fossils about 3.5 bya.

Mission 5

  • Cyanobacterial photosynthesis oxygenated the atmosphere; membrane-bound organelles, linear chromosomes and introns show eukaryotes share one ancestor.
  • Mitochondria, then (in some lineages) chloroplasts, arose by endosymbiosis: their circular DNA, bacteria-like ribosomes, division and double membranes are the evidence.
