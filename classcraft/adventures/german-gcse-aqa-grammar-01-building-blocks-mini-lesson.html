The building blocks

Before any tense or clever structure, German rests on a few foundations: every noun has a gender (der / die / das), the article changes when the noun becomes the object (the accusative), plurals nearly all take die, the verb sits in second position, and es gibt means "there is/are". Master these and every later lesson clicks.

Every noun has a gender

der, die, das — the word for "the"

German nouns are always written with a capital letter and belong to one of three genders. The word for "the" tells you which:

gender"the" (nominative)example
masculinederder Bruder — the brother
femininediedie Schwester — the sister
neuterdasdas Kind — the child

Learn the gender WITH the word. There is no reliable way to guess it from meaning — das Mädchen ("the girl") is neuter, not feminine. Always store a noun as der/die/das + noun, never the noun alone.

"a / an"

ein and eine

"A / an" is ein for masculine and neuter, and eine for feminine. This ein- family also covers kein ("not a / no") and the possessives (mein, dein…) — they all take the same endings.

gender"a" (nominative)example
masculineeinein Hund — a dog
feminineeineeine Katze — a cat
neutereinein Haus — a house

Notice: masculine and neuter look identical in the nominative (ein). That changes in the accusative — coming up in a moment.

More than one

Plurals — always "die"

In the plural, every gender uses die. The tricky part is the noun ending, which varies — often with an umlaut (ä/ö/ü). Learn each plural with its noun:

singularpluralpattern
der Hunddie Hundeadd -e
die Schwesterdie Schwesternadd -n
das Kinddie Kinderadd -er
der Bruderdie Brüderumlaut
das Autodie Autosadd -s

Umlaut alert: keep the two dots exactly — Bruder → Brüder, Apfel → Äpfel, Stadt → Städte. Never write Brueder or Aepfel: the umlaut ü/ä is a different letter from ue/ae.

Who does what?

Nominative vs accusative

German marks the job of a noun with its case. The subject (doer) is nominative; the direct object (thing the action lands on) is accusative:

Der Hund beißt den Mann.subject (nom.) — verb — object (acc.)
  • Der Hund = the one doing the biting → nominative.
  • den Mann = the one being bitten → accusative.

Word order does NOT decide the meaning the way it does in English — the case does. That is why the article endings matter so much.

The masculine trick

Only the masculine changes: der → den

Here is the good news about the accusative: only the masculine article changes. Feminine, neuter and plural look exactly the same as the nominative.

gendernominativeaccusative
masculineder / einden / einen
femininedie / einedie / eine
neuterdas / eindas / ein
pluraldiedie
  • Ich habe einen Bruder. — I have a brother. (masc. → einen)
  • Ich habe eine Schwester. — I have a sister. (fem. → unchanged)
  • Ich esse den Apfel. — I eat the apple. (masc. → den)

The classic mistake: Ich habe ein Bruder. After haben the brother is the object, so masculine ein must become einen.

The verb rule

The verb comes second

In a normal German main clause the finite verb is the second idea — not necessarily the second word, but the second "slot". Whatever you put first, the verb still follows it:

  • Ich spiele heute Fußball. — I play football today.
  • Heute spiele ich Fußball. — Today I play football. (verb still 2nd; subject moves behind it)
  • Am Wochenende sehe ich einen Film. — At the weekend I watch a film.

Inversion: when a time phrase or other element starts the sentence, the subject jumps to just after the verb. English keeps "I play"; German flips to "spiele ich". You'll drill this fully in cluster 4.

"there is / there are"

es gibt + accusative

One handy phrase covers both "there is" and "there are": es gibt. It literally means "it gives", and — importantly — it is always followed by the accusative:

  • In meiner Stadt gibt es einen Park. — There's a park. (masc. → einen)
  • Es gibt eine Schule. — There's a school.
  • Es gibt ein Kino. — There's a cinema. (neuter → ein)
  • Es gibt viele Geschäfte. — There are many shops.

Remember the accusative: because a park is the object of es gibt, masculine ein Park becomes einen Park.

Saying "no / not a"

kein — the negative "a"

To say "not a / no / not any", use kein. It takes the same endings as ein, so it also changes to keinen for a masculine object:

  • Ich habe keinen Bruder. — I have no brother. (masc. object → keinen)
  • Ich habe keine Schwester. — I have no sister.
  • Ich habe kein Haustier. — I have no pet. (neuter)

ein has no plural, but kein does: Ich habe keine Geschwister — "I have no siblings". Use kein to negate a noun, and nicht to negate a verb or the rest of a sentence.

Recap

Your building-block toolkit

Genders: every noun is der (m), die (f) or das (n) — learn the article with the word; nouns are always capitalised.

"a": ein (m/n), eine (f); kein = "not a" and takes the same endings.

Plurals: all take die; endings vary (-e, -n, -er, -s) and often add an umlaut (Bruder → Brüder).

Cases: subject = nominative, object = accusative. Only the masculine changes: der → den, ein → einen.

Word order: the finite verb is the second element; a fronted phrase pushes the subject after the verb.

es gibt = "there is/are" and always takes the accusative.
