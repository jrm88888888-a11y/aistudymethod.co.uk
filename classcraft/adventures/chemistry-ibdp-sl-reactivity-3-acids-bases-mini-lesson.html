Mission 1

Mission 1: Is water an acid or a base?

Make a guess: Is water an acid or a base?

Mission 1

Acids give protons, bases take them

  • A Brønsted–Lowry acid is a proton (H⁺) donor.
  • A Brønsted–Lowry base accepts a proton, using a lone pair.
  • HNO₃ + NH₃ → NH₄⁺ + NO₃⁻: HNO₃ donates H⁺; NH₃ accepts it.
  • H⁺(aq) and H₃O⁺(aq) are two ways of writing the same hydrated proton.
Mission 1

Base or alkali?

  • An alkali is a base that dissolves in water, releasing OH⁻(aq): NaOH, KOH, NH₃(aq).
  • Every alkali is a base; not every base is an alkali.
  • Iron(III) oxide neutralizes acids, so it is a base. It is insoluble, so not an alkali.
  • A base need not contain OH: NH₃, O²⁻ and CO₃²⁻ accept H⁺ with a lone pair.

Mission 1

Conjugate acid–base pairs

  • A conjugate pair differs by exactly one proton.
  • Conjugate base: remove one H⁺; the charge falls by one. HSO₄⁻ → SO₄²⁻.
  • Conjugate acid: add one H⁺; the charge rises by one. NH₃ → NH₄⁺.
  • Every proton transfer has two pairs: HNO₃/NO₃⁻ and NH₄⁺/NH₃.
  • The acid and base that react are not a pair; each partner is across the arrow.

Mission 1

Amphiprotic species

  • Amphiprotic: can act as a Brønsted–Lowry acid or base, depending on its partner.
  • It needs an H it can lose and a lone pair to accept H⁺.
  • Examples: H₂O, HSO₄⁻, H₂PO₄⁻, HPO₄²⁻.
  • In any one reaction it plays one role only.
Mission 1

Writing both equations

  • Water as a base: HNO₃ + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + NO₃⁻.
  • Water as an acid: NH₃ + H₂O ⇌ NH₄⁺ + OH⁻.
  • HPO₄²⁻ as a base: HPO₄²⁻ + H₃O⁺ ⇌ H₂PO₄⁻ + H₂O.
  • HPO₄²⁻ as an acid: HPO₄²⁻ + OH⁻ ⇌ PO₄³⁻ + H₂O.
  • Check: as a base it gains one H; as an acid it loses one. Charges balance.

Make a guess: Lemon juice is about pH 2; black coffee is about pH 5. How many times more H⁺ is in the lemon juice?

Mission 2

What pH measures

  • pH = −log₁₀[H⁺], with [H⁺] in mol dm⁻³. pH has no units.
  • Rearranged: [H⁺] = 10⁻ᵖᴴ. Only the pH form is printed in the data booklet.
  • Lower pH means higher [H⁺].
  • One pH unit is a tenfold change in [H⁺]; two units, a hundredfold.
  • Very concentrated solutions can have pH below 0 or above 14.
Mission 2

Non-integer pH: worked examples

  • [H⁺] = 3.2 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³: pH = −log₁₀(3.2 × 10⁻⁴) = 3.49.
  • pH 4.60: [H⁺] = 10⁻⁴·⁶⁰ = 2.5 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³.
  • Use the log key (base 10), not ln.
  • Sense check: pH 3.49 lies between 3 and 4, so [H⁺] lies between 10⁻⁴ and 10⁻³.

Mission 2

Measuring pH

  • Universal indicator: match its colour to a chart to estimate pH, to about 1 unit.
  • pH meter (probe): a precise reading, usually to 0.01.
  • Calibrate the meter first with buffer solutions of known pH.
  • Rinse the probe with distilled water between samples.
Quick check

Mission 2

Water ionizes, a little: Kw

  • Water ionizes slightly: H₂O(l) ⇌ H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq).
  • Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻] = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 298 K (data booklet).
  • Every aqueous solution contains both H⁺ and OH⁻.
  • Acidic: [H⁺] > [OH⁻]. Neutral: [H⁺] = [OH⁻]. Basic: [H⁺] < [OH⁻].
  • At 298 K, neutral means both are 1.00 × 10⁻⁷ mol dm⁻³: pH 7.00.
  • Kw changes with temperature, so neutral is pH 7.00 only at 298 K.
Mission 2

Using Kw: worked example

  • The product is fixed, so [H⁺] up tenfold means [OH⁻] down tenfold.
  • [OH⁻] = Kw ÷ [H⁺] and [H⁺] = Kw ÷ [OH⁻].
  • 0.020 mol dm⁻³ KOH is fully ionized: [OH⁻] = 0.020 mol dm⁻³.
  • [H⁺] = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ ÷ 0.020 = 5.0 × 10⁻¹³ mol dm⁻³; pH = 12.30.

Mission 3: Can a strong acid have a higher pH than a weak acid?

Make a guess: Can a strong acid have a higher pH than a weak acid?

Mission 3

Strong and weak

  • Strong acids and bases ionize completely in water.
  • Strong acids: HCl, HBr, HI, HNO₃, H₂SO₄. Strong bases: group 1 hydroxides.
  • Weak acids and bases ionize only partly: the equilibrium lies to the left.
  • Weak: carboxylic acids such as HCOOH, carbonic acid, NH₃ and amines.
  • HCOOH(aq) ⇌ HCOO⁻(aq) + H⁺(aq). NH₃(aq) + H₂O(l) ⇌ NH₄⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq).
Mission 3

Strength is not concentration

  • Concentration: amount of acid per dm³. Strength: the extent of ionization.
  • Diluting a strong acid raises its pH, but it stays strong.
  • At equal concentration, a weak acid has a higher pH and lower conductivity than a strong one.
  • It still neutralizes the same amount of base: as H⁺ is used up, more of the acid ionizes.
Quick check

Quick check
?Solution X is 0.0010 mol dm⁻³ KOH(aq). Solution Y is 2.0 mol dm⁻³ NH₃(aq). Which statement is correct?
Mission 3

Which way does the equilibrium lie?

  • An acid–base equilibrium lies on the side of the weaker acid and weaker base.
  • The stronger the acid, the weaker its conjugate base.
  • HNO₃ + H₂O → H₃O⁺ + NO₃⁻ is effectively complete: NO₃⁻ barely accepts H⁺.
  • HCOOH + H₂O ⇌ HCOO⁻ + H₃O⁺ lies left: H₃O⁺ is a stronger acid than HCOOH.

Mission 4

Oxides and hydroxides: salt + water

  • In neutralization, a base accepts the H⁺ ions from an acid.
  • Acid + metal hydroxide → salt + water: KOH + HNO₃ → KNO₃ + H₂O.
  • Acid + metal oxide → salt + water: Fe₂O₃ + 6HCl → 2FeCl₃ + 3H₂O.
  • Count the protons: each O²⁻ takes 2H⁺, so Fe₂O₃ needs 6 HCl.
  • A salt forms when an acid's H is replaced by a metal ion or another cation, such as NH₄⁺.
Mission 4

Carbonates and hydrogencarbonates: CO₂ too

  • Acid + carbonate or hydrogencarbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide.
  • CO₃²⁻ accepts two H⁺; HCO₃⁻ accepts one.
  • K₂CO₃ + 2HNO₃ → 2KNO₃ + H₂O + CO₂.
  • KHCO₃ + HNO₃ → KNO₃ + H₂O + CO₂.
  • The fizzing is the CO₂ escaping.
Mission 4

Ammonia, amines and organic acids

  • Ammonia or an amine + acid → a salt only; no water forms.
  • NH₃ + HNO₃ → NH₄NO₃. CH₃CH₂NH₂ + HCl → CH₃CH₂NH₃Cl.
  • The N lone pair takes the H⁺; there is no OH⁻ or O²⁻ to make water.
  • Weak organic acids react in the same ways: 2HCOOH + K₂CO₃ → 2HCOOK + H₂O + CO₂.
  • Metal + acid gives hydrogen. That is redox, not neutralization: see the redox lesson.

Mission 4

The parent acid and base of a salt

  • A salt's anion comes from its parent acid; its cation from its parent base.
  • KNO₃: parent acid HNO₃, parent base KOH.
  • (NH₄)₂SO₄: parent acid H₂SO₄, parent base NH₃.
  • HCOOK (potassium methanoate): parent acid HCOOH, parent base KOH.
  • Parents are the neutral acid and base, not the ions in the salt.

Mission 4

The pH curve: strong acid, strong base

  • Flask: 25.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ HCl. Burette: 0.100 mol dm⁻³ NaOH.
  • Intercept: the pH of the flask solution before any base is added, 1.00.
  • pH rises slowly, then jumps from about 3 to about 11 near 25.0 cm³.
  • Equivalence point: amounts match the equation. Here it is pH 7 (at 298 K), mid-jump.
  • After it, pH rises slowly towards 13 (about 12.5 by 50 cm³), as excess NaOH builds up.
Mission 4

Why the jump is so steep

  • pH is a log scale. Early on, most of the H⁺ is still there, so pH barely moves.
  • At 24.0 cm³, 1/25 of the H⁺ is left, in 49.0 cm³: pH about 2.7.
  • At 26.0 cm³, a little OH⁻ is in excess: pH about 11.3.
  • So 2 cm³ moves the pH by about 8.6 units: the near-vertical section.

Recap

The big ideas

  • Acids donate H⁺; bases accept it with a lone pair. Conjugate pairs differ by one H⁺.
  • Amphiprotic species, such as water, act as acid or base: one role per reaction.
  • pH = −log₁₀[H⁺]: one unit is tenfold. Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻] = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁴ at 298 K.
  • Strong means fully ionized; weak, partly. Strength is not concentration.
  • Oxides and hydroxides give salt + water; carbonates add CO₂; ammonia and amines give a salt only.
  • Strong acid–strong base curve: starts at the flask's pH, jumps steeply, equivalence at pH 7 (298 K).
