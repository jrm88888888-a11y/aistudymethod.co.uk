Mission 1

Mission 1: A discharging capacitor never quite empties. So what sets how fast it gets close?

Make a guess: A discharging capacitor never quite empties. So what sets how fast it gets close?

Mission 1

Charging, step by step

  • Cell (emf E), resistor R and uncharged capacitor C in series. Close the switch.
  • At the start: VC = 0, so the whole emf is across R. The current is largest: I0 = E/R.
  • Electrons pile onto one plate and leave the other. VC rises.
  • p.d. across R = E − VC falls, so the current falls, so charging slows.
  • After a long time: VC = E, current zero. Fully charged.
Mission 1

Discharging

  • Now connect the charged capacitor across R alone.
  • The capacitor's p.d. drives the current: I = VC/R. Electrons flow back to the plate they left. The current is reversed compared with charging.
  • Charge leaves → VC falls → current falls → charge leaves more slowly.
  • The rate of loss is proportional to the charge left. That is exponential decay.
  • It gets ever closer to zero but, in this model, never reaches it.
Mission 1

The graphs

  • Charging: Q and VC rise steeply, then level off at the final value. Current starts at I0 and decays to zero.
  • Discharging: Q, VC and I all decay with the same shape.
  • Q and VC always have the same shape, because Q = CVC.
  • Gradient of a Q–t graph = current.
  • Area under an I–t graph = charge that has flowed.
Quick check
?A capacitor is charged through a resistor by a battery. At time t1 the p.d. across the capacitor is one quarter of the battery's emf. At a later time t2 it is three quarters of the emf. What is the current at t2 divided by the current at t1?
Mission 1

The time constant

  • Time constant τ = RC. Unit: Ω × F = s.
  • Discharging: after one τ, 37% of the starting charge is left.
  • Charging: after one τ, the charge has reached 63% of its final value.
  • After about 5τ: over 99% of the way. Treat as finished.
  • Bigger R → smaller current → slower. Bigger C → more charge to move → slower.
  • The starting p.d. does not change τ.
Mission 1

The equations

  • Discharging: Q = Q0e−t/RC. The same form gives V = V0e−t/RC and I = I0e−t/RC.
  • Charging: Q = Q0(1 − e−t/RC), where Q0 is the final charge. V follows the same form; I still decays: I = I0e−t/RC.
  • At t = RC: e⁻¹ = 0.37, so 37% left; 1 − e⁻¹ = 0.63 reached.
Mission 1

Worked example

  • A 470 μF capacitor, charged to 9.0 V, discharges through 10 kΩ.
  • τ = RC = 10 × 10³ × 470 × 10⁻⁶ = 4.7 s.
  • Initial current: I0 = 9.0 ÷ 10 × 10³ = 0.90 mA.
  • After 5.0 s: V = 9.0 × e−5.0/4.7 = 3.1 V.
  • Time to fall to 1.0 V: e−t/4.7 = 1.0/9.0, so t = 4.7 × ln 9.0 = 10 s.
Quick check
?An uncharged capacitor is charged through a 3.0 kΩ resistor by an ideal battery of emf 9.0 V, all connected in series. What is the current in the resistor at the instant when the potential difference across the capacitor is 6.0 V?
Quick check
?A 50 μF capacitor is charged to 12 V and then discharged through a 200 kΩ resistor. What is the potential difference across the capacitor 15 s after the discharge starts?
Mission check

?An uncharged capacitor is charged through a resistor by a battery. Its charge takes a time t to reach 63% of its final value. The capacitor is emptied. The battery is replaced by one with twice the emf, and a second, identical resistor is added in series with the first. How long does the charge now take to reach 63% of its new final value?

The pace is set by RC alone: the time constant. Every RC, the charge falls to 37% of what it was, so it gets close to empty but never quite there.

Mission 2

Mission 2: How can a straight-line graph reveal the time constant of a curve that never reaches zero?

Make a guess: How can a straight-line graph reveal the time constant of a curve that never reaches zero?

Mission 2

Equal times, equal fractions

  • Exponential decay: in equal time intervals, the charge falls by the same fraction.
  • Every RC: × 0.37. Every 0.69RC: × ½.
  • Test for exponential: read V at equal intervals. Equal ratios V1/V0 = V2/V1 = … → exponential.
  • Example: 12.0 V, 8.0 V, 5.4 V, 3.6 V every 2.0 s: each ratio ≈ 0.67.
Mission 2

A straight line from a curve

  • Take natural logs of V = V0e−t/RC: ln V = ln V0 − t/RC.
  • Compare with y = c + mx. Plot ln V against t.
  • Straight line → the decay is exponential.
  • Gradient = −1/RC. Intercept = ln V0.
  • Logs need a pure number: plot ln V with V in volts (the log of the number of volts).
Mission 2

Worked example

  • A capacitor discharges through 100 kΩ. A graph of ln V (V in volts) against t is a straight line.
  • Gradient = −0.20 s⁻¹. Intercept = 2.48.
  • Time constant = 1 ÷ 0.20 = 5.0 s.
  • C = time constant ÷ R = 5.0 ÷ (100 × 10³) = 5.0 × 10⁻⁵ F (50 μF).
  • V0 = e2.48 = 12 V.
Quick check
?A student plots ln(I/A) against t for a capacitor discharging through a 20 kΩ resistor. The graph is a straight line of gradient −0.025 s⁻¹. What is the capacitance?
Mission 2

The practical

  • Circuit: d.c. supply, two-way switch, capacitor, resistor. Voltmeter (or data logger) across the capacitor, or an ammeter in series with R.
  • Charge fully. Switch to discharge and start timing.
  • Slow decay (time constant about a minute): read V every 10 s by hand.
  • Fast decay: use a data logger or an oscilloscope.
  • Plot ln V against t; time constant = −1/gradient.
  • A voltmeter across the capacitor is a second discharge path: use a very high-resistance meter.
  • Capacitor values have wide tolerances (often ±20%): expect the time constant to differ from the value worked out from the labels.
  • Specified practical: charging and discharging to determine the time constant.
Quick check
?A student measures the time constant of a 10 μF capacitor discharging through a 1.0 MΩ resistor, so the time constant should be 10 s. A voltmeter of resistance 10 MΩ stays connected across the capacitor throughout. How does the measured time constant compare with 10 s?
Mission check

?A student plots ln I (I in amperes) against t for a discharging capacitor. The best-fit straight line passes through ln I = −6.20 at t = 0 and ln I = −7.70 at t = 30 s. How long does the current take to fall to half of any value?

ln V (or ln I, ln Q) against t is a straight line of gradient −1/(time constant). The curve never reaches zero, but the line gives the time constant directly.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why are the markings on a spring weighing scale evenly spaced?

Make a guess: Why are the markings on a spring weighing scale evenly spaced?

Mission 3

Hooke's law

  • Stretch a spring: the force it exerts is proportional to its extension.
  • This holds only up to a limit.
  • Extension = stretched length − original (natural) length. Not the whole length.
  • F = kx. k is the spring constant: force per unit extension.
  • Unit of k: N m⁻¹. Larger k: stiffer spring.
  • Works for compression too: the extension is then the decrease in length.
Quick check
?In a lab, a student models a spring as a light spring that obeys Hooke's law. Which statement describes how the spring's force behaves in this model?
Mission 3

Worked example

  • A spring hangs with natural length 12.0 cm. A 4.0 N weight stretches it to 17.0 cm.
  • Extension = 17.0 − 12.0 = 5.0 cm = 0.050 m.
  • k = force ÷ extension = 4.0 ÷ 0.050 = 80 N m⁻¹.
  • Hang 6.0 N instead: extension = 6.0 ÷ 80 = 0.075 m = 7.5 cm.
  • New length = 12.0 + 7.5 = 19.5 cm. Always add the extension back to the natural length.
Mission 3

The force–extension graph

  • Plot force against extension. Hooke's law: a straight line through the origin.
  • Gradient = k.
  • Beyond the limit of proportionality the line curves.
  • Below the elastic limit: remove the load and it returns to its original length.
  • Beyond the elastic limit: it is permanently stretched.
Quick check
?The graph shows the magnitude F of the force exerted by a light spring that obeys Hooke's law, as a function of the spring's length L, from its natural length to a length of 20 cm. What is the spring constant of the spring?
Mission check

?A spring that obeys Hooke's law is 15.0 cm long when it supports a 3.0 N load and 18.0 cm long when it supports a 6.0 N load. What is its natural (unloaded) length?

Extension ∝ force, so each extra newton adds the same extension: evenly spaced markings.

As long as the spring stays within its limit of proportionality.

Mission 4

Mission 4: A thin steel wire and a thick steel cable carry the same load. Why does the wire stretch more?

Make a guess: A thin steel wire and a thick steel cable carry the same load. Why does the wire stretch more?

Mission 4

Stress and strain

  • Tensile stress = force ÷ cross-sectional area. Unit: Pa (N m⁻²).
  • Tensile strain = extension ÷ original length. No unit (a ratio).
  • σ = F/A; ε = Δl/l.
  • Same load on a thinner wire → larger stress.
Mission 4

The Young modulus

  • Young modulus = tensile stress ÷ tensile strain. Unit: Pa.
  • E = σ/ε, which gives E = Fl/AΔl.
  • Valid only where Hooke's law holds (up to the limit of proportionality).
  • It is the gradient of the straight part of a stress–strain graph.
  • It belongs to the material. The spring constant (Young modulus × area ÷ length) belongs to the object.
  • Steel: about 2 × 10¹¹ Pa. Rubber: about 10⁶ Pa.
Mission 4

Worked example

  • Steel wire: length 2.50 m, diameter 0.40 mm, load 20 N, E = 2.0 × 10¹¹ Pa. Extension?
  • A = π(d/2)² = π(0.20 × 10⁻³)² = 1.26 × 10⁻⁷ m². (Radius, not diameter.)
  • Stress = F/A = 20 ÷ 1.26 × 10⁻⁷ = 1.6 × 10⁸ Pa.
  • Strain = stress/E = 1.6 × 10⁸ ÷ 2.0 × 10¹¹ = 8.0 × 10⁻⁴.
  • Extension = strain × original length = 8.0 × 10⁻⁴ × 2.50 = 2.0 × 10⁻³ m (2.0 mm).
Quick check
?A metal wire of length 1.80 m and diameter 0.30 mm is loaded, and a graph of force F against extension is plotted. The straight part of the graph has a gradient of 7.9 × 10³ N m⁻¹. What is the Young modulus of the metal?
Mission 4

Measuring the Young modulus

  • A long, thin wire: a large extension from a modest load, so its percentage uncertainty is small.
  • Clamp one end. Run it over a pulley, or hang it beside an identical reference wire (Searle's apparatus).
  • Diameter: micrometer, several places and directions; average. Length: metre rule to the marker.
  • Add masses in steps; read the extension from a marker against a scale (or a vernier). Unload to check it returns: still elastic.
  • Plot force against extension. Young modulus = gradient × original length ÷ area. (Or plot stress against strain: gradient = Young modulus.)
  • The reference wire cancels temperature changes and sag of the support. Wear eye protection: the wire may snap.
Quick check
?A student repeats the Young modulus experiment with the same loads on a wire of the same metal and diameter, but only one tenth as long. The extension is read with the same scale. What is the main effect?
Mission check

?Two cables are made of the same aluminium alloy and carry the same load. Cable Q is 3 times as long as cable P and has 3 times its diameter. Both obey Hooke's law. What is the extension of Q divided by the extension of P?

Same steel means the same Young modulus. The thin wire has a smaller area, so the same load gives a larger stress, so a larger strain and a larger extension.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Stretch a spring twice as far. Does it store twice the energy?

Make a guess: Stretch a spring twice as far. Does it store twice the energy?

Mission 5

Work done stretching = area

  • Stretching a spring or wire takes work: force × distance moved.
  • The force is not constant: it rises from 0 to its final value as the extension grows from 0.
  • Work done = area under the force–extension graph.
  • In the elastic region this work is stored as elastic strain energy (also called elastic potential energy). Unload, and you get it back.
Mission 5

Energy stored = ½ × force × extension

  • Hooke's law region: the graph is a straight line, so the area is a triangle.
  • Work done = ½Fx (F = final force, x = extension), when Hooke's law is obeyed.
  • Use F = kx: E = ½(kx)x = ½kx².
  • Double the extension → four times the energy.
Mission 5

Worked example

  • Spring: k = 80 N m⁻¹, stretched by 0.050 m.
  • Force: F = kx = 80 × 0.050 = 4.0 N.
  • Energy: E = ½Fx = ½ × 4.0 × 0.050 = 0.10 J.
  • Check: ½kx² = ½ × 80 × 0.050² = 0.10 J.
  • Stretched to 0.10 m: ½ × 80 × 0.10² = 0.40 J. Twice the extension, four times the energy.
  • Not ½ × 4.0 × 0.10: the force is now 8.0 N, not 4.0 N.
Quick check
?A steel wire obeys Hooke's law. A tension of 60 N gives an extension of 1.2 mm. How much elastic strain energy does the wire store?
Mission 5

Curved graphs: use the area

  • Beyond the limit of proportionality the graph curves. ½ × force × extension no longer works.
  • Find the area: count squares, or split it into triangles and trapezia.
  • Energy of one square = (force per square) × (extension per square).
  • Plastic region (the sample stays permanently stretched when unloaded): the work done is not all stored. Most becomes internal energy (the sample warms); the wire stays longer.
Quick check
?The graph shows an idealised force–extension graph for a metal wire. The force rises linearly from 0 to 30 N over the first 4.0 mm, then stays at 30 N as the extension increases to 10.0 mm. How much work is done stretching the wire to 10.0 mm?
Mission 5

Spring energy becomes kinetic and potential energy

  • A launcher spring, k = 200 N m⁻¹, compressed by 0.050 m, fires a 0.020 kg ball.
  • Stored: E = ½kx² = ½ × 200 × 0.050² = 0.25 J.
  • Fired horizontally, all to kinetic energy: ½mv² = 0.25 → v = √(2 × 0.25 ÷ 0.020) = 5.0 m s⁻¹.
  • Fired straight up, all to gravitational potential energy at the top: Δh = 0.25 ÷ (0.020 × 9.81) = 1.3 m above the release point.
  • Real launchers lose some energy to friction and air resistance: the ball rises less.
Quick check
?The spring in a toy dart launcher has a spring constant of 400 N m⁻¹. It is compressed by 0.030 m and then released, firing a dart of mass 0.012 kg horizontally. All the elastic potential energy is transferred to the dart's kinetic energy. What is the speed of the dart?
Mission check

?A spring obeys Hooke's law. When it is compressed by 3.0 cm it stores 0.45 J. How much energy does it store when it is compressed by 4.5 cm?

Energy = ½k × (extension)², so twice the stretch stores four times the energy: twice the extension, at twice the final force.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Bend a paperclip a little and it springs back. Bend it further and it stays bent. Why?

Make a guess: Bend a paperclip a little and it springs back. Bend it further and it stays bent. Why?

Mission 6

Force–extension graph of a metal wire

  • Limit of proportionality (P): up to here, extension ∝ force (Hooke's law). Straight line.
  • Elastic limit (EL): the largest force after which the wire still returns to its original length.
  • Beyond the elastic limit: plastic region. Then the wire breaks.
Mission 6

Elastic and plastic

  • Elastic deformation: removed load → returns to its original length.
  • Plastic deformation: removed load → permanent extension.
  • The limit of proportionality and the elastic limit are different points.
  • A metal loaded into the plastic region unloads along a line parallel to the original straight line. It ends up permanently longer.
Quick check
?A wire is loaded until its force–extension graph has just started to curve. The load is then removed, and the wire returns exactly to its original length. What can you conclude about the load?
Mission 6

Three kinds of stress–strain curve

  • Ductile (copper, mild steel): straight, then a long plastic region. Can be drawn into wires. Thins (necks) before it breaks.
  • Brittle (glass, cast iron): straight right up to fracture. No plastic region. Snaps suddenly.
  • Polymeric (rubber): low stiffness, very large strain, curved. (Polythene: stretches plastically, cold-drawing.)
  • Breaking stress: the stress at which it fractures.
Quick check
?Two materials are tested to destruction. Material X: the stress–strain graph is straight all the way to fracture, at a strain of 0.001. Material Y: the graph is straight to a strain of 0.002, then curves and continues to a strain of 0.40 before fracture. Which describes the materials?
Mission check

?A copper wire is loaded beyond its elastic limit. At the maximum load of 30 N its extension is 6.0 mm. The straight first part of its force–extension graph has a gradient of 20 N per mm. The load is then removed completely. What is the permanent extension of the wire?

A small bend stays below the elastic limit: elastic, so the clip springs back. A bigger bend passes the elastic limit: the deformation becomes plastic, and a permanent bend remains.

Mission 7

Mission 7: A tiny scratch lets you snap a glass sheet. Why doesn't a scratch do the same to a copper sheet?

Make a guess: A tiny scratch lets you snap a glass sheet. Why doesn't a scratch do the same to a copper sheet?

Mission 7

Three kinds of solid

  • Crystalline: particles in a regular pattern repeated over long distances. Metals are many small crystals (grains).
  • Amorphous: no long-range order. Glasses; this course also groups ceramics here.
  • Polymeric: very long chain molecules, tangled together. Rubber, polythene.
Quick check
?Which row correctly classifies table salt (sodium chloride), obsidian (a natural volcanic glass) and nylon?
Mission 7

Metals: elastic, then plastic

  • Elastic strain: bonds stretch a little. Remove the load: atoms return. Hooke's law.
  • Plastic strain: planes of atoms slip past each other. Permanent.
  • Slip happens by dislocations: an extra half-plane of atoms that moves through the crystal.
  • A dislocation moves by breaking one row of bonds at a time. So slip needs a far smaller stress than sliding a whole plane at once.
Mission 7

Making metals stronger

  • Stop the dislocations moving → harder to deform plastically → stronger.
  • Barriers to dislocation movement:
  • Foreign atoms: different-sized atoms distort the lattice (alloys; carbon in iron makes steel).
  • Other dislocations: tangle and pin each other (work hardening: bending or hammering the metal).
  • More grain boundaries: smaller grains; a dislocation stops at a boundary.
  • Usual trade-off: stronger, but less ductile.
Mission 7

Necking and ductile fracture

  • Copper F–x graph: straight line (elastic) → yields → long plastic region → maximum force → falls → breaks.
  • After the maximum, one section thins faster: a neck forms.
  • Stress in the neck rises, so it thins further. The extension concentrates there.
  • Ductile fracture at the neck. Large plastic strain before breaking.
Quick check
?Which treatment makes a copper wire harder to stretch permanently?
Mission 7

Brittle materials: glass

  • Glass obeys Hooke's law until it breaks. No plastic region.
  • Brittle fracture by crack propagation: under tension, stress concentrates at the sharp tip of a crack.
  • Bonds at the tip break, the crack grows, the tip stays sharp. The crack runs right across.
  • No dislocations move in glass, so nothing blunts the tip.
  • Compare a metal: atoms at a crack tip slip plastically, the tip blunts, and the crack stops.
  • Surface imperfections (scratches, tiny cracks) lower the breaking stress.
  • Compression pushes cracks closed. Glass is far stronger in compression than in tension.
Mission 7

Making brittle materials stronger

  • Fewer surface imperfections: thin, freshly drawn glass fibres have a much larger breaking stress than a thick rod.
  • Put the surface under compression. A tension must first cancel it before a crack can open.
  • Toughened glass: the outer surface is cooled quickly and sets first; the inside contracts later and pulls the surface into compression.
  • Pre-stressed concrete: steel tendons are stretched, the concrete sets around them, then they are released. They squeeze the concrete into compression.
Quick check
?A toughened-glass panel survives knocks that would break ordinary glass. But if something sharp scratches deep into it, the whole panel shatters. Why?
Mission 7

Rubber: chains and hysteresis

  • Unstretched: long chain molecules are coiled and tangled, jostled by thermal motion.
  • Stretching straightens the chains against this thermal opposition. Large extension for a small force: low Young modulus.
  • Hooke's law only approximately obeyed. Once the chains are nearly straight, the rubber stiffens.
  • Hysteresis: the unloading curve lies below the loading curve.
  • Area between the curves = energy transferred to internal energy in one load–unload cycle. The rubber warms.
Quick check
?Rubber X has a large hysteresis loop; rubber Y has a very small one. A car tyre flexes and recovers on every turn of the wheel. Which rubber wastes less energy in the tyre, and why?
Mission check

?A crack is slowly growing across a sheet of acrylic, a brittle plastic, in a shop window. A technician drills a small round hole exactly at the crack's tip. The crack stops growing. Why?

A sharp crack tip concentrates stress, so in a brittle material the crack runs. Round the tip off, by slip in a metal such as copper or by a drilled hole, and the crack stops. That is why a scratch snaps glass but not copper.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Charging: Q and V rise to a limit while I decays; discharging: Q, V and I all decay exponentially.
  • τ = RC: 37% left after one τ (discharging), 63% reached (charging).
  • Discharge: Q = Q0e−t/RC (same form for V and I).

Mission 2

  • ln V = ln V0 − t/RC: plot ln V against t; gradient = −1/RC.

Mission 3

  • Extension (not length) is proportional to force: gradient of the force–extension graph = k, in N m⁻¹.
  • This holds up to the limit of proportionality.
  • Beyond the elastic limit the stretch is permanent.

Mission 4

  • Stress = F/A (Pa); strain = extension/original length (no unit); Young modulus = stress/strain, the gradient of the linear part of a stress–strain graph.
  • Measure the Young modulus with a long, thin wire: Young modulus = gradient of force against extension × original length ÷ area.

Mission 5

  • Elastic strain energy = area under the force–extension graph = ½Fx = ½kx² while Hooke's law holds; for a curved graph, find the area.

Mission 6

  • Limit of proportionality: end of the straight line. Elastic limit: beyond it, deformation is plastic and permanent.
  • Ductile: long plastic region; brittle: straight to fracture; polymeric: large strain, low stiffness.

Mission 7

  • Solids: crystalline, amorphous (glasses, ceramics), polymeric. Metals deform plastically by dislocation movement; barriers to dislocations make them stronger.
  • Brittle materials fail by crack propagation from surface flaws under tension; fewer flaws or surface compression raise the breaking stress. Rubber: chains straightening, low Young modulus, hysteresis.
