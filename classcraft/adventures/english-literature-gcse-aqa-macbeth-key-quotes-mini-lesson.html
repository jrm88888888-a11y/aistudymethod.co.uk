Macbeth

This mini-lesson drills your knowledge of Shakespeare's Macbeth for AQA GCSE English Literature: the plot arc, the characters, the big themes (ambition, kingship, guilt, the supernatural, appearance vs reality), the Jacobean context and a bank of key quotations and methods.

The story in one arc

The plot, act by act

Macbeth is a tragedy: a great man is destroyed by his own fatal flaw — ambition.

  • Act 1: Macbeth, a victorious Scottish general, meets three Witches who prophesy he'll be Thane of Cawdor, then king. Spurred on by Lady Macbeth, he plots to murder King Duncan.
  • Act 2: Macbeth kills the sleeping Duncan and seizes the crown; Duncan's sons Malcolm and Donalbain flee.
  • Act 3: Fearing the Witches' prophecy that Banquo's heirs will be kings, Macbeth has Banquo murdered; Banquo's ghost haunts him at the feast.
  • Act 4: The Witches give new prophecies; Macbeth has Macduff's family slaughtered.
  • Act 5: Guilt-ridden Lady Macbeth sleepwalks and dies; Macduff — "not of woman born" — kills Macbeth, and order is restored under Malcolm.

Tragic shape: rise → hubris → downfall → restoration of order. Macbeth's ambition lifts him high, then destroys him.

Who's who

The key characters

  • Macbeth — brave general turned murderous, paranoid tyrant; the tragic hero.
  • Lady Macbeth — his ruthless, ambitious wife who goads him to kill, then collapses under guilt.
  • Banquo — Macbeth's noble fellow general; resists the Witches' temptation and is murdered. His heirs are prophesied to be kings.
  • Duncan — the gracious, trusting King of Scotland whom Macbeth murders.
  • The Witches (Weird Sisters) — supernatural figures whose prophecies plant Macbeth's ambition.
  • Macduff — loyal nobleman; his family is slain, and he ultimately kills Macbeth.

Foil pairs: Banquo foils Macbeth (temptation resisted vs acted upon); Macduff's loyalty foils Macbeth's tyranny.

The big ideas

Themes that run through the play

  • Ambition — the engine of the tragedy; "vaulting ambition" that "o'erleaps itself".
  • Kingship & order — Duncan is the good king; Macbeth the tyrant. The play defends legitimate rule.
  • Guilt — blood that "will not out"; Lady Macbeth's "Out, damned spot!".
  • The supernatural — Witches, prophecies, the dagger, Banquo's ghost.
  • Appearance vs reality — "fair is foul"; "look like the innocent flower, but be the serpent under't".

Link them: ambition breeds the crime, the supernatural tempts it, guilt punishes it, and the theme of kingship judges it.

A bank of key quotations

Quotations worth learning

  • "Fair is foul, and foul is fair" — the Witches: moral disorder is coming; appearance vs reality.
  • "Vaulting ambition, which o'erleaps itself" — Macbeth admits ambition is his only "spur".
  • "Look like th' innocent flower, / But be the serpent under't" — Lady Macbeth on deception.
  • "Is this a dagger which I see before me" — Macbeth's hallucination before the murder.
  • "Will all great Neptune's ocean wash this blood / Clean from my hand?" — Macbeth's guilt after killing Duncan.
  • "Out, damned spot! out, I say!" — Lady Macbeth's guilt in the sleepwalking scene.

Learn short: a few embeddable words (e.g. "vaulting ambition", "damned spot") are worth more than long quotations you can't analyse.

AO2 · method & effect

Method → effect, every time

Naming a device earns almost nothing. Marks come from linking the method to the effect Shakespeare creates:

method + evidence → effect on the audiencee.g. the imperative "Out, damned spot!" (method) shows Lady Macbeth's guilt has become obsessive and uncontrollable (effect)

Methods to spot in Macbeth: metaphor, personification, imperatives, blood imagery, light/dark imagery, dramatic irony, soliloquy, iambic pentameter (and the Witches' trochaic tetrameter).

Golden rule: never stop at "this is a metaphor." Finish the sentence: "…which suggests / emphasises / makes the audience feel …".

Jacobean context · AO3

The world Shakespeare wrote for

Macbeth was written around 1606, early in the reign of King James I (James VI of Scotland). Context shapes the play's meaning:

  • James I was Scottish and believed himself descended from Banquo — so Banquo is shown as noble, and his line is prophesied to rule.
  • The divine right of kings: kings were seen as chosen by God, so killing Duncan is a crime against God and nature (regicide).
  • The Gunpowder Plot (1605): a Catholic plot to blow up James and Parliament. The play's horror of treason and equivocation reflects this fresh fear.
  • Witchcraft: James wrote a book, Daemonologie, about witches; a Jacobean audience feared the supernatural as real and evil.

Weave, don't bolt on: use context to explain a choice — e.g. Duncan's murder feels monstrous because Jacobeans saw kings as God's deputies.

Character in depth

Lady Macbeth's arc

Lady Macbeth is one of the play's most studied characters. Track how she changes:

  • Early: ruthless and commanding — she calls on spirits to "unsex me here" and fill her "from the crown to the toe top-full / Of direst cruelty".
  • Middle: she steadies Macbeth after the murder — "A little water clears us of this deed."
  • Late: guilt destroys her; she sleepwalks, obsessively "washing" her hands — "Out, damned spot!" — and dies (apparently by suicide).

Dramatic irony: she once said "a little water" would cleanse them; by the end no water can. Her collapse mirrors and inverts her early strength.

Avoid these traps

Knowledge slips to avoid

  • Wrong killer: Macbeth murders Duncan himself, but hires murderers to kill Banquo and Macduff's family.
  • The prophecy: the Witches say Banquo's heirs (not Banquo) will be kings; Macbeth is told to fear Macduff and that none "of woman born" shall harm him.
  • Macduff: he was born by caesarean ("untimely ripped"), so he is technically "not of woman born" — that is how he can kill Macbeth.
  • Feature-spotting: naming a device without the effect earns little.

Fix: get the plot facts exactly right, then always push a quotation into method → effect.
