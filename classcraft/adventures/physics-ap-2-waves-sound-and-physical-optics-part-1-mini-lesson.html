Mission 1

Mission 1: Sing a higher note. Does the sound reach the far side of the room any sooner?

Make a guess: Sing a higher note. Does the sound reach the far side of the room any sooner?

Quick check
?A loudspeaker plays a steady note to a listener 5 m away in a room with still air. Which of the following travels from the loudspeaker to the listener?
Mission 1

What a wave carries

  • A traveling wave transfers energy from place to place.
  • It does not transfer matter. Each particle oscillates about a fixed position.
  • A single disturbance is a pulse; a repeating one is a periodic wave.
Mission 1

Transverse and longitudinal

  • Transverse: oscillations perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer. Waves on a string; all EM waves.
  • Longitudinal: oscillations parallel to the direction of energy transfer. Sound; a slinky pushed along its length.
  • Longitudinal waves have compressions (particles close, pressure higher) and rarefactions (spread out, pressure lower).
  • Sound's pressure amplitude: the largest change from normal air pressure. Larger pressure amplitude: louder.
Quick check
?An electric bell rings inside a sealed glass jar and can be both seen and heard. A pump gradually removes the air from the jar while the bell keeps working. Which prediction, with its explanation, is correct?
Mission 1

Mechanical and electromagnetic

  • Mechanical waves (sound, waves on strings, water waves) need a material medium.
  • Electromagnetic waves need no medium: they cross a vacuum.
  • All EM waves travel at the same speed in a vacuum: c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s.
  • A wave's speed depends on the type of wave and the medium.
Mission 1

Describing a wave

  • Displacement: distance and direction of a particle from its rest position.
  • Amplitude A: maximum displacement.
  • Wavelength λ: distance between neighboring points that move in step, e.g. crest to crest.
  • Period T: time for one complete oscillation. Frequency f: oscillations per second, in Hz.
  • f = 1/T.
Mission 1

The wave equation

  • In one period the wave moves on one wavelength: v = λ/T, so v = fλ.
  • Written λ = v/f.
  • For sound in air and waves on a string, the medium sets v and the source sets f.
  • Change f in the same medium: λ changes, v stays the same.
  • Example: sound at 340 m/s, f = 680 Hz → λ = 340/680 = 0.50 m.
Mission 1

Two graphs, two meanings

  • Displacement–position graph: a snapshot of the whole wave. Read λ and A.
  • Displacement–time graph: one particle over time. Read T and A.
  • Same shape, different axis: always check the x-axis label.
  • At one point, starting at a crest: y = A cos(2πft). The graphs here start at zero instead, which is y = A sin(2πft); the same holds for y against x, with 2πx/λ.
Quick check
?A transverse wave travels along a stretched string. Adjacent crests are 1.2 m apart. A graph of displacement y against time t for one point on the string is a sine curve: y = 0 at t = 0, y is a maximum at t = 5 ms, y = 0 at t = 10 ms, y is a minimum at t = 15 ms and y = 0 at t = 20 ms, after which the pattern repeats. What is the wave speed?
Mission 1

Speed of a wave on a string

  • v = √(FT/(m/ℓ)), where m/ℓ is the mass per unit length.
  • Tighter string: faster. Heavier string: slower.
  • Amplitude and frequency do not change v.
  • Example: FT = 25 N, m/ℓ = 0.010 kg/m → v = √2500 = 50 m/s.
Quick check
?A pulse travels at 5.00 m/s along a taut rope. The rope is replaced by a second rope that has the same total mass but twice the length, and the tension is doubled. What is the speed of a pulse on the second rope?
Mission 1

Energy, loudness and pitch

  • Larger amplitude: more energy carried. For sound: louder.
  • Same amplitude, higher frequency: more energy (the medium oscillates faster).
  • Pitch goes with frequency: higher f, higher pitch.
Mission check

?A vibrator sends waves along a stretched string at constant tension. At 25 Hz the wavelength is 0.80 m. The frequency is raised to 100 Hz. What is the new wavelength, and how long does a wave now take to travel 4.0 m along the string?

The medium sets the speed. A higher note has a higher frequency and a shorter wavelength, but the same speed: it arrives no sooner.

Quick check
?A candle flame is held just in front of a loudspeaker playing a loud, low note. The flame flickers rapidly toward and away from the speaker, but it is not blown away from it. What does this show about the air at the flame?
Quick check
?A student at one end of a long steel railway track puts an ear to the rail. At the other end, 1.0 km away, the rail is struck once with a hammer. The student hears two separate sounds, 0.20 s and 2.9 s after the blow. Which conclusion do these observations support?
Mission 2

Speed of sound depends on the medium

  • Rough speeds: air ≈ 340 m/s, water ≈ 1500 m/s, steel ≈ 5000–6000 m/s.
  • The source sets f; the medium sets v. Into a new medium, f stays the same and v changes, so λ = v/f changes.
  • In a given medium, sound travels faster when the medium is hotter: in air, about 331 m/s at 0 °C and 343 m/s at 20 °C.
Mission 2

Loudness and pitch

  • Amplitude: maximum displacement of the air from equilibrium; for a pressure graph, the maximum change in pressure from normal.
  • Larger amplitude → louder, and the wave carries more energy.
  • Period T: time for one oscillation. Frequency f = 1/T.
  • Higher frequency → higher pitch. For the same displacement amplitude, a higher-frequency wave carries more energy.
  • Amplitude and frequency are independent: a note can be loud and low, or quiet and high.
Quick check
?Each graph shows the air pressure P at a microphone as a function of time t for one of two sounds, X and Y. The two graphs are drawn to the same scale. P0 is the pressure of the undisturbed air. Which statement about the two sounds is correct?
Mission check

?A singer on a stage is 17 m from a listener. Her sound reaches the listener 0.050 s later. Which observation is the best evidence that the air itself does not make the trip from the singer to the listener?

Same for air: each layer only oscillates about its own position. Energy reaches your ear; the air that was at the speaker stays there.

Mission 3

Mission 3: A wave reaches the boundary between two materials. Does it carry on, bounce back, or both?

Make a guess: A wave reaches the boundary between two materials. Does it carry on, bounce back, or both?

Mission 3

Reflected and transmitted

  • Wherever the wave speed changes, a boundary splits a wave in two:
    • a reflected wave, back into the first medium;
    • a transmitted wave, on into the second.
  • The energy is shared between them (some may also be absorbed).
  • Bigger change in the medium → more reflected.
    • Sound from air into water: most is reflected.
Quick check
?Predict first. Sound from a loudspeaker in air reaches the surface of a swimming pool, and sound travels on into the water. Which property of the sound is the same in the water as in the air?
Mission 3

What changes and what doesn't

  • Frequency: unchanged. Each oscillation arriving at the boundary drives one oscillation beyond it.
  • Speed: set by the medium, so it changes.
  • Wavelength: λ = v/f, so it changes in proportion to v.
  • Reflected wave: same medium, so same speed and same wavelength as the incident wave.
Quick check
?Predict first. A light string is tied to a heavy string under the same tension, so waves travel more slowly on the heavy string. An upright pulse (displaced upward) travels along the light string toward the knot. Which claim about the reflected pulse, with its reasoning, is correct?
Mission 3

Upright or upside down?

  • Fixed end (wall): the reflected pulse is inverted (phase change of π, or 180°).
  • Free end (ring on a smooth pole): reflected pulse upright.
  • Join between two strings at the same tension:
    • into a slower (heavier) string: reflected pulse inverted;
    • into a faster (lighter) string: reflected pulse upright.
  • The transmitted pulse is always upright.
  • Same rule for light: reflection off a medium of greater n (slower) gives a 180° phase change; off a smaller n, none. Used in thin films.
Mission check

?At night, a person in a brightly lit room sees a faint reflection of the room in the window, while people outside can see into the room through the same window. What does this show?

Both. Wherever the speed changes, part of a wave is reflected and part transmitted; the frequency is kept, so the wavelength follows the speed.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Radio waves and X-rays: the same kind of wave or not? And what really tells them apart?

Make a guess: Radio waves and X-rays: the same kind of wave or not? And what really tells them apart?

Quick check
?Predict first. Which statement describes the nature of electromagnetic waves?
Mission 4

What an EM wave is

  • An electromagnetic (EM) wave is an oscillating electric field and an oscillating magnetic field, traveling together.
  • The two fields are perpendicular to each other and to the direction of travel: EM waves are transverse.
  • Energy travels along the direction of travel; the fields oscillate across it.
  • EM waves need no medium: they cross a vacuum.
  • In a vacuum all EM waves travel at the same speed, c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s, whatever their wavelength.
  • Examples: the radio waves from an FM radio station, visible light, the X-rays used in medical imaging.
  • Far from the source they are modeled as plane waves: flat, parallel wavefronts.
Quick check
?The diagram shows the electric field E of an electromagnetic wave at one instant, and the direction in which the wave travels. Along which line does the magnetic field of the wave oscillate, and why?
Quick check
?Predict first. Which ranking of the wavelengths of four categories of electromagnetic waves, from longest to shortest, is correct?
Mission 4

The spectrum, in order

  • Seven regions, by decreasing wavelength: radio, microwave, infrared, visible, ultraviolet, X-ray, gamma.
  • Same order: increasing frequency (f = c/λ).
  • Visible light, longest to shortest: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, violet.
  • The regions differ only in wavelength (and so frequency). Same nature, same c.
  • Boundaries are not sharp. X-rays and gamma rays overlap: they are named by origin (X-rays from electrons, gamma rays from nuclei).
Mission check

?In a vacuum, the microwaves in a speed camera have a wavelength of 3.0 cm, and the light from a yellow lamp has a wavelength of 600 nm. How do their frequencies compare?

Microwaves and light, like radio waves and X-rays, are the same kind of wave, with the same speed c in a vacuum. Only the wavelength, and so the frequency, tells them apart.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Turning one filter against another can block light, but not sound. What does that tell us about light?

Make a guess: Turning one filter against another can block light, but not sound. What does that tell us about light?

Mission 5

Plane polarized

  • Transverse wave: oscillations perpendicular to the direction of travel. They can point in many directions across it.
  • Plane polarized: the oscillations stay in one plane that contains the direction of travel.
  • For light (EM waves), the plane of oscillation is the plane of the electric field.
  • Unpolarized light (Sun, lamps): the oscillation direction changes rapidly and randomly.
  • Longitudinal wave: oscillations along the direction of travel. Only one direction is possible, so it cannot be polarized.
  • So: if a wave can be polarized, it must be transverse.
Quick check
?Predict first. A long rope passes through a narrow slot in a board. The figure shows the view along the rope toward the board, with the slot and the direction in which a student moves the rope back and forth to send a transverse wave toward the board. Which describes the wave on the far side of the board?
Mission 5

A rope through a slot

  • Shake a rope that passes through a vertical slot.
  • Shake it up and down: the wave passes through.
  • Shake it side to side: the slot stops the rope moving. No wave beyond the slot.
  • Shake it in every direction: only the up-and-down part gets through. The wave beyond is plane polarized.
Quick check
?Predict first. Unpolarized light from a lamp passes through two ideal polarizing filters, P1 and P2. The lines drawn on the filters in the figure show their transmission axes. I1, I2 and I3 are the intensities of the light at points 1, 2 and 3. Which ranking of the intensities is correct?
Mission 5

Polarizing filters

  • A polarizing filter (Polaroid) lets through only oscillations along its transmission axis.
  • Unpolarized light in → plane polarized light out. An ideal filter passes half the intensity.
  • Add a second filter, the analyzer. Axes parallel: the light passes on. Axes crossed (at 90°): no light gets through.
  • Rotate the analyzer through a full turn: brightest at 0° and 180°, dark at 90° and 270°.
  • Intensity = average power per unit area (W/m²). Polarizing reduces it.
Quick check
?Predict first. Sunlight reflected from a wet road produces glare. Polarizing sunglasses reduce this glare much more than tinted sunglasses that let through the same fraction of direct sunlight. Which explanation is supported by physics?
Mission 5

Using polarization

  • Glare: light reflected from water, wet roads or glass is partly polarized, mostly horizontally.
  • Polarizing sunglasses have a vertical transmission axis: they block much of the glare but pass other light.
Mission check

?A student looks at a calculator's display through a polarizing filter and slowly rotates the filter. The display is bright, goes dark after 90° of rotation, and is bright again after 180°. What does this show?

Only transverse waves can be polarized. Light can be polarized and sound cannot: light is transverse, sound is longitudinal.

Mission 6

Mission 6: You walk away from a small light source. How quickly does the light reaching you fade?

Make a guess: You walk away from a small light source. How quickly does the light reaching you fade?

Mission 6

Intensity

  • Intensity: power arriving per unit area, on an area perpendicular to the wave's direction.
  • I = P/A. Unit: W/m².
  • For a wave whose power varies during each cycle, use the average power.
  • Example: sound of intensity 5.0 × 10⁻³ W/m² arriving face-on at a 0.50 m² window delivers 2.5 × 10⁻³ W.
Quick check
?Sunlight of intensity 1.0 × 10³ W/m² falls perpendicularly on a flat solar cell of area 150 cm². At what rate does the sunlight deliver energy to the cell?
Mission 6

Spreading out

  • A small (point) source sends power P equally in all directions.
  • At distance r, that power is spread over a sphere of area 4πr².
  • I = P/(4πr²): double r → area × 4 → intensity ÷ 4. Triple r → ÷ 9.
  • This is the inverse-square law: I ∝ 1/r².
  • Assumes: point source, no absorption, no reflections.
Mission 6

Worked example

  • A small lamp radiates 3.0 W of light evenly in all directions.
  • At 2.0 m: I = 3.0 ÷ (4π × 2.0²) = 6.0 × 10⁻² W/m².
  • At 4.0 m: I = 3.0 ÷ (4π × 4.0²) = 1.5 × 10⁻² W/m²: a quarter.
  • At 6.0 m: 6.6 × 10⁻³ W/m²: a ninth of the value at 2.0 m.
Quick check
?Predict first. A student sends two pulses, one after the other, along the same taut rope. For the first pulse she moves her hand up 10 cm and back down; for the second she moves it up 20 cm and back down in the same time. Which statement comparing the two pulses is correct?
Mission 6

Intensity and amplitude

  • A wave's energy grows with its amplitude.
  • Larger amplitude → more energy carried, and a louder sound. For sound, the amplitude can be the pressure amplitude.
  • Amplitude does not change the wave's speed.
Quick check
?Unpolarized light of intensity 40 W/m² passes through two ideal polarizing filters whose transmission axes are parallel. What is the intensity of the light after the second filter?
Mission check

?A small source sends out sound equally in all directions. At 2.0 m from the source the intensity is 6.0 × 10⁻³ W/m². Ignoring reflections and absorption, what is the intensity 3.0 m from the source?

The same power spreads over a sphere of area 4πr². Intensity falls as 1/r²: double the distance → a quarter; 1.5 times the distance → 1/2.25.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • A traveling wave transfers energy, not matter. Transverse: oscillation ⟂ travel; longitudinal: oscillation ∥ travel.
  • f = 1/T and v = fλ. For sound and waves on strings, the medium sets v and the source sets f.
  • Mechanical waves need a medium; EM waves cross a vacuum at c.
  • On a string, v = √(FT/(m/ℓ)). Amplitude: loudness and energy. Frequency: pitch.

Mission 2

  • Sound: a longitudinal wave of compressions and rarefactions; the medium oscillates about fixed positions and carries energy, not itself. It needs a medium; v = fλ, set by the medium.
  • Loudness ↔ amplitude (pressure amplitude); pitch ↔ frequency; speed rises with temperature.

Mission 3

  • At a boundary where the wave speed changes, a wave is partly reflected and partly transmitted; frequency is unchanged, so λ changes with v.
  • Reflection from a fixed end, or into a slower medium, inverts the pulse; from a free end, or into a faster medium, it does not.
  • Light reflecting off a greater n: 180° phase change.

Mission 4

  • EM waves: oscillating E and B fields, perpendicular to each other and to the direction of travel (transverse); no medium; all travel at c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s in a vacuum.
  • Radio → microwave → infrared → visible → UV → X-ray → gamma: wavelength falls, frequency rises.

Mission 5

  • Plane polarized: oscillations in one plane. Only transverse waves can be polarized, so polarization is evidence that light (and all EM waves) are transverse.
  • Crossed polarizing filters block all the light; rotating one varies the intensity from maximum to zero every 90°.

Mission 6

  • Intensity I = P/A (W/m²), measured on an area perpendicular to the wave.
  • Point source, no absorption: I = P/(4πr²), so I ∝ 1/r².
