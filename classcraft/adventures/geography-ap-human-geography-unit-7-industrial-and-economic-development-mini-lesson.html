Mission 1

What changed

  • The Industrial Revolution: the change from an economy of farming and handicrafts to one "dominated by industry and machine manufacturing".
  • It "began in Britain in the 18th century and from there spread to other parts of the world".
  • New features:
    • New materials: chiefly iron and steel.
    • New energy: coal and the steam engine.
    • Factories: work organized in one building, around machines.
Mission 1

Why Britain first? Resources

  • Britain had large coal fields. Near them, "Britain had the cheapest energy in the world".
  • By the end of the 1500s, charcoal and firewood in Britain were "twice the price of coal per unit of energy".
  • Cheap coal made it worth inventing machines that burn fuel instead of using human muscle.
Mission 1

Why Britain first? Technology

  • "British wages and living standards were the highest in the world".
  • High wages + cheap energy → businesses wanted machines that replace workers.
  • Steam engines kept improving: coal burned per horsepower-hour fell from 45 pounds in the early 1700s to 2 pounds by the mid-1800s.
  • Technology + natural resources = industrialization.
Quick check
?Why did industrialization begin in Britain rather than in a place with no coal?
Mission 1

More food, more people

  • Farming had already improved: enclosure "greatly improved the agricultural productivity of farms from the late 18th century".
  • More food supported more people. UK population (estimates):
    • 1820: 21.2 million
    • 1900: 41.2 million (nearly double; calculated 41,155 ÷ 21,239 = 1.94)
  • Real GDP per person rose from $3,306 to $7,594 (2011 dollars) over the same years.
Mission 1

From fields to cities

  • Fewer workers were needed on farms; factories needed workers in towns.
  • People moved to cities for industrial jobs: urbanization.
  • As industry spread to the United States, Chicago grew from 29,963 people in 1850 to 1,698,575 in 1900. That is about 57 times larger (calculated: 1,698,575 ÷ 29,963 = 56.7).
  • Diffusion: from Britain, industrialization "spread to other parts of the world". Japan made "the first successful transfer of industrialization from the West to a non-Western nation", between the 1850s and 1910.
Mission 1

New class structures

  • Before: most people were farmers or rural laborers; land meant wealth.
  • After: new groups grew up around industry:
    • Industrial working class: wage workers in factories and mines.
    • Middle class: factory owners, managers, clerks, engineers, shopkeepers.
  • Wealth came more and more from owning factories and capital, not only land.
Quick check
?Which change came with industrialization in Britain?
Mission 1

Factories are hungry

  • Industry brought "a tremendously increased use of natural resources" and "increased international trade".
  • Factories needed raw materials they could not get at home: cotton, rubber, metals, palm oil.
  • They also needed markets: more buyers for all the goods they made.
  • Investors looked overseas for both.
Mission 1

From trade to empire

  • Colonialism: one country takes control of another territory and settles or rules it.
  • Imperialism: one country extends its power over others, by force, money or politics.
  • The hunt for raw materials and markets was one factor in the rise of both.
  • Example: from November 1884 to February 1885, European powers met in Berlin to settle questions about the Congo River basin in Central Africa. In the decades after, European powers claimed most of Africa.
Quick check
?Which statement best links industrialization to imperialism?
Mission check

?Country R has just built its first steel mills and textile factories, but it has little iron ore, no cotton, and too few buyers at home. Following the pattern of industrial Europe, what is it most likely to do?

Industrialization began in Britain with new technology and cheap coal, then spread (for example to Japan from the 1850s). Factories needed raw materials and markets, a factor in the European takeover of Africa.

Mission 2

Five economic sectors

  • Primary: taking resources from nature: farming, fishing, forestry, mining.
  • Secondary: making things: manufacturing, processing, construction.
  • Tertiary: services to people and firms: retail, transport, health care, teaching.
  • Quaternary: knowledge and information: research, software, finance, data.
  • Quinary: top-level decisions: senior executives, government leaders, heads of major organizations.
Mission 2

The top two sectors

  • Quaternary jobs create, process and move information: a lab scientist, a software engineer, a financial analyst.
  • Quinary jobs make the highest-level decisions that shape whole firms, cities or countries.
  • Both are small in number but cluster in a few places: big cities, university towns, capital cities.

Mission 2

Different countries, different jobs

Share of workers by sector, 2025 (ILO modeled estimates):

  • Niger: agriculture 73.0%, industry 9.3%, services 17.7%.
  • India: agriculture 41.6%, industry 25.8%, services 32.6%.
  • China: agriculture 21.7%, industry 31.8%, services 46.5%.
  • USA: agriculture 1.5%, industry 18.9%, services 79.6%.
Mission 2

Reading the data carefully

  • The data use three groups, not five sectors.
  • Agriculture = farming, forestry, fishing (most of the primary sector).
  • Industry = mining, manufacturing, construction, utilities (secondary plus mining).
  • Services = tertiary, quaternary and quinary together.
  • Pattern: the less developed a country, the larger its primary share; the more developed, the larger its services share.

Mission 2

Change over time

  • USA: 41% of workers were in agriculture in 1900; 1.9% in 2000; 2.9% in 1991 and 1.5% in 2025.
  • China: agriculture fell from 59.6% of workers in 1991 to 21.7% in 2025; services rose from 18.9% to 46.5%.
  • Germany: industry fell from 38.4% to 26.0% of workers, 1991–2025, while services grew.

Mission 2

Why the shift happens

  • Machines and better farming let fewer farmers feed more people, freeing workers for other jobs.
  • Manufacturing grows as countries industrialize.
  • Rising incomes are spent more on services: health, education, travel, entertainment.
  • In rich countries, factory jobs then shrink as automation and outsourcing grow (deindustrialization).
Mission 2

Where knowledge work clusters

  • Spending on research and development (R&D) is one sign of a large quaternary sector.
  • R&D spending as % of GDP, 2023: Israel 6.3, South Korea 4.9, USA 3.4, Germany 3.2, China 2.6, world 2.6.
  • Quaternary and quinary jobs cluster in core countries and their largest cities, where universities, firm headquarters and governments are.
Quick check

Mission check

?Vietnam's workers by sector. 1991: agriculture 74.0%, industry 7.3%, services 18.7%. 2025: agriculture 25.0%, industry 34.8%, services 40.1%. What does this show?

Each sector has its own development pattern: primary jobs dominate less developed economies; secondary jobs grow with industrialization; tertiary, quaternary and quinary jobs dominate the most developed economies and cluster in core cities.

Mission 3

What decides where factories go

  • Resources: raw materials and energy.
  • Labor: how much workers cost and what skills they have.
  • Markets: where the buyers are.
  • Transportation: the cost of moving materials in and products out.
  • Firms weigh these to find the cheapest place to make and deliver their product.
Mission 3

Weber's least cost theory

  • Alfred Weber set out a theory of industrial location in 1909.
  • His location triangle: a market and two raw-material sources; the factory goes at the least-cost point.
  • Main cost is transport: moving raw materials to the plant plus moving the product to market.
Mission 3

Losing weight or gaining it

  • Weight-losing (bulk-reducing) industries: the product weighs much less than the inputs, so locate near raw materials. Example: smelting metal from ore.
  • Weight-gaining (bulk-gaining) industries: little weight is lost, or weight is added (often water), so locate near the market. Example: bottling soft drinks.
Quick check
?A firm turns sugar cane into sugar. The sugar weighs far less than the cane. Where does Weber's theory place the mill?
Mission 3

When labor pulls harder

  • Weber then looked for sites with lower labor costs than the least-transport-cost point.
  • If the labor saving is bigger than the extra transport cost, the factory moves to the cheap-labor site.
  • Total cost = transport + labor (+ other costs): the lowest total wins.
Quick check
?Imaginary costs per unit made. Site P: transport $50, labor $30. Site Q: transport $65, labor $10. Which site, and what is its total cost per unit?
Mission 3

The break-of-bulk point

  • A break-of-bulk point is where cargo is moved from one kind of transport to another: ship to train or truck, for example. Ports are the classic case.
  • Every transfer costs time and money. A factory at the break-of-bulk point saves a transfer: imported materials arrive by sea and are processed on the spot.
  • So heavy industries that import raw materials, such as steel or oil refining, often cluster at ports.
Mission 3

Shipping containers

  • Containers: standard-sized steel boxes moved from ship to train to truck without unpacking. Container ships "can be loaded and unloaded rapidly".
  • Containerization became significant in ocean shipping in the 1960s.
  • World container port traffic rose from 547.1 million TEU in 2010 to 913.3 million in 2024 (TEU = twenty-foot equivalent unit).
  • China's ports handled 299.7 million TEU in 2024, about 33% of the world total (our calculation).
  • Cheap, fast container shipping lets firms put factories far from their markets.
Quick check
?Why do containers make it easier for a firm to build its factory far from its customers?
Mission 3

Core, semiperiphery, periphery

Share of world manufacturing value added, 2024 (calculated from World Bank data):

  • High-income countries: about 52%.
  • China alone: about 28%.
  • Low-income countries: about 0.3%.
  • Core countries keep high-value, highly automated and research-based manufacturing.
  • Semiperiphery countries (often including China, Mexico, Vietnam) attract assembly and mass production with lower labor costs, ports and growing markets.
  • Periphery countries have little manufacturing: weak transport links, little capital and small markets.
Mission 3

Putting the factors together

  • Resources + transport → weight-losing plants near raw materials or at break-of-bulk ports.
  • Markets → weight-gaining and perishable products near buyers.
  • Labor → labor-intensive assembly in lower-wage semiperiphery locations, linked by container shipping.
  • AP move: name the factor, then explain the cost it lowers.
Mission check

?An invented soft-drink company imports flavor concentrate by sea; it is light and arrives at a coastal port. Its customers are in a big city 300 miles inland. Bottling adds a lot of weight, because the drink is mostly local water. Where should it build its bottling plant, by least cost reasoning?

Weight-losing imports (ore, coal) are processed at the break-of-bulk port; weight-gaining products (drinks) are made near the market. Least cost theory weighs resources, transport, labor and markets, which together explain why manufacturing is spread unevenly across core, semiperiphery and periphery.

Mission 4

Measuring an economy

  • GDP (gross domestic product): the value of all goods and services produced inside a country in a year.
  • GNP / GNI (gross national product / income): GDP plus income earned from abroad, minus income paid out to people and firms abroad. GNI is the term the World Bank and UN use today.
  • Per capita: divided by the population, so big and small countries can be compared.
Mission 4

When GDP and GNI differ: Ireland

  • In 2024 Ireland's GDP was about US$609 billion, but its GNI was about US$458 billion.
  • Ireland's statistics office explains that much of its GDP "includes profits that are generated here but then go straight out to the owners of companies abroad".
  • So GNI per capita is usually the better guide to the income a country's people actually receive.

Mission 4

The Human Development Index (HDI)

  • A composite measure with three dimensions: a long and healthy life (life expectancy), knowledge (years of schooling), a decent standard of living (GNI per capita).
  • Each dimension is scored from 0 to 1. Income is scored on a log scale, so each extra dollar counts for less as income rises.
  • The three scores are combined with a geometric mean: a weak dimension pulls the HDI down more than a simple average would.
Mission 4

HDI shows spatial variation among states

  • 2023: Iceland ranked 1st (0.972); South Sudan ranked 193rd (0.388); world 0.756.
  • USA 0.938 (17th); India 0.685 (130th); Niger 0.419 (188th).
  • Mapped, the HDI shows a clear pattern: the highest values in Europe, North America, Australia and Japan; the lowest mainly in sub-Saharan Africa.

The Gender Inequality Index (GII)

  • The GII measures disadvantage for women in three dimensions, from 0 (women and men fare equally) to 1:
    • reproductive health: maternal mortality and adolescent birth rate
    • empowerment: share of seats in parliament, and adults with at least some secondary education
    • labor market: labor force participation.

  • Worldwide, 48.5% of women aged 15+ were in the labor force, against 74.1% of men.

Mission 5

Women's work changes with development

  • Economist Claudia Goldin found that married women's labor force participation "first declines and then rises as countries develop": a U-shape.
  • The fall comes as work moves from "the household, family farm, and small business to the wider market".
  • The rise comes as women gain education, "particularly at the secondary level", and move into white-collar jobs.
Mission 5

The U-shape in today's data

Women aged 15+ in the labor force, 2025 (modeled ILO estimates):

  • Niger (low income, mostly farming): 72.8%.
  • India: 32.4%. Bangladesh: 38.7%, up from 24.2% in 1990.
  • USA: 56.3%. South Korea: 56.8%, up from 47.2% in 1990.
  • In the poorest countries women work, mostly on farms; with development their roles shift toward paid, formal jobs.
Quick check

More women at work in the USA

US women working full-time, year-round:

  • 1980: 22.9 million. 2024: 53.8 million.
  • Women's median earnings as a share of men's: 60.2% in 1980, 80.9% in 2024.
  • The ratio fell in both 2023 and 2024: "the second consecutive annual decrease".

Mission 5

Work without equity

  • Gender parity: equal outcomes for women and men. More work is not the same as equal pay or equal opportunity.
  • Worldwide, 48.5% of women aged 15+ were in the labor force in 2023, against 74.1% of men.
  • Women held 26.5% of seats in the world's parliaments: fewer opportunities at the top.
Mission 5

Microloans

  • Microloans (microcredit): very small loans to people that ordinary banks ignore, often to start a small local business.
  • In 2006 Muhammad Yunus and Grameen Bank (Bangladesh) won the Nobel Peace Prize "for their efforts to create economic and social development from below".
  • In 2018, microfinance institutions reported 139.9 million borrowers: 80% women, 65% rural.
Mission 5

Lenders here, borrowers there

  • Kiva lets people lend from $25 to small businesses in other countries. Kiva says 2 of every 3 of its loans have gone to women.
  • Savings in one place become a sewing machine, a market stall or livestock in another: a spatial interaction.
Quick check
?In 2018 there were 139.9 million microfinance borrowers, and 80% were women. About how many women borrowed?
Mission 5

What the trials found

  • Six randomized evaluations, in six countries on four continents, tested what happens when people get access to microcredit.
  • The researchers found "a consistent pattern of modestly positive, but not transformative, effects".
  • Loans help some households run small businesses and manage money; on average they have not lifted large numbers out of poverty.
Mission 5

Andhra Pradesh, India, 2010

  • By 2010, microfinance institutions in India had reached 27 million borrowers, growing at 80% a year.
  • In Andhra Pradesh, 83% of surveyed households had loans from more than one source; many had "too many loans and too much debt".
  • In October 2010 the state passed an ordinance "to protect the women Self Help Groups from exploitation", and loan collections "dropped dramatically".
Quick check
?What does the Andhra Pradesh crisis show about microloans?
Mission 5

And fairer prices?

  • Fair trade takes a different route: it sets a minimum price for farmers. For washed Arabica coffee it is $1.80 per pound for contracts from August 1, 2023.
  • Buyers also pay a Fairtrade Premium for community projects: €201.6 million in 2024.
  • But in 2024 only about 27% of reported certified coffee production was sold on Fairtrade terms.
  • Like small loans, fair trade is a tool, not a cure. Unit 5 also covers fair trade.
Mission check

?A charity plans to offer small loans to women in a poor rural district. Based on the evidence, what is the most likely result?

Small loans help modestly: some women start or grow businesses and raise living standards, but trials found no transformation, and in Andhra Pradesh in 2010 over-lending caused a crisis. Fairer prices help too, but only about 27% of reported certified coffee was sold on Fairtrade terms in 2024. Development has brought more women into paid work, yet in the USA women still earn about 81% of what men earn.

Mission 6

Rostow's stages of economic growth

  • W. W. Rostow, The Stages of Economic Growth (1960). Five stages: 1. Traditional society: mostly subsistence farming. 2. Preconditions for take-off: investment in transport, education, trade. 3. Take-off: a few manufacturing industries grow fast. 4. Drive to maturity: industry spreads and diversifies. 5. High mass consumption: most people buy consumer goods; services grow.

Mission 6

Limits of Rostow

  • It assumes every country can follow the same path, one stage after another.
  • It is based mainly on the history of Western Europe and the USA.
  • It looks inside each country and largely ignores how countries are linked: colonial history, trade and debt.
Mission 6

Dependency theory

  • Andre Gunder Frank's essay "The Development of Underdevelopment" (1966) is a classic statement.
  • Dependency theory: poorer countries stay poor because of their links to richer ones.
  • Colonies were set up to export raw materials and buy manufactured goods. After independence, profits and decisions often stayed with outside firms and buyers.
  • So underdevelopment is produced by the system, not just a stage to pass through.
Mission 6

Wallerstein's world system theory

  • Immanuel Wallerstein set out world-system analysis in 1974.
  • One world economy with three tiers:
    • Core: high-wage, high-tech, capital-intensive production.
    • Periphery: low-wage labor, raw materials, little control over prices.
    • Semiperiphery: a mix of both; it may be moving up or down.
  • Countries can change tier over time.

Mission 6

Commodity dependence

  • Commodities: raw materials and farm goods, such as oil, copper, coffee.
  • UNCTAD calls a country commodity-dependent when commodities are more than 60% of its merchandise exports.
  • 2019–2021: 101 of 191 UNCTAD member states were commodity-dependent; among developing countries, 95 of 142.
  • Zambia: copper was 69% of its merchandise exports (2019–2021 average).
Mission 6

Why dependence is risky

  • UNCTAD found that energy and metal prices, including copper, swing the most: the typical swing in monthly prices was more than 40% of the average price.
  • When the main export's price falls, export earnings and government budgets fall with it.
  • Nigeria: crude oil was 77% of merchandise exports (2019–2021).
  • To develop, countries try to diversify into processing and manufacturing.

Mission 6

Four lenses on one gap

  • Rostow: Korea moved through take-off to mass consumption; Zambia did not take off.
  • Dependency: Zambia's economy was built to export raw copper; control over prices and profits lay outside.
  • World system: Korea moved from the periphery toward the core; Zambia remained in the periphery.
  • Commodity dependence: copper, at 69% of Zambia's exports, ties its income to a volatile price.

Complementarity

  • Complementarity: one place has what another needs, and the reverse, so trade can flow between them.
  • Example: Persian Gulf producers export oil, and Asian economies need it. In 2024, 84% of the crude oil passing through the Strait of Hormuz went to Asian markets.
  • No complementarity, no reason to trade.
Mission 7

Comparative advantage

  • Absolute advantage: making a good with fewer resources than another country.
  • Comparative advantage: making a good at a lower opportunity cost: giving up less of other goods to make it.
  • A country gains by specializing in goods where its comparative advantage lies, then trading.

Mission 7

Neoliberal policies

  • Neoliberalism: policies that cut the government's role in markets: lower tariffs, free trade agreements, privatization, deregulation.
  • Since the late 20th century, these policies created new organizations and trade links that tie economies together.
  • Result: more interdependence: countries rely on each other's goods, markets and money.
Mission 7

The WTO

  • The World Trade Organization began on January 1, 1995.
  • It is "the only international organization dealing with the rules of trade between nations"; 166 members cover 98% of world trade.
  • In the Uruguay Round deal that created it, developed countries cut tariffs on industrial goods by 40%, from an average of 6.3% to 3.8%.

Trade blocs: EU and Mercosur

  • EU: a single market of 27 countries where "goods, services, capital and persons can circulate freely".
  • Mercosur (Southern Common Market): Argentina, Brazil, Paraguay, Uruguay and, since 2024, Bolivia; Venezuela is suspended.
  • Free trade agreements keep spreading: 388 regional trade agreements were in force on October 1, 2026.
Mission 7

OPEC: a different kind of organization

  • OPEC was formed in 1960 by Iraq, Iran, Kuwait, Saudi Arabia and Venezuela to "coordinate and unify petroleum policies".
  • In 2025 OPEC members produced about 35% of the world's crude oil.
  • It is not a free trade agreement: members coordinate supply of one export. Membership changes: the UAE left on May 1, 2026.

Tariffs

  • Tariff: a tax on imports. It makes foreign goods more expensive, which can protect domestic producers.
  • Regional and national scale: in 2016 the East African Community said it would phase in a ban on used-clothing imports. Rwanda raised tariffs on used clothing and footwear "by more than one thousand percent".

Mission 8

What is a debt crisis?

  • Governments borrow from other governments, banks and investors abroad, often in US dollars.
  • If export earnings fall or the local currency loses value, repaying in dollars gets harder.
  • A default is when a government stops paying what it owes. A debt crisis follows: no new loans, scarce imports, rising prices.
Mission 8

Sri Lanka, 2022

  • The IMF describes "the external default in 2022": Sri Lanka stopped paying its foreign debts.
  • Its economy shrank by 7.3% in 2022 and 2.0% in 2023.
  • Consumer prices rose by 49.7% on average in 2022; inflation peaked at 70% in September 2022.
Mission 8

Why the debt became unpayable

  • The IMF says "Public debt increased drastically in 2020-22 due to exchange rate depreciation, large fiscal deficits, SOE borrowing".
  • Depreciation: the rupee lost value, so each dollar of debt cost more rupees.
  • SOE: state-owned enterprises, such as energy companies, which also borrowed.
Mission 8

A crisis that crosses borders

  • Sri Lanka's creditors included a committee of foreign governments, China's Export-Import Bank, the China Development Bank and private bondholders.
  • To end the crisis, these lenders in many countries had to agree to accept less.
  • One country's default became a problem for governments, banks and investors around the world: interdependence.

Mission 8

The IMF: who decides?

  • The International Monetary Fund (IMF) has 191 member countries. It lends to countries that cannot pay for imports or debts.
  • Each member's quota sets its voting power.
  • The biggest decisions, such as changing quotas, need "approval by 85% of the total voting power".
  • The USA has about 16.5% of the votes: more than 15%, so it can block those decisions alone.
Mission 8

Sri Lanka's rescue loan and its conditions

  • March 2023: the IMF approved a 48-month loan of about US$3 billion.
  • Conditionality: Sri Lanka had to make agreed reforms, including higher taxes and cost-recovery energy pricing (charging what energy actually costs).
  • Tax revenue rose from 7.3% of GDP in 2022 to 9.8% in 2023.

Results, and who paid

  • "Inflation declined from 70 percent to around 4 percent in 2023".
  • Foreign-currency reserves reached US$5.5 billion by the end of April 2024, and the economy grew by 5.0% in 2024.
  • But higher taxes and energy prices raised costs for households. The IMF itself called for social safety net reforms "to protect the poor and the vulnerable".

Mission 8

Microlending: development from below

  • Microlending (microcredit): very small loans to poor people, often women, to start or grow a small business.
  • At Bangladesh's Grameen Bank, "no collateral is required to get the credit".
  • Up to August 2026 it had lent to 10.63 million borrowers, 97% of them women.
  • Grameen Bank and its founder, Muhammad Yunus, won the 2006 Nobel Peace Prize "for their efforts to create economic and social development from below".
Mission 8

Other alternatives

  • The BRICS countries (Brazil, Russia, India, China, South Africa) signed the agreement for a New Development Bank (NDB) in 2014.
  • Its five founders must keep at least 55% of the votes. It has 10 members, against the IMF's 191.
  • The NDB funds projects such as power and transport; it is not a crisis lender like the IMF.
  • Microlending works at the household scale; the IMF works at the national scale.

Mission 9

From Fordism to post-Fordism

  • Fordism: mass production of identical goods on assembly lines in huge factories, with big stocks of parts.
  • Post-Fordism: flexible production in smaller batches, tailored to markets, with work spread across many firms and countries.
  • Post-Fordist tools include outsourcing and just-in-time delivery.
Mission 9

Outsourcing and restructuring

  • Outsourcing: a firm contracts another firm to do part of its work, such as assembly.
  • Offshoring: moving work to another country, whether the firm owns it or contracts it out.
  • Economic restructuring: a region's economy shifts from one kind of work to another, such as from factories to services.
Mission 9

Deindustrialization in the core

  • Deindustrialization: manufacturing jobs decline in a region.
  • US manufacturing employed an average of 19.4 million people in 1979 and 12.6 million in 2025.
  • That is a fall of about 35% (calculated: 6.8 ÷ 19.4).
  • Outsourcing and offshoring are one cause; new technology that lets fewer workers make more is another.

Mission 9

Apple: outsourcing on a global scale

  • Apple, based in Cupertino, California, had about 166,000 employees in 2025.
  • "A significant majority" of its manufacturing is done by outsourcing partners located mainly in China mainland, India, Japan, South Korea, Taiwan and Vietnam.
  • Partners in Asia carry out final assembly of "substantially all" of its hardware.
  • Apple keeps design, software and services: high-value work in the core.
Mission 9

Jobs move to newly industrialized countries

  • Assembly jobs are created where partner firms operate: China, India and Vietnam are on Apple's list.
  • Developing Asia received $644 billion of foreign direct investment in 2025.
  • Apple says outsourcing "can lower operating costs", but it reduces "direct control over production and distribution".

  • In the Toyota Production System, "each process produces only what is needed for the next process in a continuous flow": just-in-time delivery.
  • Low stocks mean parts must arrive exactly when needed, so suppliers locate near the assembly plant.
  • Toyota and its affiliates run more than 50 manufacturing operations in 27 countries and regions outside Japan.
  • In North America, 74.6% of the Toyota vehicles sold were made in North America.

Mission 9

Economies of scale and agglomeration

  • Economies of scale: making more units lowers the cost of each one. Apple's partners assemble huge numbers of devices in a few places.
  • Agglomeration: firms cluster together to share suppliers, workers and infrastructure. Toyota's suppliers cluster around its plants.
  • Phones are small and light, so assembly can be concentrated; cars are bulky, and Toyota designs them for local "road conditions, climate, and values".
Mission 9

Multiplier effects and growth poles

  • Multiplier effect: new jobs create more jobs. Factory workers spend wages locally; suppliers and services grow.
  • Growth pole: a place where investment sets off growth that spreads to the wider region.
  • Example: Shenzhen became China's first special economic zone in 1980. By the end of 2024 it had 17.98 million residents.

  • Service sector: jobs that provide services, such as finance, software and retail, rather than goods.
  • High-tech industries: research-heavy work such as chip design and software.
  • Apple's services earned $109.2 billion of its $416.2 billion sales in the year to September 2025: more than a quarter.
  • In core economies, these jobs grew as factory jobs fell.

The international division of labor

  • Making one product is split into stages: design, finance and marketing; making parts; assembly.

Mission 10

Special economic zones

  • A special economic zone (SEZ) is a "geographically delimited" area where governments support industry "through fiscal and regulatory incentives and infrastructure support".
  • Typical offers: "fiscal incentives, relief from customs duties and tariffs", simpler permits and rules, and better roads, power and ports.
  • Zones often make up for "basic infrastructure for business outside these zones" that "can be poor".
Mission 10

Three kinds of zone

  • Free-trade zone: goods are stored, handled and re-exported without customs duties. "Basic free zones focused on facilitating trade logistics are most common in developed countries".
  • Export-processing zone (EPZ): factories import parts or cloth duty-free, process or assemble them, and export the products.
  • Special economic zone: the broad term; it can cover a whole city, as in Shenzhen.
  • Don't confuse a free-trade zone (a place) with a free-trade agreement between countries.

Zones spread

  • In 2019 there were about 5,400 zones in 147 economies, up from about 4,000 five years earlier, and more than 500 more planned.
  • They are part of "increasing competition for internationally mobile investment".
  • "SEZs are traditionally big employers of women, with about 60 per cent female employees on average".
Mission 10

Case study: Bangladesh's EPZs

  • Bangladesh opened its first EPZ in Chattogram in 1983.
  • In June 2025, 533,527 Bangladeshis worked in its 8 EPZs and one economic zone.
  • These zones exported US$8.22 billion of goods in 2024–25: 17.03% of Bangladesh's exports.
  • About 56% of the workers are women.
Mission 10

Case study: Shenzhen

  • Shenzhen was designated a special economic zone in August 1980, next to Hong Kong.
  • By the end of 2024 it had 17.98 million permanent residents.
  • Its exports have ranked first among mainland Chinese cities for 32 consecutive years.
  • UNCTAD's "development ladder": as countries industrialize, their zones move from basic assembly to high-tech and services.
Mission 10

Do zones work?

  • Benefits: jobs, exports and investment, especially where the rest of the economy lacks infrastructure.
  • Limits: "the performance of many zones remains below expectations".
  • "Too many zones operate as enclaves with limited impact beyond their confines".
  • SEZs "are neither a precondition nor a guarantee" of higher foreign investment.

Special economic zones, free-trade zones and export-processing zones offer duty-free imports, tax breaks and infrastructure. Combined with lower wages, they draw assembly to developing countries, while design and finance stay in the core: the international division of labor.

  • It raised food supply and population, drew workers to cities, created a working class and middle class, and pushed investors to seek raw materials and markets, feeding colonialism and imperialism.

Mission 2

  • Five sectors: primary (extract), secondary (make), tertiary (serve), quaternary (knowledge), quinary (top decisions).
  • As countries develop, workers move from primary to secondary to tertiary jobs; quaternary and quinary work clusters in core countries and big cities.

Mission 3

  • Weber's least cost theory: transport cost (weight-losing → near resources; weight-gaining → near markets), then labor savings.
  • Break-of-bulk points and shipping containers cut transfer costs; manufacturing is concentrated in the core and the semiperiphery, with little in the periphery.

Mission 4

  • Economic measures: GDP, GNP/GNI per capita, sectoral structure, formal and informal work, income distribution. Social measures: fertility, infant mortality, health care, literacy, energy use.
  • The HDI combines health, education and income to compare states; the GII measures gender inequality.

Mission 5

  • Women's roles change with development (a U-shape in participation), and more women work, but wages and opportunities are not yet equal.
  • Microloans, mostly to women, bring modest gains; they are a tool, not a cure.
  • Fair trade sets a minimum price, but only about 27% of reported certified coffee was sold on Fairtrade terms in 2024.

Mission 6

  • Rostow: five stages that every country may pass through. Dependency theory: links to richer countries keep poorer ones poor.
  • World system: core, semiperiphery, periphery. Commodity dependence: more than 60% of exports from raw materials makes income volatile.

Mission 7

  • Complementarity and comparative advantage (lower opportunity cost, not just fewer workers) are the basis of trade.
  • Neoliberal policies built the WTO, EU and Mercosur; OPEC coordinates oil supply; tariffs and other government initiatives at all scales can help or harm development.

Mission 8

  • Debt crises link countries: one default hits lenders worldwide. The IMF lends with conditions, and its biggest decisions can be blocked by the USA.
  • Microlending offers development "from below", at the household scale.

Mission 9

  • Outsourcing and restructuring cut factory jobs in the core (deindustrialization) and added them in newly industrialized countries.
  • Post-Fordism: just-in-time, economies of scale, agglomeration, multiplier effects, growth poles, services and high tech.

Mission 10

  • Outside the core, governments created SEZs, free-trade zones and export-processing zones to attract investment and exports (5,400 zones in 147 economies by 2019).
  • This built an international division of labor: lower-paid assembly in developing countries, higher-paid design, finance and marketing in the core.

Mission 11

  • Sustainable development policies tackle resource depletion, mass consumption, pollution and climate change.
  • The SDGs measure progress with indicators, for example on small-scale finance (account ownership, microfinance) and public transportation (access in cities).

Mission 12

  • Ecotourism brings income to threatened natural areas, which can fund protection and local jobs.
  • Rwanda: US$1,500 permits, small groups, 10% of park revenue shared; gorillas recovered under protection, but with trade-offs in access and land.
