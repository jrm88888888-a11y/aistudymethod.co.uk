Mission 1

Mission 1: What is a Christian's life actually for, and which purpose matters most?

Make a guess: What is a Christian's life actually for, and which purpose matters most?

Mission 1

Three purposes

  • Christians believe God made humans on purpose: life has a goal (Greek telos).
  • Three purposes stand out:
  • 1. To glorify God and have a personal relationship with him.
  • 2. To prepare for judgement: to live now in the light of eternity.
  • 3. To bring about God's kingdom on earth: justice, peace and love in this world.
  • Most Christians hold all three. The debate is about their relative importance: which comes first, and which shapes the others.
Mission 1

To glorify God

  • Glory (Hebrew kavod, "weight"; Greek doxa, "radiance"): God's worth made visible.
  • Humans cannot add to God's glory; they reflect and acknowledge it, like a mirror reflecting light.
  • God made people "for my glory" (Isaiah 43:7).
  • "Whether therefore you eat or drink, or whatever you do, do all to the glory of God." (1 Corinthians 10:31)
  • So glorifying God is not only worship on Sunday: it is every activity done for God.
  • "Let your light shine before men, that they may see your good works and glorify your Father who is in heaven." (Matthew 5:16)
Mission 1

A personal relationship with God

  • "This is eternal life, that they should know you, the only true God, and him whom you sent, Jesus Christ." (John 17:3)
  • To know God here means to be in relationship with him, not just to know facts.
  • Christians express this in prayer, worship, the sacraments and reading scripture.
  • Jesus calls God "Father" and invites followers to do the same (Matthew 6:9).
Mission 1Read the source

Made for God

> "Thou madest us for Thyself, and our heart is restless, until it repose in Thee." (Augustine, Confessions I.1, trans. Pusey)

> "Q. What is the chief end of man? A. Man's chief end is to glorify God, and to enjoy him for ever." (Westminster Shorter Catechism, 1647, Q. 1)

> "Q. Why did God make you? A. God made me to know Him, to love Him, and to serve Him in this world, and to be happy with Him forever in the next." (Baltimore Catechism, 1885, Q. 6)

Q1 Which two purposes does the Westminster answer join together?

Show answer

Glorifying God and enjoying him: worship and relationship.

Q2 What does Augustine's "restless" heart suggest about life without God?

Show answer

It is incomplete; humans are made for God and find rest only in him.

Mission 1

To prepare for judgement

  • "It is appointed for men to die once, and after this, judgement" (Hebrews 9:27).
  • "We must all be revealed before the judgement seat of Christ" (2 Corinthians 5:10).
  • The Sheep and the Goats (Matthew 25:31–46): people are judged by how they treated the hungry, the stranger and the prisoner (developed in another lesson).
  • Catholic teaching: a particular judgement at death and a last judgement at the end; life is a time to grow in grace, with confession and penance.
  • Many Protestants: believers are saved by faith; judgement assesses how they lived and brings reward, not salvation itself (1 Corinthians 3:12–15).
  • So preparing for judgement gives life seriousness: choices have eternal weight.
Mission 1

To bring about God's kingdom on earth

  • Jesus' message: "God's Kingdom is at hand! Repent, and believe in the Good News." (Mark 1:15)
  • The Lord's Prayer: "Let your Kingdom come. Let your will be done on earth as it is in heaven." (Matthew 6:10)
  • The kingdom is God's reign: justice for the poor, peace, healing (Luke 4:18).
  • Already and not yet: begun in Jesus, completed when he returns (Revelation 21:1–4).
  • Social Gospel (Walter Rauschenbusch, 1861–1918, A Theology for the Social Gospel, 1917): Christians should transform society's structures.
  • Liberation theology and Catholic social teaching: work for justice is part of faith.
  • Debate: humans build the kingdom, or witness to a kingdom only God can bring? Many evangelicals and Orthodox stress the second.
Quick check
?A Christian campaigns against unfair trade because "the Lord's Prayer asks for God's will to be done on earth". Which purpose of life is she mainly expressing?
Mission 1Diversity

Different emphases

  • Reformed: the glory of God is the ultimate purpose (Westminster Q. 1); all else serves it.
  • Catholic: to know, love and serve God, and to share his happiness in heaven (the beatific vision); judgement and social teaching both matter.
  • Orthodox: theosis: growing into union with God, chiefly through worship and sacraments.
  • Evangelical: a personal relationship with Jesus; sharing the gospel; readiness for judgement.
  • Liberal and liberation: the kingdom of justice now; less stress on judgement as threat.
  • Many Christians say the three are one purpose seen from different sides: love of God (glory, relationship) and love of neighbour (kingdom), lived before God (judgement) (Matthew 22:37–39).
Mission 1Evaluate

Is glorifying God the most important purpose?

  • Claim: Glorifying God is the most important purpose of Christian life.
  • For: God is the source and goal of everything (Romans 11:36); Westminster Q. 1 puts it first; the first commandment is love of God (Matthew 22:37–38).
  • For: If glory is "everything done for God", it includes the other two.
  • Against: Jesus' own preaching centred on the kingdom (Mark 1:15); faith without action for others is empty (Matthew 25).
  • Against: Without judgement, choices lose their eternal seriousness; many find this the strongest motive.
  • Against: A relationship with God may be more basic: glory is the overflow of knowing him (John 17:3).
  • Judge it by: Which purpose explains the others? Which did Jesus stress? Which motive is most worthy: love, fear or justice?
Mission 1Worked answer

Building an evaluation

Question: "Bringing about God's kingdom is the most important purpose of Christian life." Evaluate this view.

  • Argument: The kingdom was the centre of Jesus' preaching (Mark 1:15) and Christians pray for it daily (Matthew 6:10).
  • Counter: But Jesus called love of God the first commandment (Matthew 22:37–38), which fits glorifying God.
  • Weigh it: The kingdom is God's reign, so working for it already is glorifying God; the purposes overlap rather than compete.
  • Judgement: reached by a reason, e.g. "On balance, … is more convincing, because…".
  • AO2 tip: weigh the arguments against each other and reach a reasoned judgement; don't just list views.

Make a guess: If Christians will rise from the dead, will they get back the same body, or a new kind?

Mission 2

What is the soul?

  • Hebrew Bible: a human is a "living soul" (Hebrew nephesh): dust given God's breath (Genesis 2:7). The soul is the whole living person, not a separate part.
  • Greek thought (Plato, Phaedo): the soul is immortal and is freed from the body at death.
  • Christian dualism: the soul is created by God, spiritual, and survives death; Jesus distinguishes those who "kill the body" from the soul they cannot kill (Matthew 10:28).
  • Aquinas (1225–1274): the soul is "the form of the body" (Summa Theologiae I, q. 76, a. 1): it makes the body a living human. A soul without its body is not a complete person.
  • Some modern Christians are physicalists: no separate soul; God re-creates the whole person (e.g. Nancey Murphy, Bodies and Souls, or Spirited Bodies?, 2006).
Mission 2

Resurrection, not just immortality

  • Christian hope is the resurrection of the body, not only the survival of a soul.
  • The Apostles' Creed in Latin says carnis resurrectionem: "the resurrection of the flesh".
  • Roots: "Many of those who sleep in the dust of the earth will awake" (Daniel 12:2).
  • Jesus' resurrection is the "firstfruits": the pattern of what will happen to believers (1 Corinthians 15:20).
  • Traditional timeline: at death the soul goes to God; at the last day the dead are raised and soul and body are reunited.
  • The key question: what will the raised body be like?
Mission 2

Augustine: the resurrection of the flesh

  • Augustine (354–430), City of God (413–426), Book XXII; also the Enchiridion.
  • The same flesh will rise: whatever was lost from the body, in life or after death, will be restored (XXII.21).
  • Even a body burnt, eaten or scattered is not beyond the Creator's power: "not a hair of its head shall perish" (XXII.20–21; Luke 21:18).
  • Bodies will be perfected: deformities and blemishes removed (XXII.19); everyone raised at the age of full maturity, about thirty, Christ's age (XXII.15).
  • Sex remains: women rise as women, though there is no marriage (XXII.17; Matthew 22:30).
  • Why it mattered: against those (e.g. Platonists such as Porphyry) who said the soul must escape all bodies (XXII.26), Augustine held that the body is good and part of the person.
Mission 2Read the source

Augustine's "spiritual flesh"

> "The flesh shall then be spiritual, and subject to the spirit, but still flesh, not spirit" (Augustine, City of God XXII.21, trans. Dods)

> "Whatever, therefore, has been taken from the body, either during life or after death, shall be restored to it" (City of God XXII.21, trans. Dods)

Q1 For Augustine, does "spiritual body" mean a body made of spirit?

Show answer

No: still flesh, but wholly subject to the spirit.

Q2 Why does Augustine insist the same material is restored?

Show answer

So that the risen person is really the same person, and nothing God made is lost.

Mission 2Read the source

1 Corinthians 15

> "The body is sown perishable; it is raised imperishable. It is sown in dishonour; it is raised in glory. It is sown in weakness; it is raised in power. It is sown a natural body; it is raised a spiritual body." (1 Corinthians 15:42–44, WEBBE)

> "flesh and blood can't inherit God's Kingdom" (1 Corinthians 15:50, WEBBE)

> "For this perishable body must become imperishable, and this mortal must put on immortality." (1 Corinthians 15:53, WEBBE)

  • Paul's image (15:36–38): a seed is sown and a plant rises. Continuity, but transformation.
  • 15:51–52: "we will all be changed, in a moment, in the twinkling of an eye".

Q1 List Paul's four contrasts.

Show answer

Perishable/imperishable; dishonour/glory; weakness/power; natural/spiritual.

Q2 Does the seed image suggest the same body, or a different one?

Show answer

Both: the same seed, but a body as different as a plant.

Mission 2

Spiritual resurrection

  • Many read Paul as teaching a spiritual resurrection: the person is raised in a new, non-physical form fitted for God's presence.
  • Evidence: "flesh and blood can't inherit" (15:50); "a spiritual body" (15:44); the seed is not the plant (15:37).
  • Origen (c. 185–c. 254): the risen body keeps the form (pattern) of the earthly body, not its matter.
  • On this view identity lies in the person (the pattern or soul), not in the same particles.
  • Others (e.g. N. T. Wright, The Resurrection of the Son of God, 2003) read "spiritual body" (sōma pneumatikon) as a body animated by God's Spirit: physical, but transformed. Augustine read it similarly.
Quick check
?Which phrase from 1 Corinthians 15 is most often used to support a spiritual rather than a fleshly resurrection?
Mission 2

The model: Jesus' risen body

  • Physical: "Touch me and see, for a spirit doesn't have flesh and bones" (Luke 24:39); he eats fish (Luke 24:42–43).
  • Wounds remain: Thomas is invited to touch them (John 20:27).
  • Different: he appears in a room with locked doors (John 20:19, 26); disciples do not at first recognise him (John 20:14; 21:4).
  • Christians see this as the pattern: continuity (same person, marks of his life) and transformation (no longer limited as before).
  • Augustine's readers stress the physical; spiritual-resurrection readers stress the change.
Mission 2Diversity

Christian views today

  • Catholic: at death the soul is judged and separated from the body; at the last day God reunites it with its own, transformed body (Catechism 997–1001). The Fourth Lateran Council (1215) taught that all will rise with their own bodies.
  • Orthodox: the same hope; the body sanctified now through the sacraments; icons and relics honour the body's destiny.
  • Many Protestants: the soul is with Christ at death (Luke 23:43; Philippians 1:23); bodily resurrection at Christ's return.
  • Soul sleep / conditional immortality (e.g. Seventh-day Adventists, Christadelphians): no immortal soul; the dead "sleep" until resurrection.
  • Liberal Christians: may hold the soul's survival or a spiritual resurrection, reading bodily language as symbolic.
Mission 2Evaluate

Must the resurrected body be physical?

  • Claim: The resurrected body must be physical, the same flesh restored.
  • For: Jesus' risen body could be touched and ate food (Luke 24:39–43); the empty tomb implies the same body.
  • For: Personal identity: without the same body, is it really me (or a copy)?
  • For: The goodness of the body: God made matter good; the creeds say "resurrection of the flesh".
  • Against: 1 Corinthians 15:50: flesh and blood cannot inherit the kingdom; the seed is not the plant.
  • Against: Practical puzzles (bodies decomposed, atoms reused) led Origen and others to locate identity in form, not matter.
  • Against: Jesus' body passed through locked doors: not ordinary physicality.
  • Judge it by: What makes a person the same person? How literally should Paul's and the Gospels' language be read? What did the early Church mean by "flesh"?

Christians hold together continuity (the same person) and transformation (a glorified body). Augustine and spiritual-resurrection readings differ on how much of the old body is kept.

Mission 3

Three ways to read the last things

  • Eschatology: Christian teaching on the "last things": death, judgement, heaven, hell (and, for Catholics, purgatory).
  • Christians agree that God judges and that life with God is the goal. They differ on what kind of reality these words name.
  • Three readings, from most literal to least:
  • Places: real locations a person goes to after death, with physical and emotional joy, punishment or cleansing.
  • Spiritual states: not locations, but conditions of the person after death: with God, cut off from God, or being purified.
  • Symbols: pictures of a person's moral and spiritual life on Earth, not of anything after death.
  • Your spec's words: physical, spiritual or psychological realities. They map onto the three readings above.
Mission 3

Judgement: at death, or at the end of time?

  • Particular judgement: each person is judged at death. "It is appointed for men to die once, and after this, judgement" (Hebrews 9:27, WEBBE). Jesus to the thief: "today you will be with me in Paradise" (Luke 23:43, WEBBE).
  • Last (general) judgement: all people judged together when Christ returns (Matthew 25:31–32; Revelation 20:12).
  • Catholic teaching holds both: the soul's destiny is fixed at death; the final judgement makes it public and includes the risen body. Orthodox teaching also holds both, while praying that the departed may still be helped before the last judgement.
  • Some Protestants (e.g. Seventh-day Adventists) hold that the dead "sleep" until the last judgement: no judgement at death.
  • Realised reading: judgement happens now, in how people respond to Christ. "He who doesn't believe has been judged already" (John 3:18, WEBBE).
  • Physical, spiritual or psychological: a final judgement of risen bodies is physical; a verdict on the soul at death is spiritual; judgement as conscience now is psychological.
Mission 3

Heaven and hell as places

  • Heaven: Jesus speaks of his Father's house with "many homes" (John 14:2). Revelation pictures a new heaven and a new earth (Revelation 21:1).
  • With the resurrection of the body (see Mission 2), heaven is often understood as a real, bodily life in a renewed creation.
  • Hell: Jesus warns of Gehenna, the valley of Hinnom outside Jerusalem, remembered for child sacrifice (Jeremiah 7:31), used as a picture of punishment (Mark 9:47–48).
  • The rich man and Lazarus (Luke 16:19–31): two places after death, with "a great gulf fixed" between them (16:26).
  • Many Christians through history read these literally: actual places, actual fire.
Mission 3

Heaven and hell as spiritual states

  • Heaven as the beatific vision: seeing God directly. "Now we see in a mirror, dimly, but then face to face" (1 Corinthians 13:12, WEBBE). Aquinas made this the final happiness of the soul.
  • Pope John Paul II, general audiences (July 1999): heaven is not a place in the clouds but a living relationship with the Trinity; hell is not so much a place as a state (paraphrase).
  • Hell as self-exclusion: the Catechism of the Catholic Church (1992) calls it "definitive self-exclusion from communion with God" (CCC 1033).
  • So the pain of hell is above all loss of God, chosen freely; fire is an image of that loss.
  • These readings still treat heaven and hell as real after death; they deny only that they are locations.

Mission 3

Purgatory

  • Catholic teaching: those who die in God's grace but are not yet fully purified undergo purification after death, so as to enter heaven (CCC 1030–1031, paraphrase).
  • Defined at the Councils of Florence (1439) and Trent (1563). The living may help by prayer and the Mass offered for the dead.
  • Earlier roots: Augustine accepted that some punishments after death may purify (City of God XXI.13, XXI.24).
  • Place or state? Medieval art and Dante's Purgatorio (early 1300s) show a mountain; recent popes describe a process: Benedict XVI, Spe Salvi (2007, §47), speaks of the encounter with Christ as the fire that burns and heals (paraphrase).
  • Protestants reject purgatory: Luther attacked indulgences in 1517 and by about 1530 rejected purgatory itself as unscriptural; the Church of England's Article XXII calls the "Romish Doctrine concerning Purgatory" "a fond thing, vainly invented".
  • Orthodox Christians pray for the dead but do not accept the Western doctrine of purgatory.
Mission 3

Symbols of life here and now

  • Realised eschatology: C. H. Dodd (1884–1973), The Parables of the Kingdom (1935): the kingdom has already come in Jesus.
  • "This is eternal life, that they should know you, the only true God" (John 17:3, WEBBE): eternal life as a quality of life now.
  • Rudolf Bultmann (1884–1976), "New Testament and Mythology" (1941): heaven above and hell below belong to an ancient picture of the world; demythologise them to find their meaning for how a person lives now.
  • D. Z. Phillips (1934–2006), Death and Immortality (1970): eternal life is not more life after death but a way of living: self-giving love now.
  • On this reading: heaven = wholeness and love; hell = selfishness and alienation; purgatory = moral growth.
  • This is the psychological reading.
  • Critics: most Christians, and the creeds, mean a real life after death; symbols alone may empty the hope of the resurrection.
Quick check
?A theologian says: "To be in hell is to be locked into self-centred living; heaven is the freedom of self-giving love, here and now." Which reading is this?
Mission 3Evaluate

Are heaven, hell and purgatory best read as symbols?

  • Claim: Heaven, hell and purgatory are best understood as symbols of life on Earth.
  • For: The Bible's images (fire, worm, gates, a city of gold) are pictures; reading them literally creates puzzles (where is heaven?).
  • For: Jesus says the kingdom "is within you" (Luke 17:21, WEBBE); John speaks of eternal life now (John 17:3).
  • Against: Jesus, Paul and the creeds speak of real resurrection and real judgement; a symbol-only view changes the faith, not just its language.
  • Against: The spiritual-state reading avoids the literal puzzles without denying life after death.
  • Judgement criteria: Faithfulness to scripture and creeds · coherence with a modern picture of the universe · justice for those who suffer in this life · whether hope must reach beyond death.
Mission check

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Three purposes: glorify God and know him personally; prepare for judgement; bring about God's kingdom on earth.
  • Traditions differ in emphasis (Reformed glory; Catholic beatific vision; Orthodox theosis; evangelical relationship; liberal and liberation kingdom).

Mission 2

  • Soul: Hebrew whole-person view; Greek immortal soul; Christian dualism (soul survives); Aquinas: soul as form of the body.
  • Augustine: the same flesh restored, perfected, and spiritual in being wholly subject to the spirit (City of God XXII).
  • 1 Corinthians 15:42–44, 50–54: sown perishable, raised imperishable; "spiritual body"; flesh and blood cannot inherit; all will be changed.

Mission 3

  • Christians read heaven, hell and purgatory as places, spiritual states after death, or symbols of life now.
  • Judgement: at death (particular), at the end of time (final), or both; some see it as already happening (realised).
  • In your spec's words: physical, spiritual or psychological realities.
