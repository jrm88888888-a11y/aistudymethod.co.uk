Mission 1

Mission 1: Can a strong acid have a higher pH than a weak acid?

Make a guess: Can a strong acid have a higher pH than a weak acid?

Mission 1

Strong and weak

  • Strong acids and bases dissociate completely in water. Weak ones dissociate only slightly.
  • Strong acids: HCl, HNO3, H2SO4 (also HBr, HI). Strong bases: NaOH, KOH.
  • Weak: carboxylic acids (CH3COOH, HCOOH), NH3 and amines.
  • Strong: HNO3 → H⁺ + NO3⁻. Weak: CH3COOH ⇌ CH3COO⁻ + H⁺ (lies far to the left).
  • Weak base: NH3 + H2O ⇌ NH4⁺ + OH⁻, so [OH⁻] is far below the NH3 concentration.
  • Later, Ka puts a number on how weak an acid is.
Mission 1

Strength is not concentration

  • Concentration: amount of acid per unit volume. Strength: the extent of dissociation.
  • Diluting a strong acid raises its pH, but it stays strong.
  • At equal concentration, a weak acid has a higher pH and lower conductivity than a strong one.
  • At equal concentration, a weak base has a lower pH than a strong base.
  • A weak acid still neutralises the same amount of base: as H⁺ is used up, more of the acid dissociates.

?A solution of nitric acid, HNO3(aq), has a concentration of 0.0010 mol dm⁻³. Which statement about this solution is correct?
Quick check
?Solution X is 0.0010 mol dm⁻³ KOH(aq). Solution Y is 2.0 mol dm⁻³ NH3(aq). Which statement is correct?
Mission 1

Which way does the equilibrium lie?

  • An acid–base equilibrium lies on the side of the weaker acid and weaker base.
  • The stronger the acid, the weaker its conjugate base.
  • HNO3 + H2O → H3O⁺ + NO3⁻ is effectively complete: NO3⁻ barely accepts H⁺.
  • HCOOH + H2O ⇌ HCOO⁻ + H3O⁺ lies left: H3O⁺ is a stronger acid than HCOOH.
Quick check
?Methanoic acid is a weak acid: HCOOH(aq) + H2O(l) ⇌ HCOO⁻(aq) + H3O⁺(aq). Which statement is correct?
Mission check

?Which statement about solutions P and Q is correct?
  • P is 5.0 × 10⁻⁴ mol dm⁻³ HCl(aq). Q is 0.50 mol dm⁻³ CH3COOH(aq), measured pH 2.53.

Yes. A dilute enough strong acid has a higher pH than a concentrated weak one. Strength and concentration are different things.

Mission 2: Why can an antacid tablet make you burp?

Make a guess: Why can an antacid tablet make you burp?

Mission 2

Oxides and hydroxides: salt + water

  • In neutralisation, a base accepts the H⁺ from an acid.
  • Acid + metal hydroxide → salt + water: KOH + HNO3 → KNO3 + H2O.
  • Acid + metal oxide → salt + water: MgO + 2HCl → MgCl2 + H2O.
  • Ionic: H⁺(aq) + OH⁻(aq) → H2O(l); for a solid oxide, MgO(s) + 2H⁺(aq) → Mg²⁺(aq) + H2O(l).
  • A salt forms when the acid's H is replaced by a metal ion or another cation, such as NH4⁺.
Mission 2

Carbonates and hydrogencarbonates: CO2 too

  • Acid + carbonate or hydrogencarbonate → salt + water + carbon dioxide.
  • CO3²⁻ accepts two H⁺; HCO3⁻ accepts one.
  • K2CO3 + 2HNO3 → 2KNO3 + H2O + CO2.
  • Ionic: CO3²⁻ + 2H⁺ → H2O + CO2. HCO3⁻ + H⁺ → H2O + CO2.
  • Observation: fizzing as CO2 escapes; a solid carbonate disappears.
Mission 2

Ammonia, amines; and metals

  • Ammonia or an amine + acid → a salt only; no water forms.
  • NH3 + HNO3 → NH4NO3. CH3CH2NH2 + HCl → CH3CH2NH3Cl.
  • The N lone pair takes the H⁺; there is no OH⁻ or O²⁻ to make water.
  • Metal + acid → salt + hydrogen: Mg + 2HCl → MgCl2 + H2. That is redox, not neutralisation.
  • Ionic: Mg(s) + 2H⁺(aq) → Mg²⁺(aq) + H2(g).
Quick check
?Which reaction produces a salt and water, with no other product?
Mission 2

Group 2 compounds as bases

  • Group 2 oxides are basic. With water: CaO + H2O → Ca(OH)2 (an alkaline solution).
  • With dilute acid: CaO + 2HCl → CaCl2 + H2O.
  • Ca(OH)2 neutralises acid soils in agriculture. Mg(OH)2 and CaCO3 are antacids.
Mission 2

The parent acid and base of a salt

  • A salt's anion comes from its parent acid; its cation from its parent base.
  • KNO3: parent acid HNO3, parent base KOH.
  • (NH4)2SO4: parent acid H2SO4, parent base NH3.
  • HCOOK (potassium methanoate): parent acid HCOOH, parent base KOH.
  • Parents are the neutral acid and base, not the ions in the salt.
Quick check
?What are the parent acid and parent base of ammonium methanoate, HCOONH4?
Mission 2

The pH curve: strong acid, strong base

  • A preview: pH curves get a full mission later.
  • Flask: 25.0 cm³ of 0.100 mol dm⁻³ HCl. Burette: 0.100 mol dm⁻³ NaOH.
  • Starts at the flask's pH, 1.00, and rises slowly.
  • Close to 25.0 cm³, pH jumps from about 3 to about 11.
  • Equivalence point: amounts match the equation; here pH 7 (at 298 K, 25 °C), mid-jump.
  • After it, excess NaOH builds up: pH rises slowly towards 13 (about 12.5 at double the volume).
Mission check

?Baking powder contains sodium hydrogencarbonate and a solid acid. When the cake batter gets wet, the two react. Why does the cake rise?

Antacid tablets with CaCO3 do the same: CaCO3 + 2HCl → CaCl2 + H2O + CO2. The CO2 gas is what you burp up.

Mission 3: When iron rusts, does it gain or lose electrons?

Mission 3

  • Oxidation and reduction always happen together: a redox reaction.
  • The electron and oxidation-number definitions work for every redox reaction.
  • Memory aid: OIL RIG, Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain (of electrons).
Mission 3

Oxidation numbers: the rules

  • An uncombined element is 0: Fe, O2, Cl2.
  • A simple ion's oxidation number is its charge: Fe³⁺ is +3.
  • Usually O is −2 and H is +1. F is −1 in all its compounds.
  • Exceptions: O is −1 in peroxides (H2O2); H is −1 in metal hydrides (NaH).
  • In compounds, group 1 metals are +1; group 2 metals, +2.
  • The numbers add up to the charge on the species: 0 for a compound.
Mission 3

Worked examples

  • SO4²⁻: x + 4(−2) = −2, so S is +6.
  • H2S: 2(+1) + x = 0, so S is −2.
  • Cr2O7²⁻: 2x + 7(−2) = −2, so 2x = +12 and each Cr is +6.
  • Rust, Fe2O3: 2x + 3(−2) = 0, so each Fe is +3.
  • Write the sign first: +3, not 3+.
Mission 3

Rusting: where the electrons go

  • Simplified: 4Fe(s) + 3O2(g) → 2Fe2O3(s).
  • Fe: 0 → +3. Each Fe atom loses 3 electrons: oxidised.
  • O: 0 → −2. Each O atom gains 2 electrons: reduced.
  • Electrons move from the species oxidised to the species reduced.
  • Metals generally lose electrons to form positive ions; non-metals gain them to form negative ions.
  • The same ideas hold across the s-, p- and d-blocks: Mg → Mg²⁺, Cl2 → 2Cl⁻, Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺.
Mission 3

Oxidising and reducing agents

  • The oxidising agent takes electrons, so it is itself reduced.
  • The reducing agent gives electrons, so it is itself oxidised.
  • Agents are reactants, never products.
  • Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu. Zn: 0 → +2, oxidised: the reducing agent. Cu²⁺: +2 → 0, reduced: the oxidising agent.
Quick check
?Chlorine reacts with aqueous iron(II) ions: 2Fe²⁺(aq) + Cl2(aq) → 2Fe³⁺(aq) + 2Cl⁻(aq). Which species is the oxidising agent in this reaction?
Mission 3

Metals with acids

  • Metal + acid → salt + hydrogen. Write full equations.
  • Mg(s) + 2HCl(aq) → MgCl2(aq) + H2(g)
  • 2Al(s) + 3H2SO4(aq) → Al2(SO4)3(aq) + 3H2(g)
  • Fe(s) + H2SO4(aq) → FeSO4(aq) + H2(g)
  • The metal is oxidised (Al: 0 → +3); hydrogen is reduced (+1 → 0).
  • The other atoms (Cl, S, O) keep their oxidation numbers: they are spectators.
Mission 3

Half-equations

  • A half-equation shows the oxidation or the reduction alone, with the electrons written in.
  • Oxidation: electrons are products. Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻.
  • Reduction: electrons are reactants. Cl2 + 2e⁻ → 2Cl⁻.
  • Both atoms and charge must balance.
Mission 3

Balancing in acid: worked example

Cr2O7²⁻ → Cr³⁺ in acidic solution:

  • Balance Cr: Cr2O7²⁻ → 2Cr³⁺.
  • Balance O with H2O: add 7H2O on the right.
  • Balance H with H⁺: add 14H⁺ on the left.
  • Balance charge with e⁻: left +12, right +6, so add 6e⁻ on the left.
  • Cr2O7²⁻ + 14H⁺ + 6e⁻ → 2Cr³⁺ + 7H2O. Check: 2 Cr each fall by 3 (+6 → +3), so 6e⁻.
  • Same steps: MnO4⁻ + 8H⁺ + 5e⁻ → Mn²⁺ + 4H2O (Mn +7 → +2).
Mission 3

Combining half-equations

  • Scale so the electrons lost equal the electrons gained, then add and cancel them.
  • Oxidation: Fe²⁺ → Fe³⁺ + e⁻. Multiply by 6 to give 6e⁻.
  • Add to the dichromate(VI) half-equation from the earlier screen; cancel 6e⁻:
  • Cr2O7²⁻ + 14H⁺ + 6Fe²⁺ → 2Cr³⁺ + 7H2O + 6Fe³⁺
  • Check charge: left −2 + 14 + 12 = +24; right +6 + 18 = +24.
  • Electrons never appear in the overall equation.
Quick check
?In acidic solution, MnO4⁻(aq) is reduced to Mn²⁺(aq) and Fe²⁺(aq) is oxidised to Fe³⁺(aq). In the balanced overall redox equation, what amount of Fe²⁺ reacts with 1 mol of MnO4⁻?
Mission 3

Disproportionation

  • Disproportionation: the same element in one species is oxidised and reduced at the same time.
  • Chlorine in water treatment: Cl2 + H2O ⇌ HCl + HClO. Cl: 0 → −1 and 0 → +1.
  • Cold, dilute NaOH makes bleach: Cl2 + 2NaOH → NaCl + NaClO + H2O. Cl: 0 → −1 and +1.
Quick check
?Which reaction is a disproportionation?
Mission check

?In the thermite reaction, aluminium reacts with iron(III) oxide: Fe2O3(s) + 2Al(s) → Al2O3(s) + 2Fe(l). What happens to the iron?

Rusting: Fe 0 → +3, so iron loses electrons. In thermite, iron gains them back. Oxidation numbers tell you which way.

Mission 4

Mission 4: How can nitrogen be −3 in ammonia but +5 in nitric acid?

Make a guess: How can nitrogen be −3 in ammonia but +5 in nitric acid?

Mission 4

What an oxidation number is

  • The oxidation number is the charge an atom would have if all its bonds were ionic.
  • Shared electrons are given to the more electronegative atom.
  • So an atom's oxidation number depends on what it is bonded to.
  • Atoms in an element are 0: identical atoms, so neither takes the shared electrons.
  • Write the sign first: +2, −1. (Ion charges are written 2+, 1−.)
Mission 4

The rules

  • In a compound the oxidation numbers sum to zero; in an ion, to the ion's charge.
  • O is −2, except −1 in peroxides, such as H2O2 and Na2O2.
  • H is +1, except −1 in metal hydrides, such as NaH and CaH2.
  • Group 1 metals +1, group 2 metals +2, fluorine always −1.
  • Find the unknown atom last.
Mission 4

Worked examples

  • NH3: x + 3(+1) = 0, so N is −3. N is more electronegative than H.
  • HNO3: +1 + x + 3(−2) = 0, so N is +5. O is more electronegative than N.
  • SO4²⁻: x + 4(−2) = −2, so S is +6.
  • H2O2: 2(+1) + 2x = 0, so O is −1 (a peroxide).
  • CaH2: +2 + 2x = 0, so H is −1 (a metal hydride).
Quick check

Calculate

Mission 4

Oxidation numbers in names

  • A Roman numeral gives the oxidation number of the element before it.
  • FeCl2 is iron(II) chloride; FeCl3 is iron(III) chloride.
  • KMnO4: +1 + x + 4(−2) = 0, so Mn is +7: potassium manganate(VII).
  • Oxyanions: ClO⁻ chlorate(I), ClO2⁻ chlorate(III), ClO3⁻ chlorate(V); Cr2O7²⁻ dichromate(VI).
  • "Nitrate" and "sulfate" with no numeral mean NO3⁻ and SO4²⁻.
  • Traditional names: nitrite NO2⁻ is nitrate(III); sulfite SO3²⁻ is sulfate(IV).
Mission 4

Formulae from names

  • Use the numeral as the oxidation number, then make the total zero.
  • Iron(III) sulfate: Fe +3, SO4²⁻. 2(+3) + 3(−2) = 0, so Fe2(SO4)3.
  • Vanadium(V) oxide: V +5, O −2. 2(+5) + 5(−2) = 0, so V2O5.
  • Sodium chlorate(I): Cl +1, so the ion is ClO⁻ and the salt is NaClO.
  • The numeral is never a count of atoms.

Quick check

Mission check

?Chlorine is −1 in the chloride ion, Cl⁻, but +1 in the hypochlorite ion, ClO⁻ (in bleach). Which explains the +1?

Same element, different partners. Bonded to less electronegative atoms (N to H) it is negative; bonded to more electronegative ones (N to O) it is positive.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Strong: completely dissociated; weak: only slightly. Strength is not concentration.
  • Equal concentrations: weak acid, higher pH. Different concentrations: anything can happen.
  • An acid–base equilibrium lies towards the weaker acid and weaker base.

Mission 2

  • Oxides and hydroxides: salt + water. Carbonates: salt + water + CO2. Ammonia and amines: salt only.
  • Metal + acid gives H2, but that is redox.

Mission 3

  • Oxidation = electron loss, oxidation number rises; reduction = electron gain, oxidation number falls.
  • Half-equations: balance atoms (H2O, H⁺ in acid), then charge with e⁻; scale so electrons cancel.
  • Oxidising agents gain electrons and are reduced; reducing agents lose electrons and are oxidised.

Mission 4

  • Oxidation number: the charge if all bonds were ionic, shared electrons to the more electronegative atom.
  • Sum to zero (compound) or to the charge (ion); O −1 in peroxides, H −1 in metal hydrides.
  • Roman numerals give the oxidation number: iron(III), chlorate(V), manganate(VII).
