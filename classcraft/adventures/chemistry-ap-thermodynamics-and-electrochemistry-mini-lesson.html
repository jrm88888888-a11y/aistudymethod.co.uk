Mission 1

Mission 1: Why does perfume spread across a room but never gather back into the bottle?

Make a guess: Why does perfume spread across a room but never gather back into the bottle?

Mission 1

What entropy measures

  • Entropy, S: increases when matter or energy becomes more dispersed.
  • More ways to arrange the particles and share out their energy: higher entropy.
  • Same conditions: gas > liquid > solid.
Mission 1

Predicting the sign of ΔS

  • Look first at moles of gas: more gas formed → ΔS positive; less gas → negative.
  • Melting, boiling, subliming: ΔS positive. Freezing, condensing: negative.
  • Dissolving an ionic solid: usually positive, as the ions spread through the water. Dissolving a gas: negative.
  • A gas expanding into a larger volume at constant temperature: positive.
  • Heating raises S: the spread of particle kinetic energies broadens.

Quick check
?A sample of neon gas in a sealed, rigid container is heated from 300 K to 600 K. According to kinetic molecular theory, which change in the neon atoms accounts for the increase in the entropy of the gas?
Mission 1

Calculating ΔS°

  • Standard molar entropies, S°, are absolute values: positive for every substance, elements included.
  • ΔS = ΣS(products) − ΣS(reactants), each times its coefficient.
  • Units: J/(molrxn·K).
  • Worked: CH4(g) + 2O2(g) → CO2(g) + 2H2O(l). S / J/(mol·K): CH4 186.3, O2 205.2, CO2 213.8, H2O(l) 70.0.
  • ΔS° = [213.8 + 2(70.0)] − [186.3 + 2(205.2)] = 353.8 − 596.7 = −242.9 J/(molrxn·K). As predicted: 3 mol of gas → 1 mol.

Mission check

?A hot metal block is pressed against an identical cold one. Energy flows from hot to cold until both are at the same temperature. It never flows back to make one block hot again. Why?

Perfume molecules or energy: spread out, they have vastly more possible arrangements. So entropy increases, and the reverse, gathering back, is overwhelmingly unlikely.

Mission 2: Why does limestone need a kiln at about 900 °C to turn into lime?

Make a guess: Why does limestone need a kiln at about 900 °C to turn into lime?

Mission 2

Two drives: enthalpy and entropy

  • Whether a process is thermodynamically favored can depend on both ΔH° and ΔS°.
  • Water freezing: exothermic, but entropy falls. It happens only below 0 °C.
  • Sodium nitrate dissolving: endothermic, but entropy rises. It happens at room temperature.
Mission 2

The surroundings count too (background)

  • A reaction also changes the entropy of its surroundings.
  • Heat released spreads into the surroundings: their entropy rises. Heat absorbed: it falls.
  • The same heat matters more at low temperature.
  • You won't calculate this. Gibbs energy (next) includes it for you, using only the system's ΔH and ΔS.
Mission 2

Gibbs energy

  • ΔG° = ΔH° − TΔS°. ° means standard states: gases at 1 atm (or 1 bar), solutions 1.0 M, pure solids and liquids.
  • ΔG° < 0: thermodynamically favored. ("Spontaneous" is avoided: it does not mean fast.)
  • ΔG says nothing about rate: a thermodynamically favored reaction can be immeasurably slow.
Mission 2

Worked example: limestone at two temperatures

  • CaCO3(s) → CaO(s) + CO2(g). ΔH° = +179.2 kJ/molrxn; ΔS° = +160.2 J/(molrxn·K) (see Mission 1).
  • Units first: ΔS° = +0.1602 kJ/(molrxn·K). T in kelvin.
  • 298 K: ΔG° = 179.2 − 298(0.1602) = 179.2 − 47.7 = +131.5 kJ/molrxn. Not thermodynamically favored.
  • 1200 K: ΔG° = 179.2 − 1200(0.1602) = 179.2 − 192.2 = −13.0 kJ/molrxn. Thermodynamically favored.
  • Assumes ΔH and ΔS stay the same at every temperature.
Quick check
?For C2H4(g) + H2(g) → C2H6(g), calculate ΔG at 298 K.
  • ΔH° = −136 kJ/molrxn and ΔS° = −120.7 J/(molrxn·K). Answers in kJ/molrxn.
Mission 2

When ΔG changes sign

ΔHΔSΔG negative
−+at all temperatures
+−at no temperature
++above T = ΔH/ΔS
−−below T = ΔH/ΔS
  • At the switch-over ΔG = 0, so T = ΔH/ΔS (ΔS in kJ).
  • Limestone: T = 179.2 ÷ 0.1602 = 1118.6 K, about 1119 K (845 °C).
  • −/+ needs no calculation (always favored); +/− likewise (never favored).

Mission 2

ΔG° from ΔG°f

  • ΔG°f: the free-energy change to form 1 mol of a compound from its elements in their standard states.
  • ΔG° = ΣΔG°f(products) − ΣΔG°f(reactants), times coefficients.
  • Elements in their standard states: ΔG°f = 0.
  • Tabulated ΔG°f values are for 298 K. At other temperatures use ΔG° = ΔH° − TΔS°.
Quick check

Mission check

?Magnesium carbonate decomposes when heated: MgCO3(s) → MgO(s) + CO2(g). Why must it be heated, and above roughly what temperature does the decomposition become thermodynamically favored?
  • ΔH° = +100.7 kJ/molrxn; ΔS° = +175.1 J/(molrxn·K).

Like limestone, the decomposition is entropy-driven: a gas forms from a solid. Heat until TΔS beats ΔH: about 575 K for MgCO3, about 1119 K for CaCO3. These are estimates (constant ΔH and ΔS assumed); kilns run hotter, which also makes the reaction fast.

Mission 3

Mission 3: If ΔG° is negative, why doesn't a reaction go all the way to products?

Make a guess: If ΔG° is negative, why doesn't a reaction go all the way to products?

Mission 3

ΔG° and K

  • ΔG° = −RT ln K, so K = e−ΔG°/RT. R = 8.314 J/(mol·K): ΔG° in J/molrxn.
  • ΔG negative → ln K positive → K > 1: products favored.
  • ΔG positive → K < 1: reactants favored. ΔG = 0 → K = 1.
  • ΔG° near zero compared with RT (2.48 kJ/mol at 298 K): K near 1. Many times RT: K far from 1.
  • ln is the natural logarithm, not log10.
Mission 3

Worked example: making an ester

  • CH3COOH + C2H5OH ⇌ CH3COOC2H5 + H2O. K ≈ 4.0 at 298 K.
  • ΔG° = −8.314 × 298 × ln 4.0 = −3435 J/molrxn = −3.43 kJ/molrxn.
  • Negative, but small: K is only 4. Products are favored, yet plenty of acid and alcohol remain.
  • Compare ethene + H2 → ethane: ΔG° = −100.0 kJ/molrxn (see Mission 2) gives K = 3.4 × 10¹⁷: effectively complete.
Quick check
?For a certain reaction, ΔG° = −4.0 kJ/molrxn at 298 K. At 298 K, RT = 2.5 kJ/mol. Which statement about the value of the equilibrium constant, K, at 298 K is correct?
Mission 3

Why the reaction stops short (background)

  • Beyond your course, but it answers the video's question.
  • ΔG° is fixed for the reaction at that temperature: every species in its standard state.
  • ΔG is for the actual mixture: ΔG = ΔG° + RT ln Q.
  • Q has the form of K but uses the concentrations now.
  • Q is the reaction quotient from Unit 7.
  • Q < K: ΔG negative, net forward. Q > K: net reverse. Q = K: ΔG = 0, equilibrium.
  • So the ester mixture stops at Q = K ≈ 4, not at 100 % ester.
Mission 3

Thermodynamically favored, but slow

  • Many favored processes don't occur at a measurable rate: they are under kinetic control.
  • Usual cause: a high activation energy. At room temperature very few collisions have enough energy.
  • Diamond → graphite: ΔG° = −2.9 kJ/molrxn at 298 K, yet diamonds last forever.
  • A mixture that isn't changing is not necessarily at equilibrium.
Quick check
?A mixture of H2(g) and O2(g) can be kept in a sealed container at 25°C for years with no detectable formation of water. For the reaction 2 H2(g) + O2(g) → 2 H2O(l), ΔG° = −474 kJ/molrxn at 25°C. Which statement best explains why no water is detected?
Mission 3

Driving unfavorable reactions

  • ΔG° > 0? Supply energy continuously from outside: electricity (electrolysis, charging a battery) or light (photosynthesis).
  • Or couple it to a favored reaction that shares a common intermediate.
  • Glucose + phosphate → glucose 6-phosphate: ΔG° = +13.8 kJ/molrxn.
  • ATP + H2O → ADP + phosphate: ΔG° = −30.5 kJ/molrxn.
  • Add them: glucose + ATP → glucose 6-phosphate + ADP: ΔG° = −16.7 kJ/molrxn. Favored.
  • ΔG° values add, just like ΔH values in Hess's law.
Quick check
?A biology text states: "ATP stores energy in a high-energy phosphate bond, and this energy is released when the bond is broken, driving reactions coupled to the conversion of ATP to ADP." Which statement best evaluates how well this description represents the particulate-level origin of the energy released?
Mission 3

Cell potential away from standard conditions

  • E° applies when Q = 1.
  • As a cell runs, Q moves toward K and |E| falls. At Q = K, E = 0: a dead battery is at equilibrium.
  • Conditions further from equilibrium than Q = 1: |E| larger than E°. Closer: smaller.
  • Concentration cell: same electrodes, different concentrations. Electrons flow so that the concentrations become equal.
  • e.g. Cu | dilute Cu²⁺ ‖ concentrated Cu²⁺ | Cu: oxidation in the dilute half, reduction in the concentrated half.
Mission check

?For a reaction A(g) ⇌ B(g) at 298 K, the standard Gibbs energy change is −5.7 kJ per mole. Pure A is left until equilibrium is reached. Which best describes the equilibrium mixture?
  • ΔG° = −5.7 kJ/molrxn; R = 8.314 J/(mol·K).

A negative ΔG° means K > 1, not complete reaction. ΔG for the mixture fades to zero at equilibrium, where Q = K.

Mission 4

The electrolytic cell

  • Some changes are thermodynamically unfavored (ΔG° > 0): they don't happen on their own.
  • An external source of energy can drive them, for as long as it is supplied.
  • Electrical energy: an electrolytic cell, or charging a battery.
  • Light: photosynthesis, CO2 + H2O → glucose + O2.
  • In an electrolytic cell a DC power supply pushes electrons into one electrode and pulls them from the other.
  • Cathode: electrons pushed in; reduction. Anode: electrons pulled out; oxidation.

Quick check
?A cell contains a Cu electrode in Cu(NO3)2(aq) and a Ni electrode in Ni(NO3)2(aq), joined by a salt bridge and by wires. While the cell operates, the reaction Cu(s) + Ni²⁺(aq) → Cu²⁺(aq) + Ni(s) occurs; this reaction is thermodynamically unfavored. Which classification of the cell, with its justification, is correct?
Mission 4

How the current is carried

  • In the wires and electrodes: electrons.
  • In the electrolyte: ions moving. Cations go to the cathode; anions to the anode.
  • Solid PbBr2: Pb²⁺ and Br⁻ are held in the lattice. They can't move, so no current.
  • Molten PbBr2: the ions are free to move. The melt conducts and the bulb lights.
  • No electrons pass through the melt.
Mission 4

Molten lead(II) bromide: the products

  • At the cathode: Pb²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Pb(l). Molten lead collects below the melt.
  • At the anode: 2Br⁻ → Br2(g) + 2e⁻. Brown vapor rises.
  • Overall: PbBr2(l) → Pb(l) + Br2(g). Unfavored, so it stops when the supply is switched off.
  • Molten binary salt: the metal forms at the cathode, the nonmetal at the anode.
  • Lab: graphite (inert) electrodes; a fume hood, as bromine is toxic.
Quick check

Quick check

Mission 4

Electrolysis in the lab

  • Observations to explain: gas at an electrode; mass gain of a cathode as metal deposits.
  • Electrons are pushed by the power supply, from the anode around the circuit to the cathode.
  • No current until the solid melts, or an ionic solid dissolves in water.
  • Electrolysis of water, 2H2O(l) → 2H2(g) + O2(g), is also unfavored (ΔG° = +474 kJ/molrxn). Gas stops forming the instant the supply is switched off.
Mission check

?Aluminum is made by electrolyzing aluminum oxide, Al2O3. It is first dissolved in molten cryolite at about 950°C. Why can't solid Al2O3 simply be electrolyzed?

Same as lead(II) bromide. The ions were always there; melting sets them free to move.

Mission 5: How can you measure the voltage of half a battery, when a voltmeter needs two connections?

Make a guess: How can you measure the voltage of half a battery, when a voltmeter needs two connections?

Mission 5

Only differences can be measured

  • A voltmeter needs two electrodes, so it measures a potential difference between two half-cells.
  • No single half-cell's potential can be measured on its own.
  • So one half-cell is chosen as the reference and given the value 0 V.
  • Every other half-cell is measured against it.
Mission 5

The standard hydrogen electrode

  • Background: this is how the table of standard reduction potentials gets its zero.
  • Standard hydrogen electrode (SHE): H2 gas bubbling over platinum in a solution of H⁺(aq).
  • Conditions: H2 at 1 atm, 1 M H⁺(aq), 25 °C.
  • Half-equation: 2H⁺(aq) + 2e⁻ ⇌ H2(g). E° = 0.00 V by definition.
  • Platinum is inert: it carries electrons and gives a surface for the reaction. It is often coated with fine platinum black.
  • Zero is an agreed value, not "no reaction".
Mission 5

Building a cell to measure E°

  • Electrodes: carry electrons between the half-cell and the wire.
  • Solutions: hold the ions that react in each half-cell.
  • Salt bridge (e.g. filter paper soaked in KNO3(aq)): ions move through it to keep each solution neutral. No electrons pass through it.
  • High-resistance voltmeter: draws almost no current, so it reads the full potential difference.
Quick check
?In a galvanic cell built from a Zn electrode in Zn(NO3)2(aq) and a Cu electrode in Cu(NO3)2(aq), the zinc electrode loses mass and the copper electrode gains mass as the cell operates. Which half-reaction occurs at the anode?
Mission 5

Measuring E° for nickel

  • Connect a standard Ni²⁺/Ni half-cell to the SHE.
  • The voltmeter reads 0.25 V. Electrons flow through the wire from Ni to the SHE: Ni is oxidized, so Ni is the anode.
  • So Ni²⁺ is harder to reduce than H⁺: E°(Ni²⁺/Ni) = −0.25 V.
  • The whole difference is assigned to nickel, because the SHE is 0 V.
Mission 5

Reading a table of E° values

  • More positive E°: the species on the left is more easily reduced.
  • More negative E°: the species on the right is more easily oxidized.
  • AP calls these standard reduction potentials.
  • Background (not assessed on AP): the species most easily reduced is the strongest oxidizing agent; the one most easily oxidized is the strongest reducing agent.
  • Compare signed values: −2.37 V is the lowest value, not the "largest".
Quick check
?Standard reduction potentials at 25 °C: K⁺(aq) + e⁻ → K(s), E° = −2.92 V · Ni²⁺(aq) + 2 e⁻ → Ni(s), E° = −0.25 V · 2 H⁺(aq) + 2 e⁻ → H2(g), E° = 0.00 V · Br2(l) + 2 e⁻ → 2 Br⁻(aq), E° = +1.07 V. Which order lists the species from most easily reduced to least easily reduced?
Mission 5

E°cell from two reduction potentials

  • E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode, using the tabulated reduction potentials.
  • Cathode: where reduction happens. Anode: where oxidation happens.
  • Ni with the SHE: 0.00 − (−0.25) = +0.25 V, the reading in the earlier screen.
  • Ni anode, Cu²⁺/Cu cathode (+0.34 V): 0.34 − (−0.25) = +0.59 V.
  • Multiplying a half-reaction to balance electrons does not change its E°.
Quick check
?A galvanic cell at 25°C is built from a standard Al³⁺/Al half-cell and a standard Cu²⁺/Cu half-cell. The cell reaction is 2 Al(s) + 3 Cu²⁺(aq) → 2 Al³⁺(aq) + 3 Cu(s). Using the standard reduction potentials in the table, what is E°cell?
Mission check

?A standard Pb²⁺/Pb half-cell is connected to a standard hydrogen electrode. The voltmeter reads 0.13 V.
Electrons flow through the wire from the lead electrode to the hydrogen electrode. Which statement is correct?

You never measure half a cell alone. You measure it against the SHE, agreed to be 0 V, and the whole reading belongs to the other half-cell.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Entropy: gas > liquid > solid. More moles of gas → ΔS positive.
  • ΔS = ΣS(products) − ΣS(reactants); S of an element is not zero.
  • Units J/(molrxn·K): convert to kJ before combining with ΔH°.

Mission 2

  • ΔG° = ΔH° − TΔS°; ΔG° < 0: thermodynamically favored.
  • Same signs of ΔH and ΔS: temperature decides, switching at T = ΔH/ΔS.

Mission 3

  • ΔG° = −RT ln K: negative ΔG° → K > 1; near zero → K ≈ 1.
  • Thermodynamically favored (favored) does not mean fast: a high activation energy can stop a reaction in practice.
  • Unfavored reactions can be driven by external energy or by coupling (ΔG° values add).

Mission 4

  • External electrical energy (or light) can drive a thermodynamically unfavored process, only while it is supplied.
  • In every cell: oxidation at the anode, reduction at the cathode. A molten salt gives its metal at the cathode and its nonmetal at the anode.

Mission 5

  • Only potential differences can be measured; the SHE is the 0 V reference.
  • E° of a half-cell = the cell reading against the SHE under standard conditions, signed by direction of electron flow.
  • E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode, using reduction potentials.
