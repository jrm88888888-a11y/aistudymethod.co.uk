Mission 1

What demand means

  • Demand: the quantity of a good that buyers are willing and able to buy at each price, over a given time period.
  • Law of demand: as price rises, quantity demanded falls, ceteris paribus (other things equal).
  • A demand schedule lists the quantity demanded at each price. Illustrative data:
Price per cupQuantity demanded (cups per week)
6100
5200
4300
3400
2500
  • Plot it: the demand curve (D) slopes downward.
  • Demand is the whole curve, not one point on it.
Mission 1

Movement or shift?

  • A change in the good's own price → a movement along D.
  • A change in anything else that affects buyers → a shift of the whole curve.
  • Step 1: price falls from P1 to P2 → slide along D from point a to point b.
  • Step 2: D1 → D2 to the right: more is demanded at every price.
  • Say: "a change in quantity demanded" for a movement; "a change in demand" for a shift.
Mission 1

What shifts demand

Shift factorIncrease (D shifts right) when…
Incomeincome rises (normal good); income falls (inferior good)
Price of a substitutethe substitute's price rises
Price of a complementthe complement's price falls
Tastes and preferencesthe good becomes more fashionable or popular
Future price expectationsbuyers expect the price to rise soon
Number of consumersmore buyers enter the market
  • The determinants of demand in short: income, prices of related goods, tastes, expectations and number of buyers.
  • The opposite change shifts D left.

Mission 2

What supply means

  • Supply: the quantity that producers are willing and able to sell at each price, over a given time period.
  • Law of supply: as price rises, quantity supplied rises, ceteris paribus.
  • Why? A higher price gives sellers an incentive to produce more: extra output becomes more profitable.
  • Illustrative data: a strawberry farm.
Price per kgQuantity supplied (kg per week)
2100
3200
4300
5400
  • Plot it: the supply curve (S) slopes upward.
Mission 2

Movement or shift?

  • A change in the good's own price → a movement along S.
  • A change in anything else that affects producers → a shift of the whole curve.
  • Step 1: price rises P1 → P2 → slide up along S.
  • Step 2: S1 → S2 to the right = increase in supply: more offered at every price.
  • A shift left = decrease in supply.
  • Say: "an increase in supply shifts the curve right" — not: "the supply curve shifts up". A rightward shift looks lower on the graph.
  • Say: "a change in quantity supplied" for a movement; "a change in supply" for a shift.
Mission 2

What shifts supply

Shift factorSupply increases (S shifts right) when…
Costs of productionwages, raw material or energy prices fall
Technologybetter methods raise output from the same inputs
Indirect taxes and subsidiesa tax is cut or a subsidy is given
Number of firmsmore firms enter the market
Expectationsproducers expect lower prices later, so they sell more now
Shocksgood weather, no disease, no supply disruption
  • An indirect tax per unit shifts S left (vertically up by the tax). A subsidy shifts S right (detail in another lesson).
  • The determinants of supply: input prices, technology, taxes and subsidies, prices of other goods the firm could produce, expectations, number of sellers.

Mission 2

Real case: frost hits coffee

  • July 2021: frosts hit coffee-growing areas of Brazil, the world's largest coffee producer.
  • Damaged trees mean less coffee for sale at every price in later harvests.
  • That is a shock to supply: S shifts left, not a movement along it.
  • The price rise that followed is the result of the shift (see Mission 4); it does not cause the shift.
  • US case: in 2022, bird flu led to the loss of more than 43 million egg-laying hens in the United States.
  • Fewer hens → fewer eggs offered at every price: the supply of eggs shifted left.

Only the good's own price moves producers along S. A change in costs, technology, taxes, subsidies or the number of firms shifts it.

Mission 3: When a price is too high or too low, what pulls it back to where buyers and sellers agree?

Mission 3

Equilibrium: where the curves cross

  • Equilibrium: the price at which quantity demanded = quantity supplied.
  • At that price the market clears: every unit offered is bought, every buyer willing to pay it gets one.
  • No pressure for the price to change, unless something else changes.
  • Label it P1 and Q1 (or Pe and Qe).
  • In this model: many buyers and many sellers, none big enough to set the price alone (a competitive market).
Mission 3

Price too high: excess supply

  • At a price above Pe, quantity supplied > quantity demanded.
  • This is excess supply, or a surplus.
  • Size of the surplus = Qs − Qd at that price.
  • Signs: unsold stock piling up, discounts, "clearance" sales.
  • Only Qd is actually sold: firms cannot sell more than buyers want.
  • Market forces: sellers cut the price to shift their stock.
Mission 3

Price too low: excess demand

  • At a price below Pe, quantity demanded > quantity supplied.
  • This is excess demand, or a shortage.
  • Size of the shortage = Qd − Qs at that price.
  • Signs: queues, waiting lists, "sold out", resale above the original price.
  • Only Qs is actually bought: buyers cannot buy what is not supplied.
  • Market forces: buyers compete, so the price is bid up.
  • A shortage is not the same as scarcity. Scarcity is always present; a shortage exists only when the price is below equilibrium.

Mission 3

Worked example: finding equilibrium and the gap

  • Market for reusable water bottles, thousands per week (illustrative data).
PriceQdQs
69030
88050
107070
126090
  • Step 1: find the row where Qd = Qs: price 10, quantity 70 thousand. That is equilibrium.
  • Step 2: at a price of 12, Qs − Qd = 90 − 60 = 30: a surplus of 30 thousand. Only 60 thousand are sold.
  • Step 3: at a price of 6, Qd − Qs = 90 − 30 = 60: a shortage of 60 thousand. Only 30 thousand are bought.
  • One formula: Qs − Qd. Positive = surplus; negative = shortage. At 6: 30 − 90 = −60, a shortage of 60.
  • Always compare Qd and Qs at the same price, never with Qe.

Mission 3

Market forces: movements, not shifts

  • Surplus → price falls → quantity demanded rises and quantity supplied falls → until Qd = Qs.
  • Shortage → price rises → quantity demanded falls and quantity supplied rises → until Qd = Qs.
  • These are movements along the curves. Neither curve shifts: the price is on the axis, so it cannot shift its own curve.
  • Say: "the price falls, so quantity demanded increases and quantity supplied decreases" — not: "demand increases and supply decreases".
  • Market forces drive the price to equilibrium; the competitive equilibrium clears the market.

Mission 4

A shift starts a chain

  • Something other than the good's own price changes. One curve shifts.
  • Example: incomes rise and the good is normal. Demand shifts right, D1 → D2.
  • At the old price P1 there is now excess demand (a shortage).
  • The price is bid up. Suppliers respond by moving along S.
  • New equilibrium: higher price P2, higher quantity Q2.
  • Say: "an increase in demand causes an increase in quantity supplied" — not: "an increase in supply".
Mission 4

The four single shifts

ShiftPriceQuantity
Demand right (increase)risesrises
Demand left (decrease)fallsfalls
Supply right (increase)fallsrises
Supply left (decrease)risesfalls
  • Demand shifts: price and quantity move the same way.
  • Supply shifts: price and quantity move opposite ways.
  • Always ask first: which curve does the event shift? Usually only one.
  • A supply shift right means more is offered at every price, not a move "down" the graph.

Mission check

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Demand is the whole schedule or curve; its own price moves you along it, every other factor shifts it.
  • Price change: change in quantity demanded; determinant change: change in demand.

Mission 2

  • Supply slopes up because a higher price makes extra output profitable; own price moves you along S, every other factor shifts it.
  • Increase in supply = shift right (it looks lower), decrease = shift left.

Mission 3

  • Equilibrium: the price where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied, so the market clears.
  • Above it: surplus (Qs − Qd), the price falls. Below it: shortage (Qd − Qs), the price rises. Both adjust by movements along the curves.
  • Calculate Qs − Qd: positive is a surplus, negative a shortage.

Mission 4

  • A shift creates excess demand or supply at the old price; the price then changes and the market moves along the other curve to a new equilibrium.
  • Demand shifts move price and quantity the same way; supply shifts move them opposite ways. Two shifts at once leave one result uncertain.
