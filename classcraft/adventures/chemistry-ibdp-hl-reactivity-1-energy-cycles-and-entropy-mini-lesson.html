Mission 1

Mission 1: Carbon and hydrogen don't combine cleanly to make methane. So how does the data booklet know its ΔH⦵f?

Make a guess: Carbon and hydrogen don't combine cleanly to make methane. So how does the data booklet know its ΔH⦵f?

Mission 1

From the core lesson

  • Hess's law: ΔH is the same whatever the route from start to finish.
  • Reverse an equation: change the sign of ΔH. Multiply an equation: multiply its ΔH.
  • An arrow followed against its direction in a cycle contributes −ΔH.
  • First ionization energy: X(g) → X⁺(g) + e⁻, always endothermic.
Mission 1

Standard conditions and standard states

  • ⦵ means standard conditions: 100 kPa, a stated temperature (usually 298 K), solutions 1 mol dm⁻³.
  • Standard state: the pure, most stable form under those conditions.
  • Examples: C(s, graphite), O₂(g), Br₂(l), H₂O(l), Na(s).
Mission 1

Standard enthalpy of formation

  • ΔH⦵f: enthalpy change when 1 mol of a compound forms from its elements in their standard states.
  • So ΔH⦵f of an element in its standard state is zero.
  • Use fractions to make exactly 1 mol: 2Al(s) + 1½O₂(g) → Al₂O₃(s).
  • Start from elements, never from gaseous atoms such as C(g) or H(g).
  • Values are in the data booklet.
Quick check
?Which equation represents the standard enthalpy change of formation, ΔH⦵f, of methanol, CH₃OH(l)?
Mission 1

Standard enthalpy of combustion

  • ΔH⦵c: enthalpy change when 1 mol of a substance burns completely in oxygen, under standard conditions.
  • Complete: carbon ends as CO₂(g), hydrogen as H₂O(l), liquid in its standard state.
  • ΔH⦵c is always negative. Elements have ΔH⦵c values too: C(s) and H₂(g) burn.
  • Values are in the data booklet.
Quick check
?Which equation represents the standard enthalpy change of combustion of ethanol?
Mission 1

Using formation data

  • Reactants and products can both be made from the same elements.
  • Hess's law then gives ΔH⦵ = ΣΔH⦵f(products) − ΣΔH⦵f(reactants) (data booklet).
  • Multiply each value by its coefficient in the equation.
  • Elements in their standard states count as zero.
Mission 1

Worked example: decomposing limestone

  • CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g)
  • ΔH⦵f / kJ mol⁻¹: CaCO₃(s) −1207.6 and CaO(s) −634.9 (literature values); CO₂(g) −394 (data booklet).
  • ΔH⦵ = [−634.9 + (−394)] − [−1207.6]
  • = −1028.9 + 1207.6 = +178.7 kJ mol⁻¹. Endothermic: keep this value for Mission 4.
  • The core lesson quotes +178 kJ mol⁻¹: the same value, rounded.

Mission 1

Using combustion data

  • Reactants and products both burn to the same CO₂ and H₂O.
  • So the order flips: ΔH⦵ = ΣΔH⦵c(reactants) − ΣΔH⦵c(products) (data booklet).
  • Worked: C₂H₄(g) + H₂(g) → C₂H₆(g). ΔH⦵c / kJ mol⁻¹ (data booklet): C₂H₄ −1411; H₂ −286; C₂H₆ −1561.
  • ΔH⦵ = [−1411 + (−286)] − [−1561] = −1697 + 1561 = −136 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Elements that burn, such as C and H₂, are not zero here; O₂, CO₂ and H₂O(l) cannot burn, so they are.
  • Data sources differ slightly: 1.8's ΔH⦵f route gives −1562 for ethane; the data booklet gives −1561.
Calculate
1Ethyne can be hydrogenated to ethane: C₂H₂(g) + 2H₂(g) → C₂H₆(g). ΔH⦵c / kJ mol⁻¹ (data booklet): C₂H₂(g) −1301; H₂(g) −286; C₂H₆(g) −1561. Calculate ΔH⦵ for the reaction, in kJ mol⁻¹.
Mission check

?Methane cannot be made cleanly from carbon and hydrogen: C(s) + 2H₂(g) → CH₄(g). Which expression gives ΔH⦵f(CH₄)?

Carbon, hydrogen and methane are each burned separately and their ΔH⦵c values measured. Hess's law combines the three to give ΔH⦵f(CH₄), a value no one measures directly.

Mission 2

Mission 2: No one can pull a salt crystal apart into gaseous ions and measure the energy. So how do we know the lattice enthalpy of NaCl?

Make a guess: No one can pull a salt crystal apart into gaseous ions and measure the energy. So how do we know the lattice enthalpy of NaCl?

Mission 2

Steps that make gaseous ions

  • Enthalpy of atomization: element in its standard state → 1 mol of gaseous atoms. Always endothermic.
  • For a metal it is sublimation: Na(s) → Na(g).
  • For chlorine it is half the bond enthalpy: ½Cl₂(g) → Cl(g), ½ × 242 = +121 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • For bromine it includes vaporization: ½Br₂(l) → Br(g).
  • Ionization energies then remove electrons from gaseous atoms: endothermic.
Mission 2

Electron affinity and lattice enthalpy

  • First electron affinity: 1 mol of electrons added to 1 mol of gaseous atoms: Cl(g) + e⁻ → Cl⁻(g).
  • First electron affinities of Cl and O are exothermic.
  • Second electron affinity, O⁻(g) + e⁻ → O²⁻(g), is endothermic: the ion repels the electron.
  • Lattice enthalpy (IB): 1 mol of ionic solid → gaseous ions: NaCl(s) → Na⁺(g) + Cl⁻(g).
  • It is positive: separating the ions absorbs energy.

Quick check
?Which step on the way from Na(s) and Cl₂(g) to NaCl(s) is exothermic?
Mission 2

Reading a Born–Haber cycle

  • A Born–Haber cycle is Hess's law for an ionic solid.
  • Two routes from the elements to the solid: directly (ΔH⦵f) or through gaseous ions.
  • Arrows up are endothermic; arrows down are exothermic.
  • Both routes give the same total, so one unknown step can be found.
  • You will be given the cycle or the data; you won't have to build a full cycle.
Mission 2

Worked example: lattice enthalpy of NaCl

  • Data / kJ mol⁻¹: ΔH⦵f(NaCl) −411 and atomization Na +107 (literature values); Cl +121 (½ × 242); IE₁(Na) +496 and EA₁(Cl) −349 (data booklet).
  • Route through ions: 107 + 121 + 496 − 349 − L = ΔH⦵f = −411
  • L = 107 + 121 + 496 − 349 + 411 = +786 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • The data booklet lists 790: values from different sources differ slightly.
  • Only the lattice step releases enough energy to make ΔH⦵f negative.

Mission 2

Divalent ions: calcium chloride

  • Ca²⁺ needs IE₁ + IE₂. CaCl₂ has two Cl⁻, so double the chlorine steps.
  • Data / kJ mol⁻¹: ΔH⦵f(CaCl₂) −796, atomization Ca +178 and IE₂ +1145 (literature values); IE₁ +590, Cl +121 and EA₁(Cl) −349 (data booklet).
  • 178 + 590 + 1145 + 2(121) + 2(−349) − L = −796
  • L = 1457 + 796 = +2253 kJ mol⁻¹: far larger than NaCl. (The data booklet lists 2271.)
  • For an O²⁻ ion, add EA₁ (exothermic) and EA₂ (endothermic).

It can't be measured directly. Every other step can, so Hess's law round the Born–Haber cycle gives the one unknown. These cycle data give L(NaCl) = +786 kJ mol⁻¹; the booklet's experimental value is +790; the small difference comes from step values taken from different data sets.

Mission 3

Entropy is dispersal

  • Entropy, S: a measure of how dispersed the matter and energy in a system are.
  • More ways to arrange the particles and share out the energy: higher entropy.
  • Under the same conditions: gas > liquid > solid.
  • A gas spreading into more space is an entropy increase.
Mission 3

Predicting the sign of ΔS

  • Look first at moles of gas: more gas formed → ΔS positive; less gas → negative.
  • Melting, vaporization, sublimation: ΔS positive. Freezing, condensation: negative.
  • Dissolving an ionic solid usually raises S. Dissolving a gas lowers it.
  • Solids and liquids count for little when the amount of gas changes.
Quick check
?Which change has a negative entropy change, ΔS, for the system?
Quick check
?Consider the four changes W, X, Y and Z below at 298 K. Which change results in an increase in the entropy of the system? W: 2H₂S(g) + O₂(g) → 2S(s) + 2H₂O(l) (exothermic) X: NH₃(g) → NH₃(aq) (exothermic) Y: 2C(s) + O₂(g) → 2CO(g) (exothermic) Z: N₂(g) + 2O₂(g) → 2NO₂(g) (endothermic)
Mission 3

Calculating ΔS⦵

  • Standard entropies, S⦵, are absolute values: positive for every pure substance, elements included.
  • ΔS⦵ = ΣS⦵(products) − ΣS⦵(reactants), each times its coefficient. Units: J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.
  • Worked: CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O(l). S⦵ / J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹: CH₄ 186, CO₂ 214, H₂O(l) 70 (data booklet); O₂ 205.2 (literature value).
  • ΔS⦵ = [214 + 2(70)] − [186 + 2(205.2)] = 354 − 596.4 = −242.4 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.
  • Negative, as predicted: 3 mol of gas become 1 mol.

Mission 4: Why does limestone need a kiln at about 900 °C to turn into lime?

Make a guess: Why does limestone need a kiln at about 900 °C to turn into lime?

Mission 4

The surroundings count too

  • A change alters the entropy of the system and of its surroundings.
  • Heat released (ΔH < 0) disperses energy into the surroundings: their entropy rises.
  • ΔS(surroundings) = −ΔH/T: the same heat matters more at low temperature.
  • A change is spontaneous when the total entropy increases.
Mission 4

Gibbs energy

  • ΔG⦵ = ΔH⦵ − TΔS⦵ (data booklet) combines both entropy changes in one value.
  • When ΔG is negative (constant pressure), the change is spontaneous.
  • Units: ΔH⦵ and ΔG⦵ in kJ mol⁻¹; ΔS⦵ in J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹, so divide ΔS⦵ by 1000.
  • T is in kelvin.
  • Spontaneous says nothing about rate.
Quick check
?Water freezes spontaneously at 263 K (−10 °C), yet ΔS of the system is negative. Why?
Mission 4

Worked example: limestone at two temperatures

  • CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g): ΔH⦵ = +178.7 kJ mol⁻¹ (Mission 1); ΔS⦵ = +0.1604 kJ K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ (Mission 3).
  • 298 K: ΔG⦵ = 178.7 − 298(0.1604) = 178.7 − 47.8 = +130.9 kJ mol⁻¹. Not spontaneous.
  • 1200 K: ΔG⦵ = 178.7 − 1200(0.1604) = 178.7 − 192.5 = −13.8 kJ mol⁻¹. Spontaneous.
  • Assumes ΔH⦵ and ΔS⦵ do not change with temperature.
Calculate
1For C₂H₄(g) + H₂(g) → C₂H₆(g), ΔH⦵ = −136 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔS⦵ = −120.7 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. Calculate ΔG⦵ at 298 K, in kJ mol⁻¹.
Mission 4

When ΔG changes sign

ΔH⦵ΔS⦵Spontaneous
−+at all temperatures
+−at no temperature
++above T = ΔH⦵/ΔS⦵
−−below T = ΔH⦵/ΔS⦵
  • At the switch-over, ΔG⦵ = 0, so T = ΔH⦵/ΔS⦵ (ΔS⦵ in kJ).
  • Limestone: T = 178.7 ÷ 0.1604 = 1114 K (841 °C).
Quick check
?For a reaction, ΔH⦵ is negative and ΔS⦵ is negative. Assuming that neither value changes with temperature, which statement about the reaction is correct?
Calculate
1For C₂H₄(g) + H₂(g) → C₂H₆(g), ΔH⦵ = −136 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔS⦵ = −120.7 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. Above what temperature, in K, does the reaction stop being spontaneous?
Mission 4

Rearranging, and ΔG = 0 at a change of state

  • Rearrange for any unknown: ΔH⦵ = ΔG⦵ + TΔS⦵; ΔS⦵ = (ΔH⦵ − ΔG⦵)/T.
  • At the melting point, solid and liquid are in equilibrium: ΔG = 0.
  • So ΔS = ΔH/T for melting or boiling at that temperature.
Calculate
1For C₂H₂(g) + 2H₂(g) → C₂H₆(g) at 298 K, ΔH⦵ = −312 kJ mol⁻¹ (Mission 1) and ΔG⦵ = −243 kJ mol⁻¹ (from data-booklet ΔG⦵f values). Use T = 298 K. Calculate ΔS⦵, in J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.
Calculate
1Ice melts at 273 K, where ΔH for melting is +6.01 kJ mol⁻¹ (literature value). Calculate ΔS for melting, in J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.
Quick check
?For the change C(diamond) → C(graphite), ΔG⦵ = −2.9 kJ mol⁻¹ at 298 K. Yet diamonds show no detectable conversion into graphite at room temperature, even over centuries. Which statement accounts for both facts?
Mission check

?For CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g), ΔH⦵ = +178.7 kJ mol⁻¹ and ΔS⦵ = +160.4 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. Why must limestone be strongly heated to make lime?

Lime-making is entropy-driven: a gas forms from a solid. The estimate, about 1114 K (841 °C), assumes ΔH⦵ and ΔS⦵ stay constant, so it is approximate. Kilns run hotter, which also makes the decomposition fast.

Mission 5

Mission 5: If ΔG⦵ is negative, why doesn't a reaction go all the way to products?

Make a guess: If ΔG⦵ is negative, why doesn't a reaction go all the way to products?

Mission 5

ΔG⦵ and ΔG are different

  • ΔG⦵: a fixed value for the reaction at a given temperature, with every species in its standard state.
  • ΔG: for the actual mixture; it changes as the composition changes.
  • ΔG = ΔG⦵ + RT ln Q (data booklet). Q has the form of K but uses the concentrations now.
  • R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹, so work in J mol⁻¹.
  • ln is the natural logarithm, not log₁₀.
Mission 5

Downhill to equilibrium

  • Few products: Q < K, ΔG is negative, net forward reaction.
  • As products build up, Q rises and ΔG becomes less negative.
  • At equilibrium Q = K and ΔG = 0: no net change either way.
  • Q > K: ΔG is positive, so net reverse reaction.
Quick check
?A reaction mixture reaches equilibrium at constant temperature and pressure. Which statement about the equilibrium mixture is correct?
Mission 5

Linking ΔG⦵ and K

  • At equilibrium, 0 = ΔG⦵ + RT ln K, so ΔG⦵ = −RT ln K (data booklet).
  • ΔG⦵ negative → K > 1; positive → K < 1; zero → K = 1.
  • Worked: ethanoic acid + ethanol ⇌ ethyl ethanoate + water, K ≈ 4.0 at 298 K (literature value).
  • ΔG⦵ = −8.31 × 298 × ln 4.0 = −3433 J mol⁻¹ = −3.43 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Rearranged: K = e^(−ΔG⦵/RT).
Quick check
?For a reaction at 25 °C, the equilibrium constant, K, is 5.00 × 10¹⁸. What is ΔG⦵ for the reaction at this temperature? (R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹)
Calculate
1For C₂H₄(g) + H₂(g) ⇌ C₂H₆(g), ΔG⦵ = −100.0 kJ mol⁻¹ at 298 K. Calculate K at 298 K. (R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹)
Mission 5

Worked example: which way will a mixture go?

  • Esterification at 298 K, ΔG⦵ = −3433 J mol⁻¹. Mixture / mol dm⁻³: ester 2.0, water 2.0, acid 0.40, alcohol 1.0.
  • Q = [ester][water] / ([acid][alcohol]) = (2.0 × 2.0) / (0.40 × 1.0) = 10.
  • ΔG = −3433 + 8.31 × 298 × ln 10 = −3433 + 5702 = +2269 J mol⁻¹ (+2.27 kJ mol⁻¹).
  • ΔG is positive (Q > K): net reverse reaction until ΔG = 0.
Calculate
1For the same esterification at 298 K (ΔG⦵ = −3.43 kJ mol⁻¹), a mixture contains ethanoic acid 1.0, ethanol 1.0, ethyl ethanoate 0.50 and water 0.50 mol dm⁻³. Calculate ΔG for this mixture, in kJ mol⁻¹. (R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹)
Mission check

?For esterification at 298 K, ΔG⦵ = −3.43 kJ mol⁻¹. Yet a mixture of ethanoic acid and ethanol never turns completely into ester. Why?

A negative ΔG⦵ means K > 1, not complete reaction. The driving force, ΔG, fades to zero at equilibrium.

Recap

The big ideas

  • ΔH⦵f: 1 mol of compound from its elements in standard states. ΔH⦵c: 1 mol burned completely.
  • Formation data: products − reactants. Combustion data: reactants − products.
  • Born–Haber: two routes from elements to ionic solid; IB lattice enthalpy is positive.
  • Entropy is dispersal; moles of gas decide the sign; S⦵ of an element is not zero.
  • ΔG⦵ = ΔH⦵ − TΔS⦵ (ΔS⦵ in kJ); switch-over at T = ΔH⦵/ΔS⦵.
  • ΔG = ΔG⦵ + RT ln Q falls to zero at equilibrium, where ΔG⦵ = −RT ln K.
