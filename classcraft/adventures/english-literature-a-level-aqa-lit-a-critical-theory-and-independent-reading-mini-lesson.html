Critical theory and independent reading

The NEA — Independent critical study: texts across time is a comparative critical study of two texts, at least one of which must have been written pre-1900. You produce one extended essay of 2500 words and a bibliography. It is the one place on the course where you choose the texts, the question and the critical framework.

  • Weighting: the NEA is worth 20% of the A-level (50 marks). AO4 (connections) and AO5 (interpretations) carry unusual weight here — this is a comparison, informed by critical reading.
  • The freedom is the danger. A badly chosen question cannot be rescued by good writing, and most weak NEAs were lost at the planning stage, not the writing stage.
  • Note the difference from Lit B: this is one 2500-word comparative essay across time, not two shorter theory essays. Do not confuse the two specifications.

Work through each screen, answer the questions as you go and collect ⭐ stars. Named texts appear only as swappable exemplars — the skills are what the exam rewards, and set-text lists change. Press Start when you are ready.

Design · 1

Choosing the two texts

The comparison is the essay. Choose texts whose relationship generates an argument.

  • At least one pre-1900. This is a requirement, not a suggestion. Check the date, not your impression of the date.
  • Choose for productive difference, not similarity. Two novels that both criticise marriage will produce a dutiful essay. A pre-1900 novel that cannot imagine a woman outside marriage, set against a modern text that can imagine nothing else but her escape from it, produces an argument.
  • Genre difference is an asset. A poem and a novel, or a play and a novel, force you to compare methods rather than plots — which is exactly where AO2 and AO4 marks live.
  • Avoid texts you have studied in class. The NEA must not overlap with your examined texts (check your centre's guidance, and your teacher will confirm the rules) — and independent reading is the point of the task.
  • Choose texts with critical material available. AO5 requires engagement with different interpretations, and an obscure text with no criticism will strand you.

The test of a good pairing: can you finish this sentence in a way that surprises you? Reading these two texts together reveals something about the first that I could not have seen on its own. If not, change a text.

Design · 2

The question: the single most important decision

Your question determines your ceiling. A weak question cannot produce a strong essay.

Weak questions (they invite description):

  • Compare the presentation of love in Text A and Text B. — too broad; you will summarise.
  • How is the theme of death shown in both texts? — no argument is possible; the answer is going to be a list.
  • Which text is better? — not a literary question.

Strong questions (they contain a tension to be resolved):

  • To what extent do both texts present freedom as something their heroines can only imagine, never inhabit?
  • Both texts grant a female character the last word. Compare the extent to which either grants her authority.
  • How far do both writers use the natural world as a way of avoiding the human one?

The anatomy of a good NEA question: a debatable proposition (something a reasonable person could deny) + a comparative frame (both texts, together) + a focus tight enough to be argued in 2500 words. If your question could be answered yes without further thought, it is not a question.

AO5

Critical reading: what it is and is not

The NEA is an independent critical study, and AO5 is heavily weighted. But critical reading is not name-dropping.

  • What earns marks: understanding an interpretation, applying it to your texts, and then testing it — showing where it illuminates and where the text resists it.
  • What earns nothing: a quotation from a critic dropped into a paragraph to decorate a point you had already made. Examiners call this critical garnish, and they are not fooled by it.
  • Use critics as interlocutors. The most powerful move in an NEA is disagreement: X argues that the novel condemns its heroine; but the narrative's tenderness towards her at the very moment of her fall suggests something more divided than condemnation.
  • Theoretical frameworks count as interpretations. A sustained feminist, Marxist, post-colonial, eco-critical or psychoanalytic reading is AO5 — provided it produces claims about your texts.
  • Reception history is AO5 and AO3 at once. How each text was received on publication, and how that has changed, is evidence you can use.

Never invent, misattribute or paraphrase a critic you have not read. A fabricated critical quotation is academic malpractice, it is easily detected, and it puts your whole submission at risk. If you are not certain who said it, do not attribute it.

AO3

Texts across time: the comparison is historical

The title of this component is texts across time, and the phrase is doing work. The gap between your two texts is not an obstacle — it is your subject.

  • What could each text think? The pre-1900 text is not a failed modern one. It operates inside assumptions about class, gender, religion, empire and law that were the conditions of its thought, not lapses in it.
  • What has changed, and what has only changed its name? The most powerful NEA arguments find continuity where the reader expects rupture — a modern text that has replaced a moral vocabulary with a psychological one and is doing exactly the same thing with it.
  • Form changes with history. The three-volume Victorian novel and the fragmentary modern one are not just different styles; they were built for different readers, different markets and different beliefs about what a life is.
  • Reception across time. Both texts have been read differently at different moments, and one of them has been read for far longer. That asymmetry is worth a paragraph.

Avoid the two failure modes: presentism (scolding the older text for not being modern) and excuse-making (refusing to judge it at all). The mature position finds the older text divided against itself — seeing more than its society, and less than it wanted to.

Craft

Structuring 2500 words

2500 words is not long. Budget it.

  1. Introduction (~200 words). The two texts, the question, and your line of argument — not a summary of the texts and not a biography of the authors. State the thesis you intend to prove.
  2. Four to five idea-sections (~450 words each). Each takes one facet of the argument and handles both texts. Never write a block on Text A followed by a block on Text B — that structure produces two essays and no comparison, and it caps AO4.
  3. Within each section: claim → close textual evidence from Text A → close textual evidence from Text B → the significant difference → what it reveals. Context and criticism woven in where they bite.
  4. Conclusion (~250 words). Not a summary. Return to the question and answer it with the qualification your evidence has forced on you. The best conclusions are slightly less confident than the introduction, and are more interesting for it.
  5. Bibliography. Every text, critical work, edition and article you consulted, in a consistent format. Record it as you go.

Word count discipline: if you are over, cut the plot summary first. There is always plot summary, and it is always the least useful writing in the essay.

Academic practice: citation and integrity

The NEA assesses your ability to work as an independent literary critic, and that includes scholarly honesty.

  • Cite everything. Quotations, ideas, arguments and interpretations that are not yours must be attributed — including anything you found online, and anything an AI tool produced.
  • Keep a running bibliography from the first day. Reconstructing it at the end is how honest students accidentally commit malpractice.
  • Quote accurately. Check every quotation against the text. A misquotation in a coursework essay — where you have the book in front of you — is far less forgivable than in an exam.
  • Never fabricate a critic, a quotation or a source. Invented references are the most serious error available to you here, and they are trivially easy for a teacher to detect.
  • Your voice must be the argument. Criticism supports your thesis; it does not supply it. An essay assembled from other people's readings has no AO1 to speak of.

A useful working rule: if you could not explain a critical idea in your own words to a friend, you do not understand it well enough to use it — and if you use it anyway, the examiner will be able to tell.

The independent reader

The NEA is the closest thing on the course to what literary study actually is: choosing a book, reading it hard, and having something to say about it that nobody asked you for.

How the best NEAs get written:

  • Read the texts twice before you frame a question. The question should come out of the reading, not the other way round. A question chosen first will bend the texts to fit it.
  • Keep a notebook of resistances — the moments in each text that will not fit your emerging argument. Those are not obstacles; they are the essay. Any thesis that meets no resistance is too weak to be interesting.
  • Read criticism after you have your own reading, not before. Otherwise you will spend 2500 words agreeing with a stranger.
  • Write the introduction last. You cannot state a thesis you have not yet discovered.
  • Give the essay to somebody who has not read the texts. If they cannot follow the argument, the problem is the argument, not the reader.

The sentence that marks a first-class NEA: Reading these two texts together makes visible something in the earlier one that a century of readers, including me on first reading, had been able to look straight past. If your essay earns that sentence, it will be in the top band.

The big ideas to take into the exam

The task (7712): Independent critical study: texts across time — a COMPARATIVE study of two texts, at least one pre-1900. ONE extended essay of 2500 words plus a bibliography. 20% of the A-level

Text choice: choose for productive DIFFERENCE, not similarity; genre difference is an asset; make sure critical material exists; check the pre-1900 date

The question: a debatable proposition + a comparative frame + a focus tight enough for 2500 words. Theme-titles produce description; tension-titles produce argument

AO5: apply a reading, then TEST it — show where the text resists. Critical garnish earns nothing. NEVER fabricate or misattribute a critic

AO3 across time: what could each text think? · find continuity where you expect rupture · form changes with history · avoid both presentism and excuse-making

Structure: introduction (~200) · four or five IDEA-sections handling both texts (~450 each) · conclusion (~250). Block structure caps AO4

Integrity: cite everything, including AI tools; keep the bibliography from day one; check every quotation against the text

Working method: read the texts twice → let the question emerge → keep a notebook of resistances → read criticism AFTER you have a reading → write the introduction last
