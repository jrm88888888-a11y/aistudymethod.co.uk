Mission 1

What makes an NEA question good before you've written a word?

Make a guess: What makes an NEA question good before you've written a word?

A-level skills · coursework (NEA)Screen 1

What the NEA is for

  • The NEA is the one place where you set the task: your texts (with guidance), your question.
  • It is marked on the same AOs as the exams, so the question must open the door to all of them: argument, method, context, connections, interpretations.
  • A good question does half the work before you write: it sets up a debate, a comparison and a reason to use critics.
  • Your teacher can guide choices and give limited feedback; the work must be yours.
A-level skills · coursework (NEA)Screen 2

How your exam asks this

  • AQA Literature A, 'Independent critical study': one comparative essay of 2,500 words plus a bibliography (50 marks, 20%). Two texts by different authors, one written before 1900, neither on the exam lists. Give both equal attention, and consider readings over time for at least one.
A-level skills · coursework (NEA)Screen 3

Choosing texts

  • Check your board's rules first: dates, forms, banned set texts, translations.
  • Pair texts that light each other up: one concern, treated differently (period, form, viewpoint).
  • Choose texts with a critical conversation: you'll need readings to debate, ideally over time.
  • Choose texts you can re-read closely for months.
  • Avoid pairs linked only by a topic word (‘both have a ghost’).

Mission 2

Two critics disagree about Othello. Do you have to pick a side?

Make a guess: Two critics disagree about Othello. Do you have to pick a side?

A-level skills · critical interpretations (AO5)Screen 1

What AO5 asks

  • Every board's AO5: explore literary texts informed by different interpretations.
  • An interpretation is an argued reading of the text: a critic's essay, a director's staging, a theory (feminist, Marxist, post-colonial), or another reader's view.
  • Readings change over time: a speech admired in 1904 can be distrusted in 1937.
  • AO5 rewards debate: testing a reading against the text. A critic's name on its own earns nothing.
A-level skills · critical interpretations (AO5)Screen 2

How your exam asks this

  • AQA Literature A: AO5 counts in every question (about 12% of the A-level). Questions often give you a view to debate. In the NEA you must also show how readings of at least one text have changed over time.

Othello's last speech

A-level skills · critical interpretations (AO5)Screen 4

Two readings of the speech

  • A. C. Bradley (Shakespearean Tragedy, 1904) took Othello at his word: "not easily jealous" is "perfectly just", and at the close "the Othello of the Council-chamber and the quay of Cyprus has returned".
  • T. S. Eliot (1927) disagreed: Othello is consoling himself, thinking less of what he did than of how he'll be remembered.
  • F. R. Leavis (1937) went further: Iago succeeds fast because Othello's self-ideal makes him ready to believe.
  • The speech supports both sides: that makes it a debate.

A-level skills · critical interpretations (AO5)Screen 6

Using a critic, not dropping one

  1. State the view briefly in your own words, and say whose it is.
  2. Test it against precise evidence from the text.
  3. Qualify it: what does it explain, and what does it miss?
  4. Decide your own position. "Yes, but…" is a position.
  • You don't have to pick a side. You do have to weigh the sides.

Mission 3

Two novels, 1818 and 1898, both about science. What connection scores?

Make a guess: Two novels, 1818 and 1898, both about science. What connection scores?

A-level skills · connections and comparison (AO4)Screen 1

What AO4 asks

  • Every board's AO4: explore connections across literary texts.
  • Connect through theme (what the texts are about), method (voice, imagery, structure), genre (Gothic, science fiction, tragedy) or context (the ideas each text answers).
  • Difference counts as much as likeness: the strongest connections show two texts treating one idea in different ways.
  • AQA: even a single-text answer can place the text among others of its period or genre.
A-level skills · connections and comparison (AO4)Screen 2

How your exam asks this

  • AQA Literature A: AO4 counts in every question (about 12%). Paper 1 Section C and Paper 2 Section B each set one essay linking two texts (25 marks); the NEA compares two texts.

?Two novels, 1818 and 1898, both about science. What connection scores?

A connection scores when it goes beyond the shared topic. Compare how each text treats the idea, using precise evidence and methods from both, and explain the differences, often through context.

Recap

Big ideas

  • The NEA is your own task: choose texts and a question that open the door to every AO.
  • Pair texts with one shared concern treated differently, and a critical debate around them.
  • A strong title has a comparative frame, a precise focus, a view to test and two texts that meet your board's rules.
  • 2,500 words; two authors; one text pre-1900; readings over time on at least one text.
  • AO5 asks you to explore a text through different interpretations: critics, productions, theories and other readers, including how readings change over time.
  • Othello's last speech is read as nobility restored (Bradley, 1904) and as self-dramatising (Eliot, 1927; Leavis, 1937): the lines support both, so you weigh them.
  • Use a critic in four moves: state, test, qualify, decide. A name alone earns nothing.
  • AQA marks AO5 in every question; the NEA also needs readings over time.
  • AO4 rewards connections of theme, method, genre and context, and difference counts as much as likeness.
  • Move from topic ("both about science") to a connection that compares how each text works and why they differ.
  • Shelley's scientist gazes down at his creation; Wells turns the microscope on humanity: one idea, opposite ends of the lens.
  • AQA marks AO4 in every question, as well as the linking essays.
