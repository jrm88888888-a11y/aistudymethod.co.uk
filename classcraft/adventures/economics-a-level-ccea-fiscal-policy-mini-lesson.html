Mission 1

Mission 1: If a country's yearly deficit keeps shrinking, how can its debt still be growing?

Make a guess: If a country's yearly deficit keeps shrinking, how can its debt still be growing?

Mission 1

Three kinds of spending

  • Current spending: day-to-day running costs that are used up within the year. Salaries of teachers and nurses, medicines, office supplies.
  • Capital spending: investment in assets that last for years. New roads, rail lines, hospitals, school buildings.
  • Transfer payments: money paid to people with no good or service in return. Pensions, unemployment benefits, child benefit.
  • Transfers move income from taxpayers to recipients. They are not part of government spending (G) in AD, because no output is bought.
  • G is a component of AD. Capital spending can also raise LRAS over time (better infrastructure, more productive workers).
  • Say: "capital spending" for long-lasting assets — not: "spending on capital cities" or "big spending".
Mission 1

Where the money comes from, and what for

  • Direct taxes: on income and wealth, paid straight to the government. Income tax, corporation tax.
  • Indirect taxes: on spending, collected by sellers. VAT (sales tax), excise duties on fuel, alcohol and tobacco.
  • Tax types, rates and progressive vs regressive taxes are covered in Mission 2.

Mission 1

The budget balance

  • Budget balance = tax revenue − government spending (incl. transfers), over one year.
  • Deficit: spending > revenue. Surplus: revenue > spending. Balanced budget: equal.
  • Illustrative data (billion): tax revenue 900 · current spending 650 · capital spending 100 · transfers 250.
  • Spending = 650 + 100 + 250 = 1,000.
  • Budget balance = 900 − 1,000 = −100: a deficit of 100.
  • A deficit means the government injects more into the economy than it takes out in tax: other things equal, it adds to AD.
  • UK term: public sector net borrowing (PSNB) is the deficit.
Mission 1

Deficit vs debt

  • Deficit is a flow: how much more the government spends than it raises in one year.
  • National debt is a stock: the total owed, built up from all past borrowing.
  • Each year's deficit is borrowed, so it is added to the debt. A surplus can be used to pay debt down.
  • Picture a bath: the deficit is the water flowing in this year; the debt is the water in the tub.
  • So a smaller deficit still adds to the debt, just more slowly. Paying the debt down needs a surplus.
  • Say: "the deficit fell, so the debt grew more slowly" — not: "the deficit fell, so the debt fell".
Quick check
?A government's deficit falls from 80 billion last year to 50 billion this year. Nothing else changes. What happens to the national debt this year?
Mission 1

Why deficits change: cyclical vs structural

  • In a recession: incomes, profits and spending fall, so tax revenue falls. Unemployment rises, so benefit spending rises. The deficit grows without any new decision.
  • In a boom, the reverse: the deficit shrinks.
  • Cyclical deficit: the part caused by the economy being below its potential output. It disappears when the economy recovers.
  • Structural deficit: the part that remains even when the economy is at potential output (no output gap). It reflects long-run choices about tax rates and spending.
  • Actual deficit = cyclical part + structural part.
  • Other factors that change the size of a deficit: discretionary decisions on tax and spending · interest rates on the debt · inflation (raises tax revenue and some spending) · the population's age structure (pensions, health).

Mission 1

How deficits are financed

  • Main method: the government sells bonds (UK: gilts). A bond is a promise to pay interest each year and repay the sum at a set date.
  • Buyers: pension funds, insurers, banks, households, foreign investors, and sometimes the central bank.
  • Implications:
  • Interest must be paid every year, from future taxes.
  • If foreigners hold the debt, interest payments flow abroad.
  • Heavy borrowing can push up interest rates and crowd out private investment.
  • Borrowing from the central bank (it creates new money to buy bonds) increases the money supply: it risks inflation if output cannot rise.
Mission 1

Measuring the burden: debt as a % of GDP

  • Debt is compared with nominal GDP, because GDP is the income from which taxes are paid.
  • Debt-to-GDP ratio = (national debt ÷ nominal GDP) × 100.
  • Rough figures for gross government debt, 2023: Japan over 200% · Italy about 135% · US about 120% · UK about 100% · Germany about 62%.
  • Worked example (illustrative data, billion): debt 800 · nominal GDP 1,000 → ratio 80%.
  • Next year nominal GDP grows 5% to 1,050.

Mission 1

Why high debt can limit a government

Costs of a high debt:

  • Debt interest: money spent on interest cannot be spent on schools or health, or returned in lower taxes.
  • Credit ratings: agencies grade the risk that a government will not repay. A downgrade can raise the interest rate it must pay.
  • Example: in April 2010 Greece's rating was cut to "junk" by S&P. Its 10-year bond yields rose above 10%, and it needed EU and IMF loans.
  • Future taxes and spending: higher taxes or spending cuts may be needed later.
  • Less room to borrow in the next recession.

Possible benefits of a deficit:

  • It supports AD and jobs in a recession.
  • Borrowing for capital spending can raise future output, which helps pay the interest.
  • Government bonds give pension funds and savers a safe asset.
Mission 1Evaluate

Is a deficit a problem?

It depends on:

  • Why it exists: a cyclical deficit falls as the economy recovers; a structural deficit does not.
  • What it pays for: capital spending adds assets that can raise productive capacity; most current spending pays for today's services.
  • Interest rate vs growth: if the interest rate on the debt is below the growth rate of nominal GDP, the debt ratio is easier to keep stable.
  • Who lends: domestic lenders, long-dated bonds and borrowing in the country's own currency usually reduce the risk of a sudden crisis.
  • Starting level and confidence: the same deficit worries lenders more when debt is already high.
  • Economists disagree on how high is too high. There is no single safe debt ratio.
Mission check

?Illustrative data: a government's national debt is 300 billion at the start of Year 1. Its budget balances are: Year 1, surplus of 20 billion · Year 2, surplus of 5 billion · Year 3, deficit of 15 billion. What is the national debt at the end of Year 3?

The deficit is the yearly flow; the debt is the stock. Every deficit adds to the debt, however small.

Mission 2: Can a tax take more money from richer people and still hit poorer people harder?

Make a guess: Can a tax take more money from richer people and still hit poorer people harder?

Mission 2

Direct and indirect taxes

  • Direct tax: a tax on income or wealth, paid straight to the government by the person or firm it is levied on.
    • Income tax, corporation tax (on company profits), taxes on wealth.
  • UK direct taxes also include National Insurance contributions, capital gains tax and inheritance tax.
  • Indirect tax: a tax on spending on goods and services. The seller pays it to the government, but can pass some or all of it on to buyers in higher prices.
    • VAT (a sales tax), excise duties on fuel, alcohol and tobacco, tariffs on imports.
  • How much of an indirect tax buyers bear depends on elasticity.
  • Say: "VAT is an indirect tax" — not: "VAT is a direct tax because shoppers pay it directly at the till".
Mission 2

Why tax, and what makes a good tax

  • Reasons to tax:
    • raise revenue for public spending
    • redistribute income and wealth
    • correct market failure (taxes on pollution, tobacco)
    • manage aggregate demand (fiscal policy)
  • UK: the three biggest sources of tax revenue are income tax, National Insurance contributions and VAT. Others: corporation tax, fuel duty, council tax, business rates.
  • Main spending categories: social protection (pensions, benefits), health, education, defence, debt interest (see Mission 1).
  • Adam Smith's canons of a good tax (1776):
    • equity: in line with ability to pay
    • certainty: clear what, when and how much
    • convenience: easy to pay
    • economy: cheap to collect
  • Modern additions: efficiency (few distortions to choices) and flexibility (easy to change).

Mission 2

Progressive, proportional, regressive

  • Judge a tax by the share of income it takes, not the amount.
  • Progressive: the share of income paid in tax rises as income rises.
  • Proportional: the share stays the same at every income.
  • Regressive: the share falls as income rises.
  • A regressive tax can still take more money from richer people.
  • Say: "a regressive tax takes a larger proportion of a low income" — not: "poor people pay more money".
Mission 2

Average and marginal tax rates

  • Average tax rate (ART) = total tax ÷ total income × 100.
  • Marginal tax rate (MRT) = change in tax ÷ change in income × 100: the tax on the next unit of income.
  • Progressive: MRT > ART, so ART rises with income.
  • Proportional: MRT = ART.
  • Regressive: ART falls as income rises.
  • Moving into a higher band raises the rate only on income inside that band.
Mission 2

Worked example: income tax bands

  • Illustrative data.

    • first 10,000: 0

  • ART rises from 13.3% to 23.3% as income rises: progressive.

Mission 2

Flat taxes

  • A flat tax: one rate on all income above a tax-free allowance.
  • Illustrative data: 20% on income above 12,000.

  • With an allowance, a flat tax is mildly progressive. With no allowance, it is proportional.
  • Real case: Estonia introduced a flat income tax in 1994; several central and eastern European countries followed.
  • Supporters: simple, cheap to collect, keeps the reward for earning more. Critics: less redistribution than several rising bands.
Mission 2Evaluate

Should a country make its taxes more progressive?

It depends on:

  • How people respond: if high earners cut work, move or avoid tax, revenue may rise less than planned.
  • Value judgements about equity: how much inequality is acceptable is a normative question.
  • Which taxes change: raising income tax rates at the top differs from cutting regressive taxes on spending.
  • Administration: complex systems cost more to run and leave more room for avoidance.
Mission check

Mission 3

Mission 3: If a kitchen hires twice as many cooks, why might it not make twice as many meals?

Make a guess: If a kitchen hires twice as many cooks, why might it not make twice as many meals?

Mission 3

Short run and long run

  • Fixed factor: an input whose quantity cannot be changed quickly, e.g. a kitchen, ovens, a factory building.
  • Variable factor: an input that can be changed quickly, e.g. hours of work, ingredients, energy.
  • Short run: at least one factor of production is fixed.
  • Long run: all factors are variable. The firm can build a bigger kitchen.
  • The split is not a set number of months: a market stall can grow in weeks; a power station takes years.
Mission 3

Total, average and marginal product

  • Total product (TP): total output from a given number of workers.
  • Average product (AP): output per worker. AP = TP ÷ number of workers.
  • Marginal product (MP): the extra output from one more worker. MP = change in TP ÷ change in workers.
  • Marginal product is also called marginal returns; AP is average returns.
  • The fixed factor stays the same throughout.

Mission 3

The law of diminishing returns

  • Law of diminishing marginal returns: in the short run, as more of a variable factor is added to a fixed factor, the marginal product of the variable factor eventually falls.
  • Why: each extra cook has less of the fixed factor to work with: fewer ovens and less space per cook.
  • It is not because later cooks are worse: all cooks are assumed identical.
  • It applies only in the short run. In the long run nothing is fixed.
  • Say: "diminishing marginal returns: MP falls" — not: "total output falls".

Mission 3

How MP and AP fit together

  • When MP is above AP, AP rises: a cook who adds more than the average pulls the average up.
  • When MP is below AP, AP falls.
  • So MP cuts AP at AP's maximum (in the café kitchen: the 3rd cook, MP = AP = 12).
  • TP is at its maximum when MP = 0 (the 6th cook).
  • Same logic as test scores: a new score above your average raises it.
Mission 3

Labour productivity

  • Labour productivity = output ÷ labour input: output per worker or per hour worked.
  • What raises it:
    • more and better capital per worker (machines, computers)
    • technology: better methods and equipment
    • human capital: education, training, skills, health
    • better management and organisation; motivation
    • infrastructure (transport, broadband)
  • More workers sharing the same equipment means less capital per worker, so output per worker can fall.
  • Productivity is not production: a firm can produce more by hiring more workers while output per worker stays the same.
Mission 3

Worked example: why productivity matters

  • Illustrative data. A worker paid 600 a week makes 300 units.
  • Unit labour cost = 600 ÷ 300 = 2 per unit.
  • Training raises output to 360 units: productivity up (360 − 300) ÷ 300 × 100 = 20%.
  • Same wage: unit labour cost = 600 ÷ 360 ≈ 1.67.
  • Even if the wage rises 10% to 660: 660 ÷ 360 ≈ 1.83, still below 2.
  • Significance:
    • Competitiveness: lower costs per unit let firms cut prices or raise profits, at home and in export markets.
    • Wages: higher productivity lets firms pay higher real wages without raising costs per unit.
    • Growth: higher output per worker raises an economy's productive potential.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Current, capital and transfer spending. Transfers are not part of G.
  • Deficit = flow (one year); debt = stock (all past borrowing). A smaller deficit still adds to the debt.
  • Actual deficit = cyclical + structural; recovery removes only the cyclical part.
  • The burden is measured as debt ÷ nominal GDP; the ratio can fall even when the debt rises.

Mission 2

  • Direct taxes fall on income and wealth. Indirect taxes fall on spending.
  • Progressive, proportional or regressive: judged by the share of income paid (ART), not the amount.
  • ART = total tax ÷ income. MRT = tax on the next unit of income.

Mission 3

  • Short run: at least one factor fixed. Long run: all factors variable.
  • Law of diminishing marginal returns: adding a variable factor to a fixed factor eventually makes MP fall.
  • Labour productivity is output per worker or per hour; it rises with capital per worker, technology and human capital.
