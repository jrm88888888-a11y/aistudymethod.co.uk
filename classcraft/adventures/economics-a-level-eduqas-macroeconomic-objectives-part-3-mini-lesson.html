Mission 1

Four words for changing prices

  • Inflation: a sustained rise in the general price level.
  • Deflation: a sustained fall in the price level. The inflation rate is negative.
  • Disinflation: the inflation rate falls but stays positive. Prices still rise, more slowly.
  • Hyperinflation: extremely rapid inflation (a common benchmark: over 50% a month). Money loses its value fast.
  • Say: "inflation fell from 4% to 2%: disinflation" — not: "prices fell".
Mission 1

Worked example: reading the inflation rate

  • Illustrative data. Consumer price index (CPI) at the end of each year:

Mission 1

Expected or unexpected?

  • Nominal: measured in money. Real: adjusted for inflation (what the money buys).
  • Real interest rate ≈ nominal interest rate − inflation rate.
  • If inflation is fully expected, people build it in:
    • wages, pensions and benefits rise with prices
    • lenders set interest rates high enough to cover it
  • Then real incomes and real debts stay about the same.
  • If inflation is unexpected, contracts fixed in money terms no longer match prices. Real values change: wealth is redistributed.
  • Even fully expected inflation still has some costs.
  • Say: "a fall in real income" — not: "income fell" when money income rose more slowly than prices.
Mission 1

Worked example: who wins from a surprise?

  • Illustrative data. A bank lends 10,000 for one year at a fixed 5%. Repayment: 10,500.
  • Both sides expect 2% inflation. Expected real interest rate ≈ 5 − 2 = 3%.
  • Inflation turns out to be 7%.
  • Real interest rate ≈ 5 − 7 = −2%.
  • In start-of-year prices, 10,500 ÷ 1.07 = 9,813. The bank gets back less buying power than it lent.
  • Lender loses; borrower gains.
  • Also losing: savers on fixed rates, workers on fixed money wages, people on fixed money pensions.
  • Unexpected deflation works the other way: lenders gain, borrowers lose.

Mission 1

The costs of inflation

  • Menu costs: time and money spent changing prices (price lists, labels, websites).
  • Shoe-leather costs: time and effort spent holding less cash and moving money around to protect its value.
  • Uncertainty: high, variable inflation makes future costs and revenue hard to predict, so firms invest less. Growth can suffer.
  • Savings discouraged: if interest rates lag behind inflation, saving loses real value.
  • International competitiveness: if inflation is higher than in trading partners, exports get dearer and imports relatively cheaper (unless the exchange rate falls to offset it). The current account can worsen.
  • Inefficient resource allocation: when all prices rise fast, changes in relative prices are hard to spot, so price signals work less well.
  • Fiscal drag: if tax thresholds stay fixed while pay rises with prices, more income is taxed at higher rates.
  • Most costs grow with the rate of inflation and with how unpredictable it is.
Mission 1

Hyperinflation: when money stops working

  • At very high inflation, money fails at its jobs:
    • store of value: savings in cash are wiped out
    • medium of exchange: sellers refuse it and people turn to barter or foreign currency
    • unit of account: prices change too fast to compare
  • Real world: Zimbabwe's official annual inflation reached 231 million per cent in July 2008.
  • In 2009 Zimbabwe allowed foreign currencies such as the US dollar and the South African rand, and people stopped using its own currency.
  • Hyperinflations usually end only with a new currency or a new monetary regime (money growth).
Mission 1

Deflation: good news or bad?

  • Benign ("good") deflation: SRAS shifts right (higher productivity, cheaper commodities, new technology).
    • Price level falls, real output rises, unemployment falls.
  • Malign ("bad") deflation: AD shifts left (weak confidence, a credit crunch, a world recession).
    • Price level falls, real output falls, unemployment rises.
  • Most worries about deflation are about the malign kind.

Mission 1

The costs of deflation

  • Deferred consumption: if prices are expected to fall, households wait to buy. AD falls further.
  • Real value of debt rises: debts are fixed in money, but prices and incomes fall. Borrowers cut spending; some go bankrupt.
  • Unemployment: firms' revenue falls, but money wages are sticky downwards, so real wages rise and firms cut jobs (cyclical unemployment).
  • Policy ineffectiveness: interest rates cannot go far below zero, so real interest rates (≈ nominal − inflation) stay high when prices fall.
  • Deflationary spiral: falling prices → spending delayed → AD falls → output and jobs fall → prices fall further → …
  • Real world: US consumer prices fell by about a quarter from 1929 to 1933.
  • Real world: Japan's consumer prices fell in most years from 1999 to 2012, with interest rates close to zero.

Mission 1Evaluate

How much does inflation hurt?

It depends on:

  • The rate and its stability. Low, steady inflation costs little; high, volatile inflation costs a lot.
  • Whether it is expected. Expected inflation is built into wages and interest rates; surprises redistribute.
  • Indexation. Pensions, benefits and tax thresholds linked to prices protect people on fixed incomes.
  • Inflation abroad. What matters for competitiveness is inflation relative to trading partners, and the exchange rate.
  • Low inflation is not zero inflation. Many central banks target about 2%: a small positive rate lets real wages adjust without money pay cuts and leaves room above zero for interest rates.
Mission check

What matters is the surprise: when prices do not match what contracts assumed, wealth moves between lenders and borrowers.

Mission 2: Why can the right cure for one slump be the wrong cure for the next?

Make a guess: When an economy slumps, what should a government do first?

Mission 2

The toolkit and the policy mix

  • Three sets of tools:
    • Fiscal policy: government spending and taxes.
    • Monetary policy: interest rates, the money supply, QE.
    • Supply-side policies: raise productive capacity (education, incentives, competition).
  • Policy mix: how these are combined at one time.
  • Fiscal and monetary policy can push the same way (both expansionary) or opposite ways (one expansionary, one contractionary).
  • Policies that shift AD are demand-side; policies that shift LRAS are supply-side.
Mission 2

Combining fiscal and monetary policy

  • Step 1: start with real output below full employment.
  • This is a negative output gap.
  • Step 2: both expansionary: AD shifts right for two reasons. Real output and the price level both rise.
  • Step 3: the interest rate:
    • Fiscal expansion raises incomes and spending, so demand for money rises: rates are pushed up.
    • Monetary expansion raises the money supply: rates are pushed down.
    • Overall change: indeterminate. It depends on which effect is bigger.
  • Opposite directions flip this: the interest-rate change is certain, the AD change is not.
    • Expansionary fiscal + contractionary monetary: interest rate up; AD uncertain.
    • Contractionary fiscal + expansionary monetary: interest rate down; AD uncertain.
  • Say: "the change in the interest rate is indeterminate" — not: "it stays the same".

Mission 2

Demand shocks and supply shocks

  • An external shock starts outside the economy and outside the government's control.
  • Demand shock: e.g. a world recession cuts demand for exports.
    • AD shifts left. Real output and the price level fall.
    • Expansionary policy can push both back up: no conflict.
  • Supply shock: e.g. world energy prices jump.
    • SRAS shifts left. Real output falls and the price level rises: stagflation.
    • Raising AD helps jobs but adds to inflation. Cutting AD slows inflation but deepens the slump: a policy dilemma.
  • First step in any crisis: diagnose the shock.
  • Say: "a cost-push supply shock" — not: "inflation caused by too much spending".
Mission 2

Conflicts and trade-offs

  • Hitting one objective can make another harder:
    • Inflation vs unemployment: in the short run, along the SRPC, lower inflation may mean higher unemployment.
    • Growth vs the current account: faster growth raises imports.
    • Growth vs the environment: more output can mean more pollution and resource use.
    • Growth vs equality: gains may go mostly to some groups.
  • Unintended consequences: e.g. very low interest rates for years can push up house and share prices.
  • Macroeconomic perspectives differ:
    • Keynesian: markets can stay in a slump for a long time; demand-side policy helps.
    • Classical / monetarist: markets self-correct; policy should keep inflation low and boost supply.
  • Say: "a trade-off" when one objective is given up for another — not: "the policy failed".
Quick check
?A country that imports most of its energy is hit by a sharp rise in world energy prices. Unemployment and inflation both rise. Which statement is most accurate?
Mission 2

The Great Depression

  • USA, 1929–33: real GDP fell by about a quarter; unemployment reached about 25% in 1933.
  • Thousands of banks failed. The money supply fell by about a third.
    • The Federal Reserve did little to stop it.
    • The Smoot–Hawley tariffs (1930) raised taxes on imports; other countries hit back.
  • Recovery began after 1933: the US left the gold standard; the New Deal brought public works and bank reform.
  • UK: governments aimed to balance the budget and cut spending in 1931.
    • The UK left the gold standard in 1931. Bank Rate was cut to 2% in 1932: "cheap money".
  • Keynes's General Theory (1936) argued that governments should raise spending in a slump.
  • Two readings, both still argued:
    • Keynesian: a collapse in spending that budget-balancing made worse.
    • Monetarist: a collapse in money that the central bank allowed.
Mission 2

The 1970s: when the old cure stopped working

  • After 1945, full employment was the top priority. Governments managed AD to keep unemployment low.
  • 1973–74: oil prices roughly quadrupled. A supply shock.
  • Result: stagflation. UK inflation (RPI) peaked at about 27% in 1975.
  • Raising AD to cut unemployment now pushed inflation even higher.
  • Direct controls on inflation were tried:
    • Incomes policies: legal or agreed limits on pay rises (UK, 1970s).
    • Price controls: the US froze wages and prices for 90 days in 1971.
  • Problems with direct controls:
    • They treat symptoms, not causes.
    • Shortages and black markets where prices are held below equilibrium.
    • Hard to enforce; pent-up pay and price rises when they end.
Mission 2

Monetarism and new priorities

  • Late 1970s: inflation became the top priority.
  • USA: from 1979, the Fed (chair Paul Volcker) cut money growth. The federal funds rate reached nearly 20% in 1980–81.
    • Inflation fell from 13.5% (1980) to 3.2% (1983).
    • The cost: a deep recession; unemployment hit 10.8% in 1982.
  • UK: from 1980, the government set targets for money supply growth (monetarism).
    • Inflation fell, but unemployment rose above 3 million in the 1980s.
    • Money targets proved hard to hit and were dropped.
  • 1992: the UK set an inflation target. 1997: the Bank of England was given control of interest rates.
  • Since 2008, financial stability has moved up the list.
Mission 2

2008: the response

  • The global financial crisis began in the banks: lending froze, AD collapsed.
  • UK:
    • Bank Rate cut from 5% (2008) to 0.5% (March 2009).
    • Quantitative easing: the Bank of England bought £200bn of assets, mainly government bonds, by early 2010.
    • VAT cut from 17.5% to 15% for 13 months.
    • Government stakes in failing banks (RBS, Lloyds).
  • USA:
    • Federal funds rate cut to 0–0.25% (December 2008); large-scale asset purchases (QE).
    • $700bn fund to rescue banks (TARP, 2008).
    • $787bn stimulus package (2009).
  • Lessons drawn from the 1930s: stop the money supply and banks collapsing; support AD.
Mission 2

After 2008: the austerity debate

  • From 2010, the UK and many other countries cut deficits: fiscal consolidation ("austerity").
  • Arguments for:
    • High debt can raise borrowing costs. In 2010, Greece's borrowing costs soared.
    • A credible plan may boost confidence and keep interest rates low.
    • Smaller deficits leave room to respond to the next shock.
  • Arguments against:
    • Cutting spending in a weak economy reduces AD and growth.
    • A study by IMF economists (2013) found the multiplier in the crisis was larger than forecasters assumed, so cuts hit output harder.
    • With interest rates near zero, monetary policy could not offset the cuts.
  • Your judgement depends on: the size of the multiplier, the level of debt, and market confidence.
Quick check
?In the USA from 1929 to 1933, the money supply fell by about a third as thousands of banks failed. Which response in 2008 most clearly reflects that lesson?
Mission 2

Problems and constraints for policymakers

  • Time lags: spotting a problem, acting and the effect can take 18 months or more.
  • Poor data and forecasts: GDP figures are revised; the size of the output gap is uncertain.
  • The zero lower bound: rates near 0% leave little room to cut.
  • High public debt limits fiscal room.
  • A global economy:
    • External shocks (energy prices, world recessions, pandemics) are outside any one government's control.
    • Capital flows and exchange rates can undo interest-rate changes.
    • Low-income countries face extra limits: poverty, weak tax bases, costly borrowing.
    • Competitiveness depends on supply-side factors that change slowly.
  • Expectations and politics: short-termism before elections; rising inflation expectations.
  • Result: government failure in macro policy is possible: a policy that arrives late or is the wrong size can destabilise the economy.
Mission 2Evaluate

Which policy works best?

  • Criteria for success: are the targets met (e.g. 2% inflation)? At what cost? How fast? With what side-effects? Who gains and who loses?
  • Relative effectiveness:
    • Monetary: quick to change, but weak near 0% rates and slow to work (about 18–24 months).
    • Fiscal: strong in a deep slump, but slow to agree and limited by debt.
    • Supply-side: lasting gains in capacity, but they take years.
  • It depends on:
    • The cause of the problem: demand shock or supply shock?
    • Spare capacity: a big gap favours stimulus; little spare capacity means inflation.
    • The state of public finances and how open the economy is.
Mission check

?Two economies both face rising unemployment. In Economy X, demand for its exports has collapsed and inflation is falling below target. In Economy Y, the price of imported food and fuel has doubled and inflation is rising above target. Which judgement is best?

Diagnose before you prescribe: a demand shock calls for stimulus, a supply shock forces a choice.

Mission 3

Why trade patterns change

  • Pattern of trade: what a country exports and imports, and with whom.
  • It changes because of:
    • Comparative advantage shifts: skills, technology and investment change relative costs.
    • Emerging economies grow: new low-cost producers and fast-growing markets (China, India, Vietnam).
    • Trading blocs and bilateral agreements: trade grows inside a bloc; leaving one raises barriers.
    • Exchange rates: a depreciation makes exports cheaper abroad and imports dearer at home.
    • Income growth abroad: richer buyers want more services and high-value goods.
    • Transport and communication costs: lower costs make distant partners worthwhile.
Mission 3

The UK's changing trade

  • Partners: the EU bought 47% of UK exports in 2010 and 2011, and 42% in 2023. It is still the UK's largest partner (52% of UK imports in 2023).
  • Products: services rose from 34% of UK exports in 2000 to 52% in 2023.
  • Reasons:
    • faster growth in markets outside Europe (US, Asia)
    • leaving the EU single market and customs union (2021) raised barriers to EU trade
    • comparative advantage in services: finance, business services, education, creative industries
    • decline of manufacturing under competition from emerging economies
Quick check

Mission 3

The terms of trade

  • Terms of trade: the rate at which a country's exports exchange for its imports.
  • Measured as an index:
  • Terms of trade = (index of average export prices ÷ index of average import prices) × 100
  • Base year = 100.
  • Index rises: an improvement (favourable movement): each unit of exports buys more imports.
  • Index falls: a deterioration (unfavourable movement): each unit of exports buys fewer imports.
  • Say: "the terms of trade improved" — not: "the trade balance improved". They are different measures.

Mission 3

What changes the terms of trade

Short run:

  • Exchange rate: an appreciation raises export prices in foreign currency and lowers import prices at home: the terms of trade improve. A depreciation worsens them.
  • Relative inflation: faster inflation at home raises export prices relative to import prices.
  • World commodity prices: changes in world demand or supply for oil, metals, food.

Long run:

  • Relative productivity: faster productivity growth cuts costs and export prices.
  • Incomes: as world incomes rise, demand for food and raw materials (low YED) grows more slowly than for manufactures and services.
  • Competition: low-cost producers in emerging economies push down world prices of many manufactures.

Mission 3

Impact: it depends on why

An improvement in the terms of trade:

  • Demand-led (world demand for the exports rose): higher export revenue, incomes and jobs; the current account improves; each export buys more imports, so living standards rise.
  • Cost-led (appreciation, high domestic inflation): exports lose price competitiveness. If demand is price elastic: revenue falls, jobs go, the current account worsens.

A deterioration:

  • Imports cost more: imported inflation and lower real incomes.
  • If caused by a depreciation, exports become cheaper abroad: export volumes rise and the current account can improve.
  • Whether it does depends on the price elasticities of demand for exports and imports: the Marshall–Lerner condition.
Quick check

Mission 3

Evaluate: is an improvement good news?

It depends on…

  • Why it happened. Rising world demand for exports usually helps; higher domestic costs or an appreciation may hurt.
  • Price elasticities of demand for exports and imports.
  • Time. Trade volumes respond slowly, so short-run and long-run effects differ.
  • Openness. Where trade is a large share of GDP, the effects are larger.
  • What is imported. Dearer energy and food raise firms' costs and hit poorer households hardest.
  • Real case: Japan's index fell from 106.5 to 79.3 between 2020 and 2022: each unit of exports bought about 26% fewer imports.

Mission 4

The nation's accounts

  • The balance of payments records all transactions between a country's residents and the rest of the world over a period (a quarter or a year).
  • Credit (+): money flows in (a payment received from abroad).
  • Debit (−): money flows out (a payment made abroad).
  • Three accounts:
    • current account: trade and income flows
    • capital account: capital transfers (small)
    • financial account: buying and selling assets, lending and borrowing
  • Purpose: it shows how a country pays its way in the world, and whether it is borrowing from abroad or lending.
  • The current account balance, often as a % of GDP, is a key macroeconomic indicator.

  • Primary income: wages, interest, profits and dividends earned abroad.
  • Secondary income: transfers with nothing in return.
  • Credits > debits on an account: a surplus. Debits > credits: a deficit.
  • Say: "a current account deficit" when debits exceed credits — not: "the country made a loss".

Mission 4

Capital and financial accounts

  • Capital account (small): capital transfers, e.g. debt forgiveness; buying or selling non-produced assets, e.g. land for an embassy or a brand name.
  • Financial account: cross-border buying and selling of assets.
    • Foreign direct investment (FDI): buying at least 10% of a firm, or building a factory abroad. A lasting interest and some control.
    • Portfolio investment: buying shares (under 10%) or bonds for a financial return. No control, and easy to sell quickly.
    • Other investment: bank loans and deposits.
    • Reserve assets: the central bank's foreign currency and gold.
  • Credit (+): foreign residents buy our assets or lend to us (money in).
  • Debit (−): our residents buy foreign assets; or the central bank adds to its reserves (money out).
  • Say: "FDI is a financial account flow" — not: "an export", even though money comes in.
Mission 4

Why it always sums to zero

  • Every transaction is recorded twice: once as a credit and once as a debit (double-entry).
  • Example: a firm imports machines worth 10 bn.
    • Current account: import of goods, −10.
    • The foreign seller is paid in our currency and holds it in our banks: a foreign-owned asset, so financial account +10.
  • So a current account deficit is matched by a net inflow on the financial account: borrowing from abroad, selling assets to foreigners, or running down reserves.
  • Current + capital + financial account + net errors and omissions = 0.
  • Errors and omissions: a balancing item for transactions that are missed or measured wrongly.
  • Say: "a current account deficit" — not: "a balance of payments deficit". The balance of payments as a whole always balances.

Mission 5

Why imbalances arise

  • Cyclical deficit: in a boom, incomes rise and spending on imports rises. Firms also sell more at home and less abroad. It shrinks in a downturn.
  • Structural deficit: it persists even at normal levels of output, because of weak competitiveness:
    • productivity growing more slowly than in other countries
    • inflation (costs and prices) higher than in trading partners
    • a strong, overvalued exchange rate
    • non-price weakness: quality, design, a small export sector
  • Saving and investment: a country that invests more than it saves must borrow the gap from abroad, so it runs a current account deficit. A country that saves more than it invests runs a surplus.
  • External shocks: a rise in oil prices widens deficits of oil importers and surpluses of oil exporters; a world recession cuts everyone's exports.
  • Say: "a current account deficit" — not: "a balance of payments deficit".
Quick check
?Illustrative data. Country Q's current account deficit widened from 1% to 4% of GDP during a two-year consumer boom. When growth slowed, it shrank back to 1%. Q's costs and prices rose no faster than its trading partners'. What best describes the extra deficit?
Mission 5

A persistent deficit: why it can matter

  • Exchange rate: pressure to depreciate; dearer imports add to inflation.
  • Interest rates: higher rates may be needed to attract the inflows that finance the deficit. This hurts investment.
  • Foreign ownership: assets are sold to foreigners, so future profits and dividends flow out (primary income debits).
  • Debt: borrowing builds up debt to the rest of the world, with interest to pay.
  • Credit rating: a large, persistent deficit can lower it, raising the cost of borrowing.
  • Demand management: the government may have to use contractionary fiscal or monetary policy to cut spending on imports, at a cost to output and jobs.
  • Economic growth: negative net exports are a drag on AD now, and jobs are lost in export and import-competing industries. But if the deficit pays for investment, it can raise growth later.
Mission 5

A persistent surplus: problems too

  • Domestic consumption and investment: lower than the economy's output. Living standards could be higher now.
  • Exchange rate: pressure to appreciate, which erodes export competitiveness over time.
  • Inflation: strong export demand adds to AD and can pull prices up; an appreciating currency makes imports cheaper, which pulls the other way.
  • Employment: good for jobs in export industries, while it lasts.
  • The surplus is lent or invested abroad: it may earn low returns or be lost.
  • One country's surplus is others' deficit: trading partners may complain or turn to protectionism.

Mission 5

Two kinds of policy

  • Expenditure switching: changes relative prices, so spending switches from imports to home-produced goods.
    • a depreciation or devaluation of the currency
    • protectionism: tariffs, quotas, export subsidies
  • Expenditure reducing: cuts total spending, so imports fall too.
    • contractionary fiscal policy: higher taxes, lower government spending
    • contractionary monetary policy: higher interest rates
  • Supply-side policies: long-term competitiveness: training, infrastructure, research, flexible markets.
  • Say: "expenditure reducing cuts all spending, so imports fall with it" — not: "it cuts spending on imports only".
Mission 5

Worked example: the cost of expenditure reducing

  • Illustrative data. Current account deficit: 20 bn. GDP: 1,000 bn.
  • For every 1 bn fall in income, spending on imports falls by 0.25 bn.
  • This is the marginal propensity to import (MPM) = 0.25.
  • Fall in income needed = 20 ÷ 0.25 = 80 bn.
  • That is 80 ÷ 1,000 × 100 = 8% of GDP: a deep recession, with higher unemployment.
  • Not 20 bn: most of the lost spending falls on domestic goods, not imports.

  • Takeaway: the smaller the share of extra income spent on imports, the bigger the fall in output needed. Expenditure reducing can be costly.

Mission 5

Switching, supply side and surpluses

  • Depreciation: works only if demand for exports and imports is elastic enough (Marshall–Lerner), and only after a lag (J-curve); dearer imports raise inflation.
  • Protectionism: risks retaliation, can break trade rules (WTO), and raises prices for consumers and firms.
  • Supply-side policies: tackle the cause of a structural deficit, but are slow and uncertain.
  • To reduce a surplus:
    • expansionary fiscal or monetary policy: more spending at home, so more imports
    • allow the currency to appreciate
    • remove barriers to imports
  • Structural imbalances need structural solutions: supply-side policy, not only demand management.
Mission 5Evaluate

How much does a deficit matter, and which policy works?

It depends on:

  • The cause: a cyclical deficit corrects itself; a structural one needs supply-side policy.
  • Size, persistence and financing: a small deficit funded by long-term FDI is safer than a large one funded by short-term portfolio flows ("hot money"), which can leave suddenly.
  • What it pays for: investment can raise future output; consumption cannot repay the debt.
  • Conflicts with other objectives: expenditure reducing costs growth and jobs; depreciation adds to inflation.
  • Elasticities and time lags: switching works slowly, and only if demand responds enough.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Inflation is a rising price level; disinflation is a falling positive rate; deflation is a falling price level.
  • Unexpected inflation moves wealth from lenders, savers and people on fixed incomes to borrowers; unexpected deflation does the reverse.
  • Inflation also brings menu and shoe-leather costs, uncertainty and weaker competitiveness; malign deflation can spiral into falling output and jobs.

Mission 2

  • The policy mix combines fiscal, monetary and supply-side tools. When two policies push the same way, the AD change is certain and the interest-rate change is not, and the reverse.
  • Diagnose the shock first: demand shocks allow stimulus without conflict; supply shocks force a trade-off.
  • Priorities shifted: full employment after 1945, inflation from the late 1970s, financial stability after 2008. Each crisis was fought with lessons from the last.

Mission 3

  • Trade patterns change with comparative advantage, emerging economies, trading blocs, exchange rates, incomes and transport costs.
  • UK trade has shifted towards services and towards markets outside the EU.
  • Terms of trade = (export price index ÷ import price index) × 100; a rise is an improvement.
  • Whether an improvement is good news depends on its cause and on price elasticities.

Mission 4

  • Current account: goods, services, primary income, secondary income.
  • Capital and financial accounts record capital transfers and the buying and selling of assets (FDI, portfolio, other investment, reserves).
  • Current + capital + financial + errors and omissions = 0.

Mission 5

  • Imbalances can be cyclical or structural, and reflect saving and investment.
  • Persistent deficits and persistent surpluses both carry costs.
  • Expenditure switching changes relative prices; expenditure reducing cuts total spending (and output); supply-side policy works on competitiveness over time.
