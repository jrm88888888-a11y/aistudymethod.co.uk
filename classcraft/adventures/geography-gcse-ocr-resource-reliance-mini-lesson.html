Mission 1

Why food, water and energy matter

  • Food: health, growth and the energy to work and learn. In 2025, 645 million people were chronically undernourished.
  • Water: drinking, sanitation, farming and industry. About 2.1 billion people lack safely managed drinking water.
  • Energy: light, cooking, industry and transport. In 2024, 655 million people had no electricity.
  • Without them, people are less healthy (social well-being) and less productive (economic well-being).
Mission 1

Unequal use

  • Energy per person, 2024: USA 76,800 kWh; India 7,813 kWh.
  • Water footprint per consumer (1996–2005): USA 2,842 m³ a year; India 1,089 m³.
  • If everyone lived like the average person in Qatar, a year's nature would be used up by 4 February 2026; for the UK, by 22 May.
  • High-income countries consume far more per person; many low-income countries lack enough.
Quick check
?The average US consumer's water footprint is 2,842 m³ a year; India's is 1,089 m³. How many times as large is the US figure?
Mission 1

When demand outstrips supply

  • Demand rises: world population 3.06 billion (1961) → 8.06 billion (2023).
  • Wealth: richer diets need more land and water; China's meat supply reached 73.5 kg per person in 2023.
  • Supply is limited: arable land per person halved, 0.359 → 0.173 ha (1961–2023).
  • Climate change has "reduced food and water security" (IPCC, high confidence).
  • Conflict and disruption can cut supply suddenly, as at the Strait of Hormuz in 2026.
Mission 1

One system: water, food and energy

  • Farming uses water: 72% of freshwater withdrawals.
  • Food uses energy: the food chain uses about 30% of the world's energy.
  • Water uses energy: to "extract, pump, lift, collect, transport and treat water".
  • So a shock to one resource can spread to the others.
Mission 1

The 2026 Hormuz shock

  • In 2025, about 20 million barrels of oil a day passed through the Strait of Hormuz.
  • In March–May 2026, flows averaged about 2.7 million a day.
  • The UN: this hit "key inputs for food production – including fertilizers, sulphur, natural gas and fuels", raising costs across food systems.
  • "Import-dependent countries are especially vulnerable".
Quick check
?Which country was most exposed to the 2026 Hormuz disruption?
Mission check

?A long drought lowers rivers, so hydroelectric stations make less electricity and its price rises. Many farmers there use electric pumps to irrigate. What is the most likely effect on food?

Food, water and energy are linked. In 2026, an energy shock at Hormuz made fertiliser, gas and fuel dearer, so farmers and import-dependent countries far away felt it.

Mission 2: Forests, reefs and rivers feed, shelter and protect us for free. So why do we keep cutting them down?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR GCSE Geography B (J384)

  • 8.1a how humans use and modify environments: mechanised farming and commercial fishing (food), deforestation and mining (energy), reservoirs and water transfer schemes (water)
Mission 2

Goods and services

  • The biosphere is the living layer of the Earth: plants, animals and the soils and waters they live in.
  • Ecosystem services are "the benefits people obtain from ecosystems".
  • Goods: things we take — food, water, timber, fuel, medicine.
  • Services: things ecosystems do for us — protect coasts, regulate climate, clean water, attract tourists.
Mission 2

The goods

  • Food: crops, fish, wild fruit and game.
  • Medicine: "Many drugs are now being developed from compounds discovered in coral reef animals and plants".
  • Building materials: timber, bamboo, thatch.
  • Fuel: wood and charcoal. In 2024 only 22% of people in sub-Saharan Africa had clean cooking, against 75% worldwide; most of the rest cook with fuels such as wood.
Quick check
?Which of these is a good, not a service, from an ecosystem?
Mission 2

The services

  • Natural protection: mangroves "stabilize the coastline, reducing erosion from storm surges, currents, waves, and tides".
  • Climate regulation: about half the CO₂ people emit each year stays in the air; "the rest is absorbed by Earth's land ecosystems and oceans".
  • Tourism: Australia's Great Barrier Reef is reported to contribute $9 billion a year to the economy (a study quoted by the park authority).
  • Health: medicines from reefs and forests, clean water and clean air.
Mission 2

From local use to commercial exploitation

  • Local people have always used ecosystems for subsistence: food, fuel and shelter for their own families.
  • Now ecosystems are exploited commercially: cleared or mined on a large scale to sell goods in global markets.
  • Example (minerals): Indonesia's share of world nickel mine production rose from about 30% in 2019 to about 67% in 2025 (calculated from USGS figures). Nickel is used in electric-vehicle batteries.
  • OCR groups the big uses under food, energy and water (next screens).
Mission 2

Food: mechanised farming

  • Mechanised farming: machines and large fields replace hand labour, so huge areas can be farmed by few people.
  • In South America the area of soybeans "more than doubled from 26.4 Mha to 55.1 Mha" between 2000 and 2019.
  • "Across the continent, 9% of forest loss was converted to soybean by 2016", nearly half of it in Brazil's Cerrado savanna.
  • Modification: natural forest and grassland replaced by one crop (a monoculture).
Mission 2

Food: commercial fishing

  • Large boats with big nets catch fish to sell worldwide.
  • In FAO's latest count, wild catch was 92 million tonnes; fish farming (aquaculture) produced 103 million tonnes.
  • In 2023, 62.4% of assessed fish stocks were "biologically sustainable".
  • Modification: fewer large fish, changed food webs, damage to the seabed from some gear.
Quick check
?In 2023, 62.4% of assessed fish stocks were biologically sustainable. What share were not?
Mission 2

Energy: deforestation and mining

  • Deforestation: forests are cleared for fuelwood, charcoal, farms and mines.
  • World forest area fell from about 4.34 billion hectares in 1990 to 4.14 billion in 2025: a net loss of about 203 million hectares, or 4.7% (calculated from FAO data).
  • Mining for energy: coal and uranium are dug from open pits or deep mines. Open pits remove the whole ecosystem above the rock.
Mission 2

Water: reservoirs and transfer schemes

  • A reservoir is an artificial lake made by building a dam across a valley. The valley behind it is flooded.
  • A water transfer scheme moves water from a wet area to where it is needed, through rivers, pipes or tunnels.
  • Kielder Water, Northumberland (1982), holds about 200 billion litres.
  • Its water can be released into the Tyne, Derwent and Tees to supply homes and industry in north-east England: a reservoir and a transfer scheme.
Quick check
?Which is an example of a water transfer scheme?
Mission 2

Using goods can cost services

  • Between 2000 and 2020, the biggest single cause of mangrove loss worldwide was aquaculture (fish and shrimp ponds): 27% of losses.
  • The pond owner gains a good (shrimp to sell). The coast loses a service (protection from waves).
  • The people who gain are often not the people who lose.
Mission check

?A government plans to clear a mangrove forest on a low coast to build shrimp ponds for export. Which outcome is most likely?

Ecosystems give goods that can be sold (shrimp, timber) and services that are usually free (protection, climate regulation). Clearing for goods is why ecosystems keep being cut down: the losses fall on people who are not paid for them.

Make a guess: In 2023 the world's food supply came to about 3,000 calories per person per day. So why did around 645 million people go hungry in 2025?

Specification: what this mission covers

Mission 3

Food security

  • FAO: "Food security exists when all people, at all times, have physical and economic access to sufficient, safe and nutritious food to meet their dietary needs and food preferences for an active and healthy life".
  • Food insecurity: people cannot get enough safe, nutritious food, all the time.
  • Note "all people" and "access": a country can have plenty of food while some of its people go hungry.
Mission 3

Calories per person: the world pattern

  • FAO measures the food available per person per day in kilocalories (kcal), its dietary energy supply.
  • World: 2,825 kcal in 2010; 3,016 kcal in 2023.
  • 2023: Europe 3,490 · Americas 3,438 · Asia 2,991 · Africa 2,556 kcal per person per day.
  • This is food supply, not what people actually eat (calorie intake): food later wasted in shops and homes still counts.
Mission 3

Surplus and deficit

  • Some countries have far more food per person than others. 2023, kcal per person per day: USA 3,947 · Brazil 3,423 · India 2,669 · Ethiopia 2,558.
  • A food surplus: a country produces more than it needs and can export the rest.
  • A food deficit: a country produces less than it needs, so it must import food or its people go short.
  • Richer countries can afford to import food, so a deficit in farming need not mean hunger.
Quick check
?In 2023, Europe's food supply was 3,490 kcal per person per day and Africa's was 2,556. By what percentage was Europe's supply larger than Africa's?
Mission 3

Who goes hungry?

  • 2025: 7.8% of the world's population, around 645 million people, were undernourished: their calorie intake was too low.
  • Africa: about 309 million hungry people, 20.0% of its population; Asia: 292 million.
  • A wider measure, moderate or severe food insecurity (2025): Africa 56.6% · Latin America and the Caribbean 22.9% · Asia 20.3% · Northern America and Europe 8.7%.
Mission 3

The Global Hunger Index

  • The Global Hunger Index (GHI) combines four indicators: undernourishment and child deaths (one-third each), child stunting and child wasting (one-sixth each).
  • Scores run from 0 (no hunger) to 100. Bands: low ≤9.9; moderate 10.0–19.9; serious 20.0–34.9; alarming 35.0–49.9; extremely alarming ≥50.0.
  • 2026: world 18.1 (moderate). Hunger is serious in Africa south of the Sahara and South Asia. Somalia scored 47.0, ranked 122nd of 122.
Quick check
?Which of these is not one of the four indicators in the Global Hunger Index?
Mission 3

Physical factors affecting food supply

  • Climate: drought and floods ruin harvests. Drought in 2010–11, then spikes in staple food prices, were causes of Somalia's famine.
  • Pests and disease: from early 2020, a massive desert locust upsurge broke out across eastern Africa, south-west Asia and around the Red Sea. One square kilometre of swarm can eat as much food in a day as 35,000 people.
  • Water stress: north-west India lost groundwater equal to a 4 cm layer of water every year (2002–2008). Rainfall was close to normal; pumping for irrigation and other uses was the likely cause.
Mission 3

Human factors affecting food supply

  • Poverty: people cannot buy food or farm inputs. A healthy diet is unaffordable for one in three people worldwide (SOFI 2026).
  • Conflict: fighting stops farming and blocks aid. Somalia's 2011 famine "predominantly affected Al-Shabaab areas".
  • Technology: seeds, fertiliser and irrigation raise yields. Asia's cereal production almost doubled from 1970 to 1995, "from 313 to 650 million tons per year". Farmers without them get far less from their land.
  • Prices: food prices rise when energy and fertiliser cost more.
Quick check
?Somalia's 2011 famine killed about 258,000 people. Which set of causes best explains it?
Mission check

?Country X has an average food supply of 3,200 kcal per person per day, well above the world average. Yet a survey finds many children in its poorest rural district are stunted. What best explains this?

The 2023 supply of 3,016 kcal per person is an average of food available. Hunger depends on access: whether people can afford or reach food. Africa's supply is 2,556 kcal per person, and 20.0% of Africans are undernourished.

Mission 4: In 1798, Thomas Malthus argued that population would outrun food. Since 1961, the world's population has grown about 2.6 times. Has food per person risen or fallen?

Make a guess: In 1798, Thomas Malthus argued that population would outrun food. Since 1961, the world's population has grown about 2.6 times. Has food per person risen or fallen?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR GCSE Geography B (J384)

  • 8.2a Malthusian versus Boserupian views of population and food supply
Mission 4

The numbers since 1961

  • World population: 3.06 billion (1961) → 8.06 billion (2023).
  • World cereal yield: 1,422 → 4,229 kg per hectare.
  • Food supply per person: 2,196 kcal a day (1961) → 2,884 kcal a day (2013).
  • Yet in 2025, 645 million people were still chronically undernourished.
Quick check
?Food supply rose from 2,196 to 2,884 kcal per person per day (1961–2013). What was the percentage increase?
Mission 4

Malthus: the pessimistic view

  • Thomas Malthus (1798): "Population, when unchecked, increases in a geometrical ratio. Subsistence increases only in an arithmetical ratio".
  • Population doubles again and again (1, 2, 4, 8…); food grows by equal steps (1, 2, 3, 4…).
  • So population would outrun food, until checks cut it:
    • preventive checks lower births, e.g. marrying later
    • positive checks raise deaths, e.g. famine and disease.
Mission 4

Boserup: the optimistic view

  • Ester Boserup (1965) argued the reverse: "population growth is the central source of demand driving the intensification of cultivation".
  • More people push farmers to find new methods, such as irrigation for two or three crops a year and Green Revolution seeds and fertiliser.
  • In short: necessity is the mother of invention.
Quick check
?"As villages grew, farmers dug irrigation channels and began growing two crops a year." Which view does this support?
Mission 4

Who was right?

  • Globally, yields roughly tripled while population grew about 2.6 times (1961–2023): closer to Boserup.
  • But 645 million people were undernourished in 2025: food is not shared evenly.
  • Malthus did not foresee new technology, trade in food, or birth rates falling as incomes rise.
  • Boserup assumed slow growth and time to adapt; fast growth or worn-out soils can outpace new methods.
  • One study of Bangladeshi villages found the answer "depends in a large part on temporal and spatial scale".
Mission check

?In an imaginary country, population doubled between 1990 and 2020, while food production tripled. What happened to food per person?

Since 1961, population grew about 2.6 times, but yields roughly tripled, so food per person rose (2,196 → 2,884 kcal a day, 1961–2013). Yet 645 million people are still undernourished: enough food on average does not mean food for all.

Mission 5

Two ways to grow more food

  • Farm more land: clear forest or grassland, which costs habitats and carbon.
  • Get more from each field (intensification): water, better seeds, fertiliser, new technology.
  • In Asia's Green Revolution, the area planted with cereals grew by just 0.42% a year (1967–82); the extra food came from higher yields.
Mission 5

Irrigation

  • Irrigation: supplying water to crops by canals, wells, pumps, sprinklers or drip pipes.
  • It lets farmers grow crops in dry seasons and dry places, and grow more than one crop a year.
  • Risks: irrigation can cause salt build-up and falling groundwater where more is pumped than rain replaces.
  • North-west India lost groundwater equal to a 4 cm layer of water each year (2002–2008), likely from pumping for irrigation and other uses.
Mission 5

The Green Revolution

  • From the 1960s, high-yielding varieties (HYVs) of rice and wheat were grown with fertiliser, pesticides and irrigation.
  • About 40% of Asia's cereal area grew these varieties by 1980, and about 80% by 2000.
  • Asia's cereal production almost doubled from 1970 to 1995, "from 313 to 650 million tons per year". Population grew 60%, but calories available per person rose by nearly 30%.
Quick check
?A farmer is given high-yielding rice seeds but has no irrigation and cannot afford fertiliser. What is the most likely result?
Mission 5

Green Revolution: who gained, and at what cost?

  • In Punjab, India, where land was shared fairly equally, the gains cut poverty sharply. In neighbouring Bihar, "where the land system was more feudal, poverty reduction was more limited".
  • Overuse of fertilisers and pesticides polluted waterways and killed beneficial insects and wildlife.
  • The lesson: technology raises supply, but who owns land and who can buy inputs decides who benefits.
Mission 5

Biotechnology: GM crops

  • Genetically modified (GM) crops have genes changed in a laboratory, for example to resist insects or add vitamins.
  • A 2016 US National Academies review found "no substantiated evidence of a difference in risks to human health" from GM crops then on sale, and, in US data for soybean, cotton and maize, "no evidence that GE crops had changed the rate of increase in yields".
  • Golden Rice adds beta-carotene to fight vitamin A deficiency, which affects more than 15% of Filipino children under six. The Philippines approved it in July 2021; a court revoked the permit in April 2024.
Mission 5

Hydroponics, aeroponics and vertical farms

  • Hydroponics: plants grow with roots in water containing nutrients, without soil.
  • Aeroponics: roots hang in air and are sprayed with a nutrient mist.
  • Vertical farms stack these indoors under lights. Singapore imports over 90% of its food and has only about 1% of its land for growing food.
  • But lettuce from an indoor plant factory needs 247 kWh of purchased energy per kg (dry weight), against 70 kWh in a Dutch greenhouse.
Quick check
?Using the figures earlier in this mission, how many times as much purchased energy does the plant factory use per kg of lettuce as the greenhouse?
Mission 5

Brazil: food statistics over time

  • Brazil is upper-middle income: an EDC (emerging and developing country) in OCR's terms.
  • Food supply per person (kcal per day): 2,220 (1961) → 2,728 (1990) → 3,303 (2010) → 3,423 (2023).
  • Share of people undernourished (three-year averages): 10.5% (2001) → below 2.5% from 2012 → 3.4% (2021) → below 2.5% (2023).
  • FAO reports any value under 2.5% as "<2.5%": that is the line for leaving the Hunger Map.
Mission 5

Brazil, past attempt: Zero Hunger (from 2003)

  • The Zero Hunger (Fome Zero) project, launched in October 2001, became the government's main strategy from 2003.
  • Bolsa Família gave cash to poor families who kept their children in school and attended health check-ups.
  • By 2013 it reached nearly 14 million households, about a quarter of Brazilians, at around 0.5–0.6% of GDP.
  • Effectiveness: undernourishment fell from 10.5% (2001) to below 2.5% by 2012, and Brazil left the Hunger Map in 2014. The timing fits; other factors, such as economic growth, also mattered.
Mission 5

Brazil, present attempt: Brazil Without Hunger

  • Brazil returned to the Hunger Map between 2019 and 2022, after an economic crisis and COVID-19.
  • The Brasil Sem Fome (Brazil Without Hunger) plan followed; undernourishment averaged below 2.5% in 2022–2024 and Brazil left the map again in 2025.
  • School meals: by law, at least 30% of federal school-meal funds had to buy food from family farms (2009); a 2025 law raised this to at least 45%.
  • Effectiveness: quick success, but it depends on keeping the policy going.
Quick check
?Which judgement on Brazil's attempts is best supported by the statistics?
Mission 5

More food is not the whole answer

  • Brazil's food supply was already above 3,300 kcal per person in 2010, and stayed there.
  • Yet undernourishment rose from below 2.5% to 3.4% in 2021, during an economic crisis and COVID-19.
  • Strategies that raise supply (irrigation, HYVs, GM, hydroponics) need strategies that give people access: incomes, cash transfers, school meals.
Mission check

?A country doubles its wheat harvest with new seeds and irrigation. Then a recession puts millions out of work and hunger rises. What would most directly cut hunger now, and keep it down?

Brazil had plenty of food; hunger tracked access. Cash for poor families and school meals bought from family farms cut undernourishment below 2.5%; recession and COVID-19 pushed it back up; sustained policy brought it down again.

Mission 6

What makes food supply sustainable?

  • Sustainable food supply meets people's needs now without damaging the land, water and wildlife future food depends on.
  • It must work for the environment, the economy (affordable, farmers earn a living) and people (everyone can get enough).
Mission 6

Belo Horizonte, Brazil

  • A city of about 2.5 million people in south-east Brazil.
  • Brazil is an EDC in OCR's IMF-based terms: the IMF lists it as an emerging market economy. It is the same country as your Brazil case study.
  • In 1993, a city law made access to food a right and set up a food secretariat.
  • Its food system has about 20 programmes and uses about 2% of the city's yearly budget.
Mission 6

Three pillars

  • 1. Food for those in need: popular restaurants (low-cost meals), school meals and a food bank.
  • 2. Fair markets: ABasteCer low-price produce markets and "straight from the farm" and organic fairs.
  • 3. Growing food: community gardens, productive backyards and school gardens using agroecology; the city also buys from family farms.
Quick check
?Which Belo Horizonte programme increases the supply of food grown locally?
Mission 6

Why is it sustainable?

  • Environmental: local, organic and agroecological food; direct trading cuts transport.
  • Economic: farmers sell straight to buyers, so producers earn more and consumers pay less.
  • Social: food is treated as a right, not charity; nutrition education runs alongside.
Mission 6

How successful has it been?

  • The World Future Council, which promotes the scheme, says its programmes "have eliminated hunger and malnutrition from the city". Elsewhere the same page says "near elimination". That is the promoter's claim, not an independent study.
  • Researchers call it "a world pioneer" in urban food policy, and describe both strengths and challenges.
  • An earlier study warned it was "premature" to call it a model.
  • Its ideas were copied nationally, in Brazil's Zero Hunger strategy.
Mission 6

Bottom-up: urban gardens and permaculture

  • Bottom-up: run by local people and groups, not imposed from above.
  • Environmental: food grown where it is eaten; compost recycles waste; more plant variety.
  • Economic: cheap to start; families save money on food.
  • Social: community skills and healthier diets.
  • Limits: small scale, limited city land, and reliance on volunteers or funding.
Quick check
?What is the main limit of urban gardens as a way to feed a city?
Mission 6

Top-down and bottom-up together

  • Belo Horizonte's scheme is government-driven, but its community gardens depend on local people.
  • So it combines top-down support (law, budget, markets) with bottom-up action (gardens, backyards).
  • Exam move: "assess the sustainability" means judging environmental, economic and social sides, then deciding overall.
Mission check

?A city in an emerging country wants more people to eat well while supporting nearby small farmers. Which plan follows Belo Horizonte's approach most closely?

Belo Horizonte made food a right in 1993, then backed it with low-cost restaurants, direct-from-farm markets and urban gardens, for about 2% of the city budget. Its promoters claim it ended hunger in the city; researchers praise it but warn against treating it as a ready-made model.

Mission 7

Three tests of sustainability

  • Environmental: does it protect soil, water, wildlife and climate?
  • Economic: does it pay farmers fairly and keep food affordable?
  • Social: does it improve people's lives and health, fairly?
  • A strategy can pass one test and fail another.
Mission 7

Ethical consumerism: fair trade

  • Ethical consumerism: shoppers choosing goods for their effect on people and the planet.
  • Fairtrade guarantees farmers a minimum price; for coffee, $1.80 per pound from August 2023.
  • Farmers also get a Fairtrade Premium: €201.6 million in 2024, spent on projects their groups choose.
  • In 2024 there were about 1.87 million Fairtrade farmers and workers.
Mission 7

Fair trade: how sustainable?

  • Economic: a price floor protects farmers when world prices fall.
  • But: only about 27% of Fairtrade-certified coffee was actually sold on Fairtrade terms in 2024; the rest did not earn the Fairtrade price.
  • Social: Premium funds community projects. But one study in Ethiopia and Uganda found hired workers in Fairtrade areas sometimes "significantly worse paid".
  • Scale: Fair Trade coffee was 1.8% of world coffee exports in 2009; its impact is "hotly debated".
Quick check
?A co-operative grows 100 tonnes of Fairtrade-certified coffee but buyers take only 27 tonnes on Fairtrade terms. What does this show?
Mission 7

Food waste

  • 1.05 billion tonnes of food were wasted in 2022: almost one-fifth of the food available to consumers.
  • That is about 132 kg per person per year.
  • Households wasted 60%, food services 28%, shops 12%.
  • Food loss and waste cause 8–10% of world greenhouse gas emissions.
Mission 7

Cutting waste: how sustainable?

  • Environmental: less land, water and fertiliser used for food nobody eats; lower emissions.
  • Economic: households and businesses save money.
  • Social: surplus can go to food banks instead of bins.
  • Evidence it works: UNEP reports food waste cut by 18% in the UK.
Quick check
?Where would a campaign to cut food waste have the biggest effect?
Mission 7

Organic versus intensive

  • Intensive: high inputs (synthetic fertiliser, pesticides, machinery) for high yields per hectare.
  • Organic: no synthetic fertiliser or pesticides. Biodiversity: about 30% more species on average.
  • Yield: organic averages about 19% less food per hectare. Mixing crops or rotating them cut the gap to about 8–9%.
  • Per kg of food, organic needs more land, causes more nutrient pollution (eutrophication), uses less energy, with similar greenhouse gas emissions.
  • Economic: organic food usually costs more to buy; intensive food is cheaper but has hidden environmental costs.
  • Food choice matters too: beef and lamb (ruminant meat) have about 100 times the environmental impact of plant foods.
Quick check
?If a country switched all its farms to organic without changing what people eat, what would most likely happen?
Mission 7

GM crops

  • Genetically modified (GM) crops have genes changed in a lab to add a trait, such as insect resistance.
  • Health: no substantiated evidence of a difference in risk to human health compared with conventional crops.
  • Environment: insect-resistant crops cut insecticide poisonings, but some weeds evolved resistance to the main herbicide used.
  • Yield: in the USA, no evidence GM crops changed the rate at which yields rose.
  • Social: Golden Rice, bred to fight vitamin A deficiency, was approved in the Philippines in 2021; a court revoked its permit in 2024.
Mission 7

Hydroponics

  • Hydroponics: crops grown in a water-based nutrient solution, without soil.
  • Environmental: little land needed; useful where farmland is scarce. But indoor plant factories need about 247 kWh of electricity per kg of lettuce (dry weight), against 70 kWh in a Dutch greenhouse.
  • Economic: high running costs; Singapore's farms report "inflationary pressures on energy and manpower costs" and "some exits".
  • Social: fresh food grown close to cities.
Quick check
?Why is indoor hydroponic farming hard to make economically sustainable?
Mission 7

Comparing the strategies

  • Fair trade: helps farmer incomes, but only where shoppers buy enough.
  • Cutting waste: wins on all three tests, but relies on changing habits.
  • Organic: better for wildlife on the farm, but needs more land per kg of food.
  • GM: no proven health risk, but mixed results and public resistance.
  • Hydroponics: saves land, but costly in energy and money.
  • Exam move: use the three tests, give evidence for each, then judge.
Mission check

?Researchers compared many farming systems and foods. Which change would cut the environmental impact of a country's food the most?

Going all-organic is not clearly better: organic farms hold about 30% more species, but yields are about 19% lower, so the same food needs more land. A review of 742 farming systems found that dietary shifts and more efficient use of inputs would bring bigger benefits than switching to organic.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Food, water and energy underpin health and the economy; use is very unequal; demand can outstrip supply as population and wealth grow.
  • A shock to one resource spreads through the others.

Mission 2

  • Goods (food, medicine, building materials, fuel, water) are taken; services (protection, climate regulation, tourism, health) are provided.
  • Commercial exploitation modifies ecosystems: mechanised farming and fishing (food), deforestation and mining (energy, minerals), reservoirs and transfers (water).

Mission 3

  • Food security: all people, at all times, can get enough safe, nutritious food. Supply per person is highest in Europe and the Americas and lowest in Africa.
  • Physical factors (climate, pests, water stress) and human factors (poverty, conflict, technology, prices) affect supply and access.
  • The Global Hunger Index combines undernourishment, child deaths, stunting and wasting.

Mission 4

  • Food per person has risen since 1961, but hunger remains.
  • Malthus: population outruns food until checks act. Boserup: population pressure drives new farming methods. Global data fit Boserup better, but scale matters.

Mission 5

  • More food: irrigation, the Green Revolution's HYVs, GM crops, hydroponics and aeroponics. Each raises supply, with costs (groundwater, pollution, energy, inequality).
  • Brazil: supply 2,220 → 3,423 kcal (1961–2023); undernourishment 10.5% (2001) → <2.5%; Zero Hunger (past) and Brazil Without Hunger with school meals (present).
  • Ending hunger needs access as well as supply, kept up through crises.

Mission 6

  • Belo Horizonte, Brazil (1993): food as a right; popular restaurants, a food bank, direct-from-farm and organic markets, and community gardens. Bottom-up urban gardens and permaculture are sustainable at small scale but cannot feed a city alone.

Mission 7

  • Judge each food strategy on environmental, economic and social sustainability.
  • Fair trade and cutting waste help but depend on shoppers; organic helps wildlife but needs more land; GM has no proven health risk but mixed results; hydroponics saves land but uses much energy.
