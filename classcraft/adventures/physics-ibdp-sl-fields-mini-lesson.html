Mission 1

Mission 1: Rub a balloon and it becomes charged. Where did that charge come from?

Make a guess: Rub a balloon and it becomes charged. Where did that charge come from?

Mission 1

Two kinds of charge

  • Charge is positive or negative. Like charges repel; unlike charges attract.
  • Protons: +e. Electrons: −e. Neutrons: no charge.
  • e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, the elementary charge.
  • Neutral object: equal numbers of protons and electrons. Charged object: it has gained or lost electrons.
  • Charge comes only in whole-number multiples of e: Q = ±ne. Charge is quantised.
Quick check
?A small plastic bead has a net charge of −4.8 × 10⁻¹⁷ C. Which statement correctly describes the bead? Use e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C.
Mission 1

Evidence: Millikan's oil drops

  • Tiny charged oil drops between horizontal plates: p.d. V, gap d, uniform electric field E = V/d. (E: force per coulomb of charge.)
  • Adjust V until a drop hovers: electric force up = weight down.
  • qV/d = mg, so q = mgd/V. (The drop's weight comes from its falling speed with the field off.)
  • Example: weight 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁴ N, d = 8.0 mm, V = 400 V → q = 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁴ × 8.0 × 10⁻³ ÷ 400 = 6.4 × 10⁻¹⁹ C = 4e.
  • Over many drops: every charge a whole-number multiple of 1.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ C. Never 1.5e, never 2.3e.
  • Evidence that charge is quantised.
Quick check
?In an experiment of Millikan's type, the charges on four oil drops were measured in arbitrary units as 4.2, 7.0, 9.8 and 15.4 units. Assuming that charge is quantised, what is the largest value of the basic unit of charge that is consistent with all four results?
Quick check
?Predict first. A polythene rod is rubbed with a dry woollen cloth and gains a charge of −8.0 nC. Which statement about the cloth after the rubbing is correct?
Mission 1

Charging by friction: charge is conserved

  • Charge is conserved: it is never created or destroyed, only moved.
  • Rubbing moves electrons from one surface to the other. Protons stay put, locked in nuclei.
  • Polythene rubbed with wool: electrons move wool → polythene. Polythene −, wool +.
  • The two charges are equal and opposite: the total stays zero.
  • So the balloon's charge came from the material it was rubbed on.
Mission 1

Conductors, insulators, contact

  • Conductors (metals): electrons free to move. Added charge spreads over the surface.
  • Insulators: electrons bound to atoms or molecules. Added charge stays where it was put.
  • Charging by contact: touch a charged conductor to a neutral one. Charge flows until both share it; the total is unchanged.
  • Two identical conducting spheres share the total equally.
Mission 1

Induction: why neutral things are attracted

  • Charged rod near a neutral conductor: its free electrons move. Near side: opposite charge. Far side: same charge. This is induced charge separation.
  • The near side is closer, so the attraction wins: the neutral object is pulled towards the rod.
  • Neutral insulator (a wall): electrons cannot flow, but each molecule polarises slightly. The near surface gains a slight opposite charge: weaker attraction.
  • So a charged object attracts any neutral object. Only repulsion proves both are charged.
Mission 1

Charging by induction

  • 1. Hold a negative rod near a neutral metal sphere on an insulating stand.
  • 2. Connect the sphere to Earth: repelled electrons flow to Earth.
  • 3. Remove the Earth wire, rod still in place.
  • 4. Remove the rod. The sphere is left positive, charge spread over its surface.
  • The rod never touches; it loses none of its charge.
  • Earth is so large it can give or take electrons and stay effectively neutral.
  • Connecting to Earth: earthing (grounding).
Quick check
?A negatively charged rod is held near a neutral metal sphere on an insulating stand, and the sphere is connected to earth by a wire. This time the rod is taken away first, and only then is the wire removed. What is the final charge on the sphere?
Mission check

?A rod on an insulating handle has a charge of −6.0 nC. It touches a neutral metal sphere on an insulating stand and is then pulled away. Nothing else touches either object. Afterwards, the rod's charge is −2.0 nC. What is the charge on the sphere?

Charge is never created: it is moved, as electrons, from one object to another, and the total is conserved. The balloon's charge came from the material it was rubbed on.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Halve the distance between two charges. How many times bigger does the force between them get?

Make a guess: Halve the distance between two charges. How many times bigger does the force between them get?

Mission 2

Coulomb's law

  • The force between two point charges is proportional to the product of the charges and to 1/r²: an inverse-square law.
  • It acts along the line joining them: like charges repel, unlike attract. Each exerts an equal and opposite force on the other.
  • F = kq1q2/r², with k = 1/(4πε0) = 8.99 × 10⁹ N m² C⁻².
  • ε0 = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² C² N⁻¹ m⁻², the permittivity of free space.
Mission 2

Spheres act as points

  • A uniformly charged sphere acts, outside itself, like a point charge at its centre.
  • So r is measured centre to centre.
  • Two spheres 2.0 cm across with a 3.0 cm gap: r = 1.0 + 3.0 + 1.0 = 5.0 cm.
Mission 2

Worked example

  • +3.0 nC and +5.0 nC, centres 4.0 cm apart, in air.
  • F = 8.99 × 10⁹ × (3.0 × 10⁻⁹)(5.0 × 10⁻⁹) ÷ 0.040² = 8.4 × 10⁻⁵ N.
  • Both positive: repulsion.
  • Halve r: force × 2² = 4 times: 3.4 × 10⁻⁴ N.
  • Double one charge: force × 2.
Quick check
?Two small charged spheres whose centres are 0.60 m apart exert electric forces of magnitude 1.0 N on each other. Their charges do not change. At what centre-to-centre separation would the forces have a magnitude of 9.0 N?
Mission 2

Charges inside a material

  • Charges immersed in an insulating material: it polarises and weakens the force.
  • Replace ε0 by the material's permittivity ε: F = q1q2/(4πεr²).
  • Larger ε, smaller force. Force = (ε0/ε) × its value in a vacuum.
  • Air: ε ≈ ε0. Water: ε ≈ 80ε0: forces between charges in water are about 1/80 as strong.
Quick check
?Two small spheres, treated as point charges, carry charges of +4.0 nC and +6.0 nC. They are 2.0 cm apart, immersed in an oil of permittivity ε = 4.2 × 10⁻¹¹ C² N⁻¹ m⁻². Take ε0 = 8.85 × 10⁻¹² C² N⁻¹ m⁻². What is the magnitude of the electric force between them?
Mission 2

More than two charges

  • Find each force separately, then add them as vectors.
  • A (+2.0 nC) at x = 0; B (+1.0 nC) at x = 0.10 m; C (−3.0 nC) at x = 0.30 m. Force on B:
  • From A: repulsion, pushes B in +x: 1.8 × 10⁻⁶ N.
  • From C (0.20 m away): attraction, pulls B towards C, also +x: 6.7 × 10⁻⁷ N.
  • Resultant: 2.5 × 10⁻⁶ N in +x.
  • Not in a line? Resolve into components, add, then use Pythagoras.
Quick check
?Three point charges are fixed on the x-axis: P (+4.0 nC) at x = 0, Q (+1.0 nC) at x = 0.10 m and R (+2.0 nC) at x = 0.30 m. Take k = 1/(4πε0) = 8.99 × 10⁹ N m² C⁻². What is the magnitude of the resultant electric force on Q?
Mission check

?Two small charged spheres exert electric forces of magnitude F on each other. The charge on each sphere is halved, and the distance between them is reduced to one third of its value. What is the magnitude of the force now?

Force ∝ 1/r². Halving the distance makes the force 4 times bigger; a third of the distance makes it 9 times bigger.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Field lines are just drawn on paper. How can their spacing tell you how strong a force is?

Make a guess: Field lines are just drawn on paper. How can their spacing tell you how strong a force is?

Mission 3

The electric field

  • Electric field: a region where a charge feels a force. Charges create it; it is there even when nothing is placed in it.
  • E = F/q: the force per unit positive charge.
  • A vector. Unit: N C⁻¹.
  • Direction: the push on a positive charge: away from +, towards −.
  • A negative charge is pushed opposite to E.
Quick check
?At point P, a charge of +2.0 µC experiences an electric force of 5.0 × 10⁻³ N directed east. This charge is removed and a charge of −6.0 µC is placed at P; the charges that produce the field do not move. Taking east as positive, what is the electric force on the −6.0 µC charge?
Quick check
?Predict first. A student sketches the electric field lines around two equal positive point charges a short distance apart. Which description of a correct sketch is right?
Mission 3

Field lines

  • A field line shows the direction of the force on a positive charge.
  • Lines start on + charges and end on − charges (or run to or from far away).
  • Lines never cross: the field has one direction at each point.
  • Closer lines = stronger field. Lines are drawn so their density matches E.
  • Patterns: single + (radially out) · single − (radially in) · + and − pair · two + (a neutral point between) · parallel plates.
  • A field line is not the path a charge follows.
Mission 3

The field of a point charge

  • From F = kQq/r² and E = F/q: E = kQ/r².
  • Inverse square: double r, E falls to ¼.
  • Field lines agree: the same lines pass through every sphere round the charge, area 4πr². Line density ∝ 1/r².
  • A charged sphere: outside, its field is that of a point charge at its centre.
  • +4.0 nC, at 0.20 m: E = 8.99 × 10⁹ × 4.0 × 10⁻⁹ ÷ 0.20² = 9.0 × 10² N C⁻¹, away from the charge.
Mission 3

Adding fields

  • Several charges: add their fields as vectors.
  • +4.0 nC and +1.0 nC, 0.40 m apart. Where between them is E = 0?
  • There the two fields point opposite ways. Equal when 4.0/x² = 1.0/(0.40 − x)², x measured from the 4.0 nC charge.
  • Square roots: 2/x = 1/(0.40 − x), so 2(0.40 − x) = x and x = 0.27 m.
  • The neutral point is nearer the smaller charge.
Quick check
?The diagram shows two point charges, Q1 = +4.0 nC and Q2 = −2.0 nC, fixed on a line, and a point P on the same line. Take k = 1/(4πε0) = 8.99 × 10⁹ N m² C⁻². What is the magnitude of the resultant electric field strength at P?
Mission 3

The uniform field between plates

  • Two parallel plates, p.d. V, separation d.
  • Between them, away from the edges: a uniform field, the same strength and direction everywhere. Lines parallel, evenly spaced, + to −.
  • E = V/d. Unit V m⁻¹ = N C⁻¹.
  • Near the edges the field weakens and the lines bulge outwards: edge effects.
  • 200 V across plates 4.0 cm apart: E = 200 ÷ 0.040 = 5.0 × 10³ V m⁻¹.
  • Force on a proton: eE = 8.0 × 10⁻¹⁶ N, the same anywhere between the plates.
Mission check

?The field lines of a long, straight charged wire spread out radially, in planes at right angles to the wire: they spread in two dimensions, not three. Point P is 2.0 cm from the wire and point Q is 6.0 cm from it, both far from the wire's ends. How does the electric field strength at P compare with that at Q?

Lines are drawn so that their density matches E. Closer lines, stronger field. A point charge's lines spread over spheres (area ∝ r²), so E ∝ 1/r²; a long wire's spread over cylinders (area ∝ r), so E ∝ 1/r.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Charge a metal ball. Where does the extra charge end up, and what is the field inside the metal?

Make a guess: Charge a metal ball. Where does the extra charge end up, and what is the field inside the metal?

Mission 4

Why the field inside is zero

  • A metal has free electrons that move at the slightest force.
  • Electrostatic equilibrium: no charge is moving.
  • Any field inside the metal would push the free electrons, so they would move.
  • So in equilibrium: E = 0 everywhere inside the metal.
  • Electrons rearrange to make this happen in a tiny fraction of a second.
Quick check
?Predict first. A hollow metal sphere on an insulating stand is given a positive charge. Which description of the electric field lines is correct?
Mission 4

Where the charge goes

  • E = 0 inside → no net charge inside. All excess charge sits on the outer surface.
  • Just outside, the field is perpendicular to the surface. A sideways part would push surface charges along.
  • Sphere: charge spreads evenly; field lines are radial outside, as if from a point charge at the centre.
  • Hollow sphere: the same outside. No field lines in the metal or in the empty cavity.
Mission check

?A solid lump of metal with a sharp point at one end carries excess charge and has settled. Which statement is correct?

Free electrons move until the field inside the metal is zero, whatever its shape; that puts all the excess charge on the outer surface, with the field ⟂ to it.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Switch on a current in a wire just above a compass. Which way does the needle turn, and why?

Make a guess: Switch on a current in a wire just above a compass. Which way does the needle turn, and why?

Mission 5

Where magnetic fields come from

  • A magnetic field is produced by moving charges (an electric current) and by permanent magnets.
  • It exerts forces on moving charges, currents and magnetic materials (iron, steel).
  • A charge at rest feels no magnetic force.
Mission 5

Field lines

  • Direction of a field line: the way the north end of a compass needle points.
  • Closer lines = stronger field. Lines never cross.
  • Field lines are closed loops: outside a magnet they run N → S; inside, S → N.
  • Bar magnet: lines leave the N pole, curve around, enter the S pole, and continue back through the magnet.
Quick check
?Point C is at the centre of a bar magnet, inside it. Point D is just outside the magnet, beside its centre. How does the magnitude of the magnetic field at C compare with that at D?
Mission 5

Field of a long straight wire

  • Field lines are concentric circles centred on the wire, in planes at right angles to it.
  • Right-hand grip rule: thumb along the current; curled fingers give the field direction.
  • Lines spread out with distance: the field gets weaker away from the wire.
  • End-on views: ⊙ current out of the page → field anticlockwise; ⊗ into the page → clockwise.
Quick check
?A long, straight, vertical wire carries a steady current upwards. Point P is 5.0 cm due east of the wire, level with it. What is the direction of the magnetic field of the wire at P?
Mission 5

Flat coil and solenoid

  • Flat coil: the circles around each part of the wire combine. At the centre the field is along the axis.
  • Solenoid (long coil): inside, straight, parallel, evenly spaced lines (uniform field). Outside, the pattern of a bar magnet.
  • Direction: curl the fingers of your right hand the way the current flows around the turns; the thumb points along the field inside, towards the coil's N end.
Quick check
?You look at the left end of a long solenoid. Seen from there, the current in its turns flows clockwise. Which end of the solenoid is its north end?
Mission check

?A flat horizontal circular coil carries a steady current that flows clockwise when viewed from above. What is the direction of the magnetic field at the centre of the coil?

Same rule, new shape. Around a straight wire the field circles the current, so a compass needle just below a wire laid along it swings towards lying across the wire, tangent to a circle centred on it: fully across if the wire's field is much stronger than Earth's. Reverse the current and it swings the other way.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Like charges repel, unlike attract; e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C; charge is quantised in multiples of e.
  • Charge is conserved: friction, contact and induction move electrons; they never create charge.
  • Earthing lets electrons flow to or from Earth: charging by induction leaves the opposite charge to the rod.

Mission 2

  • F = kq1q2/r², k = 1/(4πε0); in a material use ε in place of ε0.

Mission 3

  • E = F/q: force per unit positive charge; field lines show its direction, and their density its strength.
  • Point charge: E = kQ/r²; parallel plates: uniform, E = V/d, with edge effects.

Mission 4

  • In a conductor in equilibrium: E = 0 inside the metal, excess charge on the outer surface, field ⟂ to the surface; a charged sphere acts outside like a point charge.

Mission 5

  • Magnetic fields come from moving charges and magnets; field lines are closed loops, closer lines mean a stronger field.
  • Straight wire: circles, direction by the right-hand grip rule. Solenoid: uniform inside, bar-magnet pattern outside.
