Mission 1: The Prophet died in 632. Who should lead next, and why does the answer still divide Muslims?

Make a guess: The Prophet died in 632. Who should lead next, and why does the answer still divide Muslims?

Mission 1

The question in 632

  • 632 CE: the Prophet Muhammad dies in Madinah. Muslims believe he is the last prophet: revelation has ended (33:40).
  • But the community still needs leadership: to lead prayer, keep unity, judge, defend.
  • Two questions are tangled together:
  • Who should lead?
  • What kind of authority does the leader have: political only, or religious guidance too?
  • Muslims answered differently. The answers became Sunni and Shi'a Islam.
  • "Muhammad is no more than a messenger: many Were the messenger that passed away before him." (Qur'an 3:144, Yusuf Ali) Abu Bakr recited this verse on the day of the Prophet's death.
Mission 1

The Sunni account: Abu Bakr

  • While the Prophet's family prepared his burial, the Ansar (Madinan helpers) met at the Saqifah (meeting hall) of Banu Sa'ida to choose a leader.
  • Abu Bakr, Umar and Abu Ubaydah joined them. Umar pledged loyalty (bay'ah) to Abu Bakr; the others followed; the wider community pledged next day in the mosque.
  • Why Abu Bakr? The Prophet's companion in the cave during the Hijrah, "the second of two" (9:40); an early believer; father of Aishah; and asked by the Prophet to lead the prayers in his last illness (Bukhari; paraphrase).
  • Title: Khalifat Rasul Allah, successor (caliph) of the Messenger of God: a political leader, not a prophet.
  • Sunni view: the Prophet named no successor; the community chose by consensus.
Mission 1

The Shi'a account: Ali

  • Ali ibn Abi Talib: the Prophet's cousin, raised in his house, among the first to believe, married to his daughter Fatimah; father of Hasan and Husain.
  • Ghadir Khumm, March 632, on the way back from the Farewell Pilgrimage: the Prophet took Ali's hand and said, "For whomever I am his mawla (master), Ali is his mawla" (paraphrase; reported in Shi'a and Sunni collections, e.g. Tirmidhi).
  • Shi'a: this was designation (nass): God's choice of Ali as leader, announced by the Prophet.
  • Hadiths of the family: the Two Weighty Things, the Qur'an and the Prophet's household (Muslim; paraphrase); "You are to me as Aaron was to Moses, except there is no prophet after me" (Bukhari, Muslim; paraphrase).
  • So for Shi'a Muslims, Ali was the rightful leader and first Imam; the Saqifah set aside God's choice.
  • Ali gave allegiance to Abu Bakr later (after Fatimah's death, in Sunni reports) and kept the community's unity.

The Prophet's family

> "And Allah only wishes to remove all abomination from you, ye members of the Family, and to make you pure and spotless." (Qur'an 33:33, Yusuf Ali)

> "No reward do I ask of you for this except the love of those near of kin." (Qur'an 42:23, Yusuf Ali)

  • Ahl al-bayt: "people of the house", the Prophet's family.
  • Shi'a: 33:33 refers to the "people of the cloak": the Prophet, Ali, Fatimah, Hasan and Husain (a hadith in Muslim tells how he covered them with his cloak). It points to their purity and their right to guide.
  • Sunnis: the verse sits among verses addressed to the Prophet's wives, who are included; Sunnis also love and honour the family.

Mission 1

The four Rightly Guided Caliphs

  • Rashidun ("rightly guided"): the Sunni name for the first four caliphs.
  • Abu Bakr (632–634): the Ridda wars bring rebel tribes back; the first gathering of the Qur'an begins.
  • Umar (634–644): named by Abu Bakr; great expansion; the diwan (register of pensions); the Hijri calendar; title Amir al-Mu'minin (Commander of the Believers). Before dying he named a council (shura) of six.
  • Uthman (644–656): chosen by the shura; one standard copy of the Qur'an sent to the cities; accused of favouring his Umayyad relatives; killed by rebels in Madinah.
  • Ali (656–661): chosen in Madinah after Uthman's murder; his rule is torn by civil war.
  • Sunni view: a golden age, the model of just rule by shura and the Sunnah.
Mission 1

The spread of Islam under the caliphs

  • In about 20 years Muslim armies took Syria, Iraq, Egypt and much of Persia (Yarmuk 636; Jerusalem 637–8; Egypt 640–2; Nahavand 642).
  • Political: the Byzantine and Persian empires were exhausted by their long war (602–628).
  • Religious: unity under Islam replaced tribal feuds; a sense of mission.
  • Migration: Arab tribes settled in new garrison towns (amsar): Basra, Kufa, Fustat.
  • Economic and social: new lands paid tax; Jews and Christians (dhimmi, protected people) kept their faith and paid the jizya tax.
  • Spread of rule ≠ spread of faith: most conquered people became Muslim slowly, over centuries.

Mission 1

Ali's caliphate and the first fitna

  • Fitna: trial, civil strife. 656–661 is the first fitna.
  • Battle of the Camel (656): Ali against Aishah, Talha and Zubayr, who demanded justice for Uthman.
  • Siffin (657): Ali against Mu'awiya, Uthman's Umayyad kinsman and governor of Syria. Ali accepts arbitration.
  • A group rejects the arbitration ("judgement belongs to God alone") and leaves: the Kharijites.
  • 661: a Kharijite kills Ali at prayer in Kufa. His son Hasan makes peace with Mu'awiya, who becomes caliph.
  • Sunnis: Ali is the fourth Rightly Guided Caliph. Shi'a: Ali is the first Imam; his short rule was the only time the rightful Imam also ruled (with Hasan's months).
  • Political and religious disagreement are now joined: who rules, and who guides.
Mission 1

Scholars on the succession

  • Karen Armstrong (b. 1944), Islam: A Short History (2000): the early caliphs tried to build the just community the Qur'an called for; the disputes came from the strain of turning that ideal into the rule of an empire (paraphrase).
  • Seyyed Hossein Nasr (b. 1933), The Heart of Islam (2002): Sunnism and Shi'ism are two legitimate dimensions of one Islam. The dispute was about the nature of authority: for Sunnis the caliph guards the shari'ah; for Shi'a the Imam also interprets its inner meaning (paraphrase).
Mission 1Evaluate

Designation or consensus?

  • Claim: The Shi'a account of the succession is better supported than the Sunni account.
  • For: Ghadir Khumm is reported in Sunni sources too; the Qur'an honours prophets' families.
  • For: the hasty Saqifah meeting, without the Prophet's family present, looks less like a full consensus.
  • Against: the Prophet appointed Abu Bakr to lead prayer; most Companions accepted him; Ali himself pledged allegiance.
  • Against: "mawla" has several meanings; nothing in the Qur'an names Ali.
  • A further view: Nasr: both traditions preserve real parts of Islam; asking "which is right" may miss what each protects.
  • Criteria: Which sources count (Sunni or Shi'a hadith)? Does early acceptance prove legitimacy? Is leadership political, spiritual, or both?

Mission 2

From caliphate to kingship

  • Khalifah (caliph): "successor" or "deputy" of the Messenger, leading the ummah.
  • A well-known hadith: the caliphate would last thirty years, then kingship would follow (Tirmidhi, Abu Dawud; paraphrase).
  • Thirty years after 632 brings us to about 661: the end of Ali's rule and Hasan's peace with Mu'awiya.
  • Ibn Khaldun (1332–1406), Muqaddimah (1377): the caliphate changed gradually into mulk (royal power), keeping religious forms for a time.
  • From 661 the title "caliph" continued, but it was passed down in dynasties.
Mission 2

The Umayyads (661–750)

  • Mu'awiya (r. 661–680): capital Damascus; the first caliph to name his own son, Yazid, as heir: the start of hereditary (dynastic) rule.
  • 680: Husain refuses allegiance to Yazid and is killed at Karbala (see Mission 4).
  • Abd al-Malik (r. 685–705): builds the Dome of the Rock in Jerusalem (c. 691–2); makes Arabic the language of government; issues coins with Qur'anic words, no images.
  • Expansion: Spain (711), Sindh (711–712), Central Asia; halted in France (Tours/Poitiers, 732).
  • Criticised for worldly kingship and for treating non-Arab converts (mawali) as second class.
  • Umar II (r. 717–720) is remembered by Sunnis for piety and justice, an exception.
  • Overthrown in 750; one Umayyad prince escapes to Spain.

Mission 2

The Abbasids (750–1258)

  • A revolt from Khurasan (Persia) under black banners, calling for a ruler "acceptable from the family of Muhammad". Many Shi'a hoped for a descendant of Ali.
  • But power went to descendants of the Prophet's uncle al-Abbas: the Abbasids. Shi'a hopes were disappointed.
  • Baghdad founded 762 as a new capital. Harun al-Rashid (r. 786–809); al-Ma'mun (r. 813–833) supports translation and science (the House of Wisdom).
  • 833: al-Ma'mun's mihna (test) tries to force scholars to say the Qur'an is created. It fails (abandoned c. 848).
  • Result: religious authority rests with the ulama (scholars) and the law schools, not the caliph.
  • From 945 the caliph is a figurehead under the Buyids (Shi'a), then the Seljuk sultans (1055).
  • 1258: the Mongols sack Baghdad and kill the caliph.
Mission 2

What was a caliph for?

  • Al-Mawardi (974–1058), al-Ahkam al-Sultaniyya (The Ordinances of Government): the classic Sunni theory. He calls the caliph "imam".
  • Duties: protect the faith; enforce the shari'ah and judge justly; defend the borders; collect zakah and taxes; appoint honest officials.
  • Qualifications: just character, knowledge of the law, sound body and mind, courage; descent from Quraysh (the Prophet's tribe).
  • How chosen: by those with authority to "loose and bind" (leading figures), or named by the previous caliph. Both follow the Rashidun (Saqifah; Umar).
  • Limits: the caliph is not a prophet; he cannot change revelation or define doctrine.
  • Sunni authority is thus split: the caliph rules; the ulama interpret.
  • Nasr (b. 1933): for Sunnis the caliph was the symbol of the ummah's unity and guardian of the shari'ah, not its interpreter (paraphrase).

Mission 2

Later caliphates

  • Three caliphs at once in the 10th century: the Abbasid in Baghdad, the Fatimid in Cairo, the Umayyad in Córdoba.
  • Fatimids (909–1171): Isma'ili Shi'a caliphs claiming descent from Fatimah; found Cairo (969) and al-Azhar (970–972).
  • Umayyads of Córdoba: Abd al-Rahman III declares himself caliph (929).
  • Abbasid "shadow" caliphs in Cairo under the Mamluk sultans (1261–1517).
  • Ottomans: after taking Egypt (1517), sultans gradually use the title; Abdulhamid II (r. 1876–1909) stresses it to unite Muslims worldwide.
  • So the caliphate in practice was sometimes real power, sometimes a symbol, sometimes a rival claim.
  • Your spec: "some later examples of caliphs and of how the caliphate was expressed in practice".
Mission 2

1924 and after

  • 3 March 1924: the Turkish Grand National Assembly abolishes the Ottoman caliphate.
  • In India the Khilafat Movement (1919–1924) had campaigned to save it.
  • Ali Abd al-Raziq (1888–1966), Islam and the Foundations of Governance (1925): the Qur'an and Sunnah set no fixed form of state; the caliphate is a human institution. Al-Azhar condemned the book.
  • Today most Muslims live in nation states; the Organisation of Islamic Cooperation (1969) links governments.
  • A few movements campaign for a new caliphate. When the group known as ISIS declared one in 2014, many leading Muslim scholars wrote publicly to reject it.
  • For most Muslims the ummah's unity now rests on shared faith, worship and law, not a single ruler.

Mission 2Evaluate

Was the caliphate after 661 a true caliphate?

  • Claim: After the Rightly Guided Caliphs, the caliphate was kingship in religious dress.
  • For: hereditary succession (Yazid); the "thirty years" hadith; Ibn Khaldun's account of mulk.
  • For: Shi'a Muslims: rulers who killed Husain could not be God's representatives.
  • Against: later caliphs led prayers, defended borders, upheld the shari'ah courts and spread learning.
  • Against: Sunni jurists accepted imperfect rulers to avoid fitna (civil strife): order under the law mattered more than an ideal ruler.
  • Armstrong (b. 1944): the Abbasids ruled as absolute monarchs in the Persian style, while the shari'ah and its scholars kept alive a more egalitarian Qur'anic ideal (paraphrase).
  • Criteria: Is a caliphate defined by how the ruler is chosen, or by what he does? Can political and religious authority be separated?

Mission 3

The party of Ali

  • Shi'a comes from Shi'at Ali: "the party (followers) of Ali".
  • A minority of Muslims worldwide; the majority in Iran, Iraq, Azerbaijan and Bahrain; large communities in Lebanon, Pakistan, India and Britain.
  • Shared with Sunnis: one God, the Qur'an, the Prophet as the final messenger, salah, fasting, Hajj, the Day of Judgement.
  • The distinctive belief: Imamah (the Imamate): after the Prophet, God appointed Imams from his family to guide the community (see Mission 1).
  • Origins: Ali's designation at Ghadir Khumm (as Shi'a understand it); the martyrdom of Husain at Karbala in 680 (see Mission 4).
Mission 3

Who is an Imam?

  • Designated (nass): each Imam is named by the Prophet or the Imam before him, by God's command.
  • Ma'sum (infallible): protected by God from sin and error ('ismah).
  • Knowledge ('ilm): the Imam knows the outer and inner meaning of the Qur'an and the Prophet's teaching.
  • Hujjah (proof of God): the earth is never without one (al-Kulayni, al-Kafi; paraphrase).
  • So the Imams' words and actions are an authoritative source beside the Qur'an and the Prophet's Sunnah. Their reported sayings fill Shi'a hadith collections such as al-Kafi (10th century).
  • The Imam is not a prophet: revelation ended with Muhammad. He explains and guards it.
Mission 3Read the source

Verses Shi'a scholars cite

> "He said: 'I will make thee an Imam to the Nations.' He pleaded: 'And also (Imams) from my offspring!' He answered: 'But My Promise is not within the reach of evil-doers.'" (Qur'an 2:124, Yusuf Ali)

> "Obey Allah, and obey the Messenger, and those charged with authority among you." (Qur'an 4:59, Yusuf Ali)

  • 2:124: God makes Abraham an Imam after testing him. Shi'a reading: Imamate is God's appointment, and no wrongdoer can hold it; so the Imam must be sinless.
  • 4:59: Shi'a reading: "those charged with authority" are the infallible Imams, since God would not command obedience to someone who might lead into error. Sunni reading: rulers and scholars, obeyed within the limits of God's law.
  • With 33:33 (which speaks of God purifying the Prophet's Family; Shi'a apply it to the Prophet, Ali, Fatimah, Hasan and Husain), these verses ground belief in the Imams.

Mission 3

The Twelve Imams

  • 1 Ali (d. 661, Najaf) · 2 Hasan (d. 670, Madinah) · 3 Husain (d. 680, Karbala)
  • 4 Ali Zayn al-Abidin · 5 Muhammad al-Baqir · 6 Ja'far al-Sadiq (d. 765): great teacher of law; Shi'a law is called Ja'fari
  • 7 Musa al-Kazim · 8 Ali al-Rida (d. 818; shrine in Mashhad, Iran) · 9 Muhammad al-Jawad
  • 10 Ali al-Hadi · 11 Hasan al-Askari (d. 874, Samarra) · 12 Muhammad al-Mahdi: believed by Twelvers to be in occultation
  • Shi'a accounts hold that most Imams were killed or poisoned by rulers: a line of martyrs for justice.
  • Their tombs are major places of ziyarah (visiting pilgrimage).

Mission 3Diversity

Twelvers, Seveners and others

  • Shi'a groups split over which son carried the Imamate.
  • Twelvers (Ithna 'Ashariyyah): the great majority of Shi'a; twelve Imams; the last in occultation.
  • Isma'ilis ("Seveners"): after Ja'far al-Sadiq (d. 765), they followed his son Isma'il and his line, not Musa al-Kazim. Strictly, "Seveners" were those who held the line ended with the seventh Imam. The Fatimid caliphs were Isma'ili (see Mission 2).
  • Isma'ilis today: Nizaris, led by a living, present Imam, the Aga Khan; Bohras, whose Imam is hidden and who are led by a Da'i.
  • Zaydis ("Fivers"): follow Zayd, son of the fourth Imam; any learned descendant of Hasan or Husain who rises against injustice may be Imam; mainly in Yemen.
Mission 3

Authority while the Imam is hidden

  • In the greater occultation, Twelvers hold that qualified jurists (mujtahids) represent the Imam's guidance in law.
  • Each believer follows (taqlid) a living marja' al-taqlid ("source of imitation"), e.g. Grand Ayatollah Ali al-Sistani (b. 1930) in Najaf; the marja' receives part of the khums.
  • Wilayat al-faqih (guardianship of the jurist): Ayatollah Khomeini (1902–1989), lectures of 1970: a just jurist should also hold political rule. This became the basis of the Islamic Republic of Iran (1979).
  • Many Shi'a jurists, including al-Sistani, give jurists a narrower role in politics.
  • So authority flows: God → Prophet → Imams → (in occultation) jurists on the Imam's behalf, never replacing him.

Mission 4

680: a refusal

  • Husain (Hussein): son of Ali and Fatimah, grandson of the Prophet; for Shi'a Muslims the third Imam.
  • 680: Mu'awiya dies; his son Yazid demands an oath of allegiance (bay'ah). Husain refuses: Yazid's rule is unjust and hereditary (see Mission 2).
  • Letters from Kufa in Iraq invite Husain to lead them. He sets out from Makkah with his family and a small band of companions.
  • Husain's stated aim (Shi'a sources, paraphrase): not pride or power, but to reform the community of his grandfather, to enjoin good and forbid evil.
  • Yazid's governor in Kufa crushes support; the Kufans do not come.
Mission 4

Ten days at Karbala

  • 2 Muharram 61 AH: Husain's party is forced to camp at Karbala, by the Euphrates.
  • 7 Muharram: the army cuts them off from the river. Days of thirst follow.
  • 10 Muharram, Ashura (10 October 680): Husain's companions and family fall one by one.
  • Abbas, his half-brother, is killed trying to bring water; his infant son Ali al-Asghar is struck by an arrow.
  • Husain is killed; his head is taken to Kufa and Damascus.
  • The women and children, led by his sister Zaynab, are taken captive. Zaynab speaks out boldly in Yazid's court. His son Ali Zayn al-Abidin, too ill to fight, survives as the fourth Imam.
Mission 4Read the source

Not dead, but living

> "And say not of those who are slain in the way of Allah: 'They are dead.' Nay, they are living, though ye perceive (it) not." (Qur'an 2:154, Yusuf Ali)

> "Who say, when afflicted with calamity: 'To Allah We belong, and to Him is our return'" (Qur'an 2:156, Yusuf Ali)

  • Shahid: martyr, "witness": one who dies for God's cause bears witness to the truth with his life.
  • Shi'a Muslims read these verses of Husain: his death was victory, not defeat; he lives with God.
  • 2:156 is said at every loss; mourners recite it for Husain.
  • A hadith: "Husain is from me, and I am from Husain" (Tirmidhi; paraphrase).

Mission 4

What Karbala means

  • Martyrdom: for Shi'a Muslims, Husain chose death over approving injustice; they believe his sacrifice kept true Islam from being corrupted by power.
  • Justice against tyranny: a well-known Shi'a saying: "Every day is Ashura, every land is Karbala": each age has its Yazid and its Husain.
  • Persecution: the Imams after Husain lived under hostile rulers; Shi'a history is remembered as a history of the oppressed.
  • Grief and love: sayings of the Imams promise reward for tears shed for Husain (Shi'a hadith; paraphrase). Mourning shows love for the ahl al-bayt.
  • Intercession: many Shi'a hope for Husain's intercession on the Day of Judgement.
  • Guilt and loyalty: Kufa failed Husain. Mourning asks: would I have stood with him?
Mission 4

Muharram rituals

  • The first ten days of Muharram build up to Ashura. Black clothes; no weddings or celebrations.
  • Majlis (pl. majalis): gatherings in a husayniyyah or imambargah where a speaker recounts the events, day by day (rawda).
  • Marthiya and nawha: elegies and laments, sung by the gathering.
  • Matam: rhythmic beating of the chest in grief.
  • Processions: banners (alam), a replica of Husain's tomb (South Asia), a riderless horse for Husain's horse; in Iran, ta'ziyeh passion plays.
  • Sabil: free water and drinks given to passers-by, remembering the thirst at Karbala.
Mission 4Diversity

Self-mortification: a debate

  • Some mourners go beyond matam: zanjir (striking the back with chains, sometimes with blades) or tatbir (cutting the scalp with a blade) to share Husain's suffering.
  • Supporters: a sign of love and grief; a pledge of readiness to suffer for justice; an old custom.
  • Critics within Shi'a Islam: Islam forbids harming the body; it gives outsiders a false picture of the faith. Many leading jurists forbid tatbir (e.g. Iran's Ayatollah Khamenei, 1994); others permit it only if it causes no serious harm.
  • Alternatives: many communities, including in Britain, hold blood donation drives at Ashura: giving blood to save life.
  • Most Shi'a Muslims mourn with majlis and matam; self-cutting is a minority practice.
Mission 4

Pilgrimage to Karbala

  • Husain's shrine, and Abbas's beside it, stand in Karbala, Iraq: among the holiest places for Shi'a Muslims.
  • Pilgrims visit (ziyarah) on Ashura and through the year; they recite Ziyarat Ashura, a greeting and lament for Husain.
  • Arba'een: 40 days after Ashura (20 Safar), millions walk to Karbala, many from Najaf, about 80 km; local people give free food, water and rest on the way.
  • Under Saddam Hussein these gatherings were restricted; after 2003 they grew hugely.
  • Pilgrimage brings Husain's story into the body: thirst, walking, sacrifice.
  • Your spec: "pilgrimages".

Mission 4

Scholars: Halm and Ahmed

  • Heinz Halm (b. 1942), Shi'a Islam: From Religion to Revolution (1997) and Shi'ism (2nd ed., 2004).
  • Halm traces how the meaning of Karbala changed (paraphrase):
  • Penitence: the Tawwabun (Penitents), Kufans who had failed Husain, died seeking atonement (685).
  • Public mourning: under the Buyid rulers of Baghdad (from 963) Ashura became a public ritual.
  • Drama and narrative: under the Safavids (from 1501) recitations and passion plays grew.
  • Revolution: in the 20th century, Karbala was reread as a call to active resistance; it shaped the Iranian revolution (1978–79).
  • N. Ahmed (paraphrase): Karbala as a turning point of Islamic history, a contest between principle and power whose memory kept alive protest against unjust rule.
  • So: the event is fixed, but its significance has been re-interpreted in each age.
Mission 4

Ashura and Shi'a identity

  • Identity: Ashura is where Shi'a Muslims most visibly say who they are: lovers of the ahl al-bayt, people who refuse injustice.
  • Community: local associations (anjuman) organise processions; families sponsor majalis and food as a vow (nazr); children learn the nawhas.
  • In Britain: processions in London and other cities; open majalis and blood drives invite neighbours in.
  • Sunni Muslims also mark 10 Muharram: the Prophet fasted that day, remembering God's saving of Moses (Bukhari; paraphrase). Many Sunnis, especially in South Asia, also honour Husain.
Mission 4Evaluate

Does a focus on suffering misrepresent Shi'a identity?

  • Claim: Ashura's focus on suffering gives a false picture of Shi'a identity.
  • For: outsiders see blood and weeping, not belief; self-harm images are used to mock or fear Shi'a Muslims.
  • For: the meaning of Karbala has shifted over time (penitence, public mourning, protest), so suffering is not the only possible centre.
  • Halm traces these shifts.
  • Against: suffering is not the goal; it is witness to justice and love for the Prophet's family.
  • Against: grief binds the community and keeps the memory of persecution alive, which is part of its identity.
  • Criteria: Who decides what represents a tradition: insiders or observers? Is suffering the message, or the way the message is told?

Mission 5

What Sufism is

  • Sufism (Arabic tasawwuf): the inward, mystical dimension of Islam. Its goal is to know and love God directly, not only to obey.
  • A Sufi seeks to purify the heart, remember God constantly and draw near to Him.
  • Most Sufis have been Sunni Muslims who follow a law school (madhhab) and pray the five daily prayers; some are Shi'a.
  • The name probably comes from suf, the coarse wool early ascetics wore. Other suggested roots: safa' (purity) and the ahl al-suffa, poor Companions who lived at the Prophet's mosque.
  • "Sufism" is a European coinage of about 1800; Sufis usually speak of the path (tariqa) or of ihsan.
Mission 5

Roots in the Qur'an and the Prophet's life

  • Sufis see their path as rooted in Islam's own sources, not borrowed.
  • Qur'an: God is close ("We are nearer to him than his jugular vein", Qur'an 50:16, Pickthall); God is to be remembered much (33:41).
  • The Prophet's practice: his retreats in the cave of Hira, his long night prayers, and the Night Journey and Ascension (isra' and mi'raj) are read as models of inner devotion.
  • Hadith: the Gabriel hadith defines ihsan: worshipping God as if you see Him (paraphrase; Sahih Muslim, Book of Faith).
  • Early Western scholars looked for outside sources: Christian monks, Neoplatonist philosophy, Indian ascetics. Most specialists now see some contact but stress Qur'anic roots (Louis Massignon, 1883–1962, argued this from the vocabulary of early Sufis).
Mission 5Read the source

A people God loves

> "soon will Allah produce a people whom He will love as they will love Him" (Qur'an 5:54, Yusuf Ali)

> "Verily in the remembrance of Allah do hearts find rest!" (Qur'an 13:28, Pickthall)

  • Sufis read 5:54 as showing that love between God and the believer runs both ways.
  • 13:28 grounds the practice of remembrance (dhikr) as the cure for a restless heart.

Q1 What does 5:54 say about who loves first?

Show answer

The verse names God's love before the people's love; Sufis took God's love as the start of the path.

Q2 Why might a Sufi use 13:28 against a purely outward religion?

Show answer

Rest of heart comes through remembering God, not only through outward acts.

Mission 5

Why a more personal faith developed

  • Wealth and empire: under the Umayyads (661–750) the Muslim state grew rich. Some pious Muslims felt luxury was choking faith and turned to renunciation (zuhd).
  • Civil wars (fitna) among Muslims left many wary of power and politics.
  • Law and theology were becoming technical sciences. Some looked for the inner life that rules and debates could not supply.
  • Early figures:
  • Hasan al-Basri (642–728), Basra: fear of God, sorrow for sin, distrust of the world.
  • Rabi'a al-'Adawiyya (d. 801), Basra: worship from love of God alone, not from fear of Hell or hope of Paradise (her prayers survive only in later sources).
  • The movement shifted from fear to love and from renunciation to knowledge of God (ma'rifa).

Mission 5

Baghdad, sobriety and synthesis

  • Al-Junayd of Baghdad (d. 910): taught a sober Sufism, in which the mystic returns from experience of God to ordinary life under the law.
  • Al-Hallaj (c. 858–922): his ecstatic saying "I am the Real" (Ana al-Haqq) and political suspicion led to his execution in Baghdad in 922.
  • Manuals showed Sufism inside Sunni orthodoxy: al-Sarraj (d. 988), Kitab al-Luma'; al-Qushayri (d. 1072), Risala (1045).
  • Al-Ghazali (1058–1111), The Revival of the Religious Sciences (Ihya' 'Ulum al-Din): a leading Sunni jurist and theologian who found certainty on the Sufi path and argued that law, theology and Sufism belong together.
  • After al-Ghazali, sober Sufism was widely accepted as part of Sunni learning.
Mission 5

The orders and the spread

  • From the 12th century, orders (tariqa, plural turuq) formed around a founder and a chain of teachers (silsila):
  • Qadiriyya: Abd al-Qadir al-Jilani (1077–1166), Baghdad.
  • Chishtiyya: Mu'in al-Din Chishti (d. 1236), Ajmer, India.
  • Mevleviyya: followers of Jalal al-Din Rumi (1207–1273), Konya, Anatolia.
  • Naqshbandiyya: Baha' al-Din Naqshband (1318–1389), Bukhara, Central Asia.
  • Shadhiliyya: Abu'l-Hasan al-Shadhili (d. 1258), North Africa and Egypt.
  • Sufis travelled with traders and armies to South Asia, Central Asia, Anatolia, West Africa and Southeast Asia. Many historians credit them with much conversion there; others stress rulers and trade. Usually all three worked together.
Mission 5

How Sufism penetrated society

  • Lodges (Arabic zawiya, Persian khanqah, Turkish tekke) gave food, shelter, teaching and prayer to travellers and the poor.
  • Shrines (South Asian dargah) of saints became centres of pilgrimage (ziyara) and of the yearly festival ('urs) for a saint's death-day.
  • Rulers sought Sufi blessing: Mughal emperors honoured Chishti shrines; Ottoman life was linked to the Mevlevi and other orders.
  • Local culture: Sufis taught in local languages and poetry (Persian, Turkish, Urdu, Punjabi, Swahili, Malay), so Islam took local forms.
  • For centuries, belonging to an order was a normal part of Sunni religious life in much of the Muslim world, for scholars and ordinary people alike.

Mission 5Diversity

Sunni responses

  • Integration: most Sunni scholars, after al-Qushayri and al-Ghazali, accepted sober Sufism; many jurists were themselves Sufis.
  • Ibn Taymiyya (1263–1328): attacked the idea of the "oneness of being" linked with Ibn 'Arabi, and asking saints at shrines for help. He respected early Sufis and the ideal of purifying the heart.
  • Muhammad ibn 'Abd al-Wahhab (1703–1792) and later Salafi reformers: saw shrine veneration and appeals to saints as shirk (associating others with God) and many practices as bid'a (blameworthy innovation).
  • South Asia: Deobandis (from 1867) kept Sufi initiation but criticised shrine customs; Barelwis defend saint-veneration and celebrate the Prophet's birthday.
  • Modernist states: Turkey closed the orders and lodges in 1925 as obstacles to a modern secular nation.
Mission 5Diversity

Shi'a responses

  • Shared ground: most Sufi chains trace back to the Prophet through 'Ali; Shi'a and Sufis both speak of walaya (closeness to God, spiritual authority) and of an inner meaning of the Qur'an.
  • Safavid Iran: the dynasty began as the Safaviyya Sufi order; after 1501 it made Twelver Shi'ism the state faith. Later Safavid scholars, notably Muhammad Baqir Majlisi (d. 1699), opposed the orders.
  • Why the tension: for Twelver Shi'a the Imam is the true spiritual guide. A Sufi shaykh's authority can seem to compete with the Imams and the scholars (mujtahids).
  • Today: Shi'a orders exist (e.g. the Ni'matullahi); many Shi'a scholars prefer 'irfan (mystical knowledge, studied in seminaries) to the orders.
Mission 5

Scholars on Sufism's place

  • William C. Chittick (b. 1943), Sufism: A Short Introduction (2000): Sufism is best seen as the interiorising of Islam, the dimension of ihsan, not a sect alongside Sunni and Shi'a (paraphrase).
  • Ron Geaves, The Sufis of Britain (2000): in South Asian and British Muslim life, Sufi devotion is part of traditional Sunni Islam; its sharpest critics are reformist Muslims, not outsiders (paraphrase).
  • Common ground: Sufis themselves claim to be Muslims living Islam fully, not members of a separate religion.
Mission 5Evaluate

Is Sufism a separate tradition?

  • Claim: Sufism is a separate tradition within Islam, like Sunni and Shi'a.
  • For: distinct institutions (orders, lodges, shrines), leaders (shaykhs, saints), rituals (dhikr gatherings, sama') and texts.
  • For: Salafi critics and secular states have treated it as a distinct, even rival, form of religion.
  • Against: it has no separate creed, law or line of succession; Sufis are Sunni or Shi'a and follow a law school.
  • Against: "Sufism" is partly an outside label for what Muslims call ihsan or tasawwuf.
  • Scholars: Chittick sees it as the interiorising of Islam, not a sect; Geaves finds it woven into ordinary Sunni identity.
  • Judgement criteria: What makes a "tradition" (creed? law? institutions?)? Whose view counts: Sufis, critics or historians? Does the answer differ by time and place?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Sunnis: the Prophet named no successor; the community chose Abu Bakr by consensus; the four Rightly Guided Caliphs are a model of just rule.
  • Shi'a: the Prophet designated Ali at Ghadir Khumm; leadership belongs to the purified family, the ahl al-bayt, through the Imams.
  • Under the caliphs Islam's rule spread from Arabia to Egypt, Syria, Iraq and Persia.
  • The first fitna (656–661) joined political and religious disagreement and hardened the division.

Mission 2

  • From 661 the caliphate became dynastic: Umayyads (Damascus, 661–750), Abbasids (Baghdad, 750–1258), later rival and Ottoman caliphs until 1924.
  • Sunni theory (al-Mawardi): the caliph protects the faith and enforces the shari'ah; the ulama interpret it. The Shi'a Imam is designated and infallible.
  • Whether the dynastic caliphate was a true caliphate or kingship remains debated.

Mission 3

  • Shi'a Muslims believe God appointed twelve (or, for Isma'ilis, a different line of) Imams from the Prophet's family: designated, infallible, sources of guidance.
  • Twelvers believe the twelfth Imam is hidden (in occultation); qualified jurists act for him meanwhile.

Mission 4

  • 10 Muharram 61 AH (680 CE): Husain, refusing to approve Yazid's unjust rule, was killed at Karbala with his family and companions.
  • Muharram rites (majlis, rawda, nawha, matam, processions, ziyarah, Arba'een) mourn Husain and renew loyalty to justice and to the ahl al-bayt.
  • Self-mortification is a minority practice, debated among Shi'a jurists.
  • Halm shows Karbala's meaning has been re-interpreted over time.

Mission 5

  • Sufism (tasawwuf) is the inward path of Islam; it grew from Qur'anic devotion and early renunciation into the orders that spread across the Muslim world from the 12th century.
  • Most Sunni scholars integrated sober Sufism after al-Ghazali; Ibn Taymiyya, Wahhabi and Salafi reformers attacked shrine practices and monism; Shi'a scholars share ideas of walaya but often distrust the orders.
  • Chittick: the interiorising of Islam, not a sect; Geaves: part of traditional Sunni identity in South Asia and Britain.
