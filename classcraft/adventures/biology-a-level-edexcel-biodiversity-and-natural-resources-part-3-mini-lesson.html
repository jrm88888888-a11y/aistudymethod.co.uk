Mission 1

Mission 1: How can just two words tell you where any living thing fits among millions?

Make a guess: How can just two words tell you where any living thing fits among millions?

Mission 1

Too many to know one by one

  • About 1.2 million species are catalogued; an estimated 8.7 million species of eukaryotes exist.
  • Classification: sorting organisms into groups.
  • Why classify?
    • identify an organism and name it without confusion;
    • predict features of a group's members;
    • show evolutionary relationships;
Mission 1

The species: the unit everything rests on

  • Every classification is built on the species.
  • Two organisms are the same species if they can interbreed to produce fertile offspring.
  • Classification uses similarities and differences in phenotype and in genotype, built around the species concept.
Mission 1

Groups within groups

  • Each group is a taxon (plural taxa).
  • Domain → kingdom → phylum → class → order → family → genus → species.
  • Smaller groups sit inside larger ones, with no overlap.
  • Lower taxa: fewer species, more features shared.
  • Humans: Eukarya · Animalia · Chordata · Mammalia · Primates · Hominidae · Homo · Homo sapiens.
Quick check
?The red fox, Vulpes vulpes, and the grey wolf, Canis lupus, are both placed in the family Canidae. Which statement must be true?
Mission 1

Two-part names

  • Binomial system (Linnaeus): every species has a two-part name.
  • 1st word: genus, with a capital letter.
  • 2nd word: species, all lower case. It means nothing on its own.
  • Italics in print; underline when handwritten.
  • Same genus = similar traits, usually close relatives: Panthera leo (lion), Panthera tigris (tiger).
  • After first use, shorten the genus: P. leo.
  • Say: "Homo sapiens" — not: "Homo Sapiens", and not "sapiens" alone.
Mission 1

Why not just use common names?

  • A binomial is universal: the same in every country and language.
  • Common names mislead. "Robin" means Erithacus rubecula in Britain but Turdus migratorius in North America.
  • One species can have many local names; a "starfish" is not a fish.
  • The genus shows at once which species are close relatives.
Quick check
?Which statement about the scientific name of the tiger, Panthera tigris, is correct?
Mission 1

Classify by ancestry

  • Phylogenetic classification: groups reflect evolutionary origins and relationships.
  • Ideal: every member of a group descends from one common ancestor.
  • Advantage: features of a group can be predicted. A newly found member probably shares them.
  • Evidence: anatomy, plus DNA and protein sequences.
  • Classification is tentative: it changes as new evidence arrives.
Quick check
?What is the main advantage of a classification in which every group corresponds to evolutionary relationships?
Mission check

?The raven is Corvus corax, the rook is Corvus frugilegus and the magpie is Pica pica. What can be concluded from these names alone?

Two words: the genus places it among its closest relatives; the species word picks it out from them.

Mission 2

Clues to who is related

  • Relatives share features inherited from a common ancestor.
  • Evidence:
    • anatomy: homologous structures, in living species and fossils;
    • DNA and mRNA base sequences;
    • amino acid sequences of proteins.
  • After two lineages split, mutations build up separately in each.
  • So: fewer differences → more recent common ancestor → closer relatives.
  • Say: "share a more recent common ancestor" — not: "one evolved from the other".

Mission 2

Reading a cladogram

  • A cladogram shows the order in which lineages branched.
  • Root: the base; the common ancestor of every group shown.
  • Node: a branch point; a hypothetical common ancestor, where one lineage split in two.
  • Terminal branch: ends at one group.
  • Clade: an ancestor and all of its descendants.
  • How related? Find the most recent node they share. Not which tips sit side by side.
  • No living group at a tip is the ancestor of another.
Quick check

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why are some microbes that look just like bacteria placed further from them than you are from a mushroom?

Make a guess: Why are some microbes that look just like bacteria placed further from them than you are from a mushroom?

Mission 3

One chemistry, many cell plans

  • Every living thing contains the same four classes of biochemicals: carbohydrates, lipids, proteins, nucleic acids.
  • All store genetic information in DNA and use ATP.
  • The big differences are in cell structure and in how organisms get energy and carbon.
  • These differences sort life into its largest groups.
Mission 3

Three domains

  • 1977: Carl Woese and George Fox compared ribosomal RNA (rRNA) base sequences from many organisms.
  • Prokaryotes fell into two groups, as different from each other as either is from eukaryotes.
  • So life has three domains, a level above kingdoms: Bacteria, Archaea, Eukarya.
  • Edexcel names: Bacteria, Archaea, Eukaryota.
  • You and a mushroom: both Eukarya, one domain.
  • Archaea look like bacteria: no nucleus. But their key molecules differ, and some are closer to ours.
  • Say: "archaea are a separate domain" — not: "archaea are unusual bacteria".
Quick check
?Which evidence was used in 1977 to propose that all organisms should be classified into three domains?
Mission 3

Kingdoms or domains? New data on trial

  • Five kingdoms: based on observable features. All prokaryotes: one kingdom.
  • Three domains: based on molecular evidence. Prokaryotes: two domains. Protoctista, Fungi, Plantae and Animalia all sit inside Eukarya.
  • Domains add a level above kingdoms. The eukaryote kingdoms stay; kingdom Prokaryotae is split into two domains.
  • How it was accepted:
    • published in a peer-reviewed journal (1977);
    • doubted at first; other scientists tested it with other genes and molecules;
    • three domains formally proposed in 1990, then widely accepted.
  • This is critical evaluation of new data by the scientific community.
Mission check

?A single-celled organism is found in ordinary garden soil. It has no nucleus. Its key molecules, including its ribosomal RNA, match those of known archaea, not bacteria. How should it be classified?

Looks put it with the bacteria; its molecules put it in a domain of its own.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Linen cloth and strong rope are both made from plant stems. What makes those stems so tough?

Make a guess: Linen cloth and strong rope are both made from plant stems. What makes those stems so tough?

Mission 4

From cellulose to microfibrils

  • Cellulose: long, straight chains of β-glucose.
  • Parallel chains are held together by many hydrogen bonds.
  • Bundles of chains form microfibrils: very high tensile strength (they resist being pulled apart).
  • In a cell wall, microfibrils lie in layers. Each layer runs at a different angle.
  • Like plywood: strong in several directions, not just one.
Mission 4

Thick walls, stiffened with lignin

  • Some cells lay down a thick secondary wall inside the first wall: secondary thickening.
  • More layers of microfibrils, at different angles.
  • Lignin is added: it stiffens the wall, resists squashing and makes it waterproof.
  • The cell contents die. What is left is a strong, empty tube.
  • Xylem vessels: long hollow tubes, end walls gone. Lignin stops them collapsing. They carry water and mineral ions, and support the stem.
  • Sclerenchyma fibres: long, narrow cells with very thick lignified walls and tapering, overlapping ends. Support only.
Quick check
?What gives a cellulose microfibril its high tensile strength?
Mission 4

Fibres we use

  • Flax (linen), hemp and jute: bundles of sclerenchyma fibres from the stem.
  • Uses: rope, sacking, textiles, and reinforcement in composite panels (e.g. car door linings).
  • Wood, mostly xylem: timber; its cellulose fibres make paper.
  • What we exploit: high tensile strength, flexibility, low density, renewable supply.
Mission 4Practical

Core practical 8: tensile strength of plant fibres

  • Extract fibres from stems (e.g. by soaking, then pulling them out). Cut to the same length.
  • Clamp one end. Hang a mass holder on the other.
  • Add masses in small, equal steps until the fibre snaps. Record the breaking mass: the measure of strength in the core practical.
  • Repeat with several fibres. Wear eye protection; put a tray under the masses.
  • Key variable: thickness. Measure diameter (micrometre, or eyepiece graticule).
  • To compare fibres of different thickness: breaking force F = m × g (g = 9.81 N kg⁻¹); tensile strength = F ÷ cross-sectional area (π r²). Units: Pa (N m⁻²) or MPa.
Mission 4

Worked example: tensile strength

Illustrative data: a fibre 0.50 mm in diameter snaps with 4.0 kg hung from it.

  • Radius: 0.25 mm = 2.5 × 10⁻⁴ m.
  • Area: π × (2.5 × 10⁻⁴)² = 1.96 × 10⁻⁷ m².
  • Force: 4.0 × 9.81 = 39.2 N.
  • Tensile strength: 39.2 ÷ 1.96 × 10⁻⁷ = 2.0 × 10⁸ Pa = 200 MPa.
  • Check: radius, not diameter; force in N, not mass in kg; area in m².
Quick check
?A fibre 0.40 mm in diameter snaps when a mass of 2.0 kg hangs from it. Taking g = 9.81 N kg⁻¹, what is its tensile strength?

Mission 4

Plants instead of oil

  • Most plastics are made from crude oil: finite, and most do not biodegrade.
  • Plant alternatives: starch-based plastics (packaging, compostable bags); plant-fibre composites (hemp or flax in panels); fibres for rope and textiles.
  • Arguments for:
  • renewable: a new crop grows each year
  • many are biodegradable or compostable
  • their carbon was recently taken from the air by photosynthesis
  • Arguments against:
  • crops need land, water, fertiliser and energy to grow and process; land may compete with food
  • some break down only in industrial composting
  • some are weaker, or soften when wet
  • Sustainable: meets today's needs without compromising future generations' ability to meet theirs.
Quick check
?A company switches a product from an oil-based plastic to a starch-based plastic. Which statement is correct?
Mission check

?Two kinds of long cell from a hemp stem have the same width. Type X has a thick secondary wall: many layers of cellulose microfibrils at different angles, with lignin. Type Y has a thin wall of cellulose only. Which prediction is best supported?

Straight cellulose chains, built into microfibrils, layered at angles and stiffened with lignin: that is what makes stems tough enough for rope.

Mission 5

Mission 5: How does water reach the top of the tallest tree with no pump and no energy spent by the tree?

Make a guess: How does water reach the top of the tallest tree with no pump and no energy spent by the tree?

Mission 5

Xylem vessels: built for flow

  • Xylem carries water and mineral ions from roots to leaves.
  • Dead cells, no contents: nothing obstructs the flow.
  • End walls lost: cells join into one continuous tube.
  • Lignified walls: rigid, so the vessel does not collapse under tension; also waterproof, and support the plant.
  • Pits: gaps in the lignin. Water moves sideways between vessels and out to leaf cells.
  • Secondary thickening: layers of cellulose microfibrils at different angles, impregnated with lignin, make the walls strong and rigid (see Mission 4).
  • Stomata (leaf pores) open to let carbon dioxide in, and water vapour escapes through them. Evaporation pulls water up the xylem from the roots; cohesion between water molecules keeps the column unbroken. Passive: no pump, no ATP.
  • Say: "lignin stops the vessel collapsing under tension" — not: "lignin is only for support".
Quick check
?Why are the walls of xylem vessels thickened with lignin?
Mission 5

Where the xylem is

  • Stem: vascular bundles in a ring near the outside. Pith in the centre.
  • In each bundle: xylem inside, phloem outside, cambium between.
  • Cortex between the ring and the epidermis.
  • A cap of sclerenchyma fibres sits outside the phloem.
Quick check
?A student is drawing a plan diagram from a micrograph of a transverse section of a dicotyledonous stem. Which description matches what should be drawn?
Mission check

?A xylem vessel in a leaf vein is blocked by an air bubble. Water still reaches the leaf cells beyond the blockage. Which feature of xylem makes this possible?

Every feature of a xylem vessel serves one job: keeping a continuous path for water from root to leaf.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Why does a plant lose water fastest on a warm, dry, windy day, and how can you measure it?

Make a guess: Why does a plant lose water fastest on a warm, dry, windy day, and how can you measure it?

Mission 6Practical

The potometer

  • Practical technique: using instrumentation such as a potometer.
  • Cut the shoot under water, at a slant. Fit it to the potometer under water: no air gaps.
  • Seal joints with petroleum jelly. Dry the leaves. Let the shoot settle for 5 minutes.
  • Let in one air bubble. Time how far it moves along the scale. Reset it from the reservoir. Repeat; take a mean.
  • Change one factor (e.g. fan, lamp distance). Keep the others, and the same shoot, constant.
  • Four outside factors to test: more light opens stomata wider; higher temperature, lower humidity and more air movement each steepen the water-vapour gradient from leaf to air. Each makes the bubble move faster.
  • Say: "a potometer measures water uptake" — not: "water loss". A little water is used in photosynthesis or kept in cells.
Mission 6

Worked example: rate from the bubble

Illustrative data: capillary radius 0.50 mm; bubble moves 45 mm in 5.0 min.

  • Water taken up = volume of a cylinder = πr²l.
  • = π × (0.50 mm)² × 45 mm = 35 mm³.
  • Rate = 35 mm³ ÷ 5.0 min = 7.1 mm³ min⁻¹.
  • To compare shoots, divide by leaf area (trace leaves on squared paper).
Quick check
?In a potometer, the capillary tube has a radius of 0.40 mm. The air bubble moves 30 mm in 10 minutes. What is the rate of water uptake?
Mission 6Practical

Looking inside a stem

  • Core practical 6: identify sclerenchyma fibres, phloem sieve tubes and xylem vessels in stems with a light microscope.
  • Stain: toluidine blue turns lignified walls (xylem, sclerenchyma) blue-green and other walls purple. Phloroglucinol with acid turns lignin red.
  • Thin sections let light through: cut with a sharp blade, keep sections wet.
Mission 6

Drawing a plan diagram

  • A plan diagram shows tissue outlines only: no individual cells.
  • Sharp pencil, clear continuous lines, no shading.
  • Keep the proportions of the tissues true.
  • Label with ruled lines that do not cross.
Quick check
?A celery stalk stood for two hours in water containing a red dye. In a transverse section, red spots form a ring near the outside of the stalk. Under the microscope, each red spot is the inner part of a vascular bundle. Which tissue has carried the dye?
Mission check

?A stem section is stained with toluidine blue. Lignified walls stain blue-green; walls of cellulose only stain purple. In each vascular bundle, two tissues stain blue-green: wide, empty cells on the inner side, and narrow cells with very thick walls at the outer edge. What are the two tissues?

The stain picks out lignin; position and cell shape tell xylem from sclerenchyma.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • A binomial names the genus (capital) and the species (lower case); together they name one species, used worldwide.
  • Taxa are nested with no overlap, from domain (or kingdom) down to species, and the ideal grouping follows common ancestry.

Mission 2

  • Differences in DNA, mRNA and protein sequences build up after lineages split, so fewer differences mean a more recent common ancestor.
  • On a cladogram, relatedness is the most recent shared node; every tree is a hypothesis open to new evidence.

Mission 3

  • Archaea look like bacteria but are a separate domain of life, and are metabolically very diverse.

Mission 4

  • Thick secondary walls, with many layers of cellulose microfibrils at different angles and with lignin, give xylem vessels and sclerenchyma fibres high tensile strength.
  • Tensile strength = breaking force ÷ cross-sectional area. Plant fibres and starch can replace some oil-based materials; each switch has costs as well as benefits.

Mission 5

  • Xylem vessels are dead, hollow, lignified tubes with pits: a continuous path that does not collapse under tension.

Mission 6

  • A potometer measures water uptake: volume = πr²l, then divide by time.
  • Lignin stains identify xylem (and sclerenchyma); plan diagrams show tissue outlines only.
