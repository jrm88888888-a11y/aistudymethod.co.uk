Mission 1

Mission 1: When a flame is blown out, where does it go? And what does that tell Buddhists about nibbana?

Make a guess: When a flame is blown out, where does it go? And what does that tell Buddhists about nibbana?

Mission 1

The Third Truth: nirodha

  • Nirodha means cessation: suffering can end.
  • The first sermon (Samyutta Nikaya 56.11, paraphrase): the end of suffering is the complete fading away and ending of craving: giving it up, letting it go, being free of it.
  • The logic: suffering has a cause (craving). Remove the cause, and suffering ceases.
  • This is the good news of the Four Truths: dukkha is not the last word.
Mission 1

Samsara: the cycle

  • Samsara: the cycle of birth, death and rebirth, with no known beginning.
  • Beings are reborn in one of the six realms, according to their kamma.
  • Craving and ignorance keep the cycle turning.
  • Rebirth is not a soul moving to a new body; it is a stream of causes continuing (anatta).
  • Even a good rebirth, as a god, is still inside samsara and still impermanent.
  • Release from samsara is nibbana.
Mission 1

What "nibbana" means

  • Nibbana (Sanskrit nirvana) means "blowing out" or "extinguishing".
  • What is blown out? The three fires: greed, hatred and ignorance.
  • So nibbana is not a heaven or a place, and not the destruction of a person.
  • It is described as peace, freedom, the unconditioned, the highest happiness.
  • A blown-out fire: does it go north, south, east or west? The Buddha said the question does not fit (Majjhima Nikaya 72, paraphrase). Nibbana is beyond ordinary words.
Mission 1

Nibbana in life, and at death

  • Nibbana in this life: the fires are out, but body and mind continue. The Buddha reached this at 35 under the Bodhi tree.
  • Such a person is an arahant ("worthy one"): free of craving, living with wisdom and compassion.
  • Parinibbana ("final nibbana"): at death, an arahant is not reborn. The Buddha passed into parinibbana at 80.
  • Theravada texts call these nibbana "with remainder" and "without remainder".
  • Importance today: nibbana gives Buddhists hope and a direction; it motivates meditation, keeping the precepts and letting go of craving in daily life.

Mission 1Read the source

The unconditioned

> "Health is the greatest of gifts, contentedness the best riches; trust is the best of relationships, Nirvana the highest happiness." (Dhammapada 204, Müller)

> "He who overcomes this fierce thirst, difficult to be conquered in this world, sufferings fall off from him, like water-drops from a lotus leaf." (Dhammapada 336, Müller)

  • Udana 8.3 (paraphrase): there is something unborn, unbecome, unmade, unconditioned. If there were not, there would be no escape from what is born, made and conditioned.

Q1 How does verse 336 picture suffering leaving someone who overcomes craving?

Q2 Why does Udana 8.3 describe nibbana mostly by what it is not?

Show answer

Mission 1

What is Enlightenment?

  • Enlightenment (bodhi: "awakening") means seeing reality as it really is: the three marks, dependent arising, the Four Truths.
  • With it come freedom from the three poisons, wisdom and compassion.
  • Different meanings, depending on who is enlightened:
  • an arahant: freed by following the Buddha's path;
  • a Buddha: found the path and teaches it to others;
  • in Mahayana, a bodhisattva on the way to full Buddhahood, for the sake of all beings.
Mission 1Diversity

Interpretations

  • Theravada: the goal is nibbana as an arahant, by one's own effort on the Eightfold Path. Nibbana is the unconditioned, wholly different from samsara.
  • Mahayana: the highest goal is full Buddhahood. Bodhisattvas work for the release of all beings.
  • Nagarjuna (c. 150–250 CE) taught that nirvana and samsara are not two separate places: nirvana is seeing samsara truly, as empty (Mulamadhyamakakarika 25, paraphrase).
  • Zen: awakening (satori, kensho) can come suddenly, here and now, through meditation.
  • Pure Land: faith in Amitabha Buddha leads to rebirth in his Pure Land, where Enlightenment is easier to reach.

Mission 1Compare

Buddhist and Christian beliefs about the afterlife

  • Number of lives: Buddhists, many lives in samsara; Christians, one life, then judgement.
  • What decides the future: Buddhists, kamma, with no judge; Christians, God's judgement, with grace and mercy.
  • What continues: Buddhists, a stream of causes, no fixed self (anatta); Christians, the soul, and the resurrection of the body.
  • The goal: Buddhists, nibbana, release from rebirth, not a place; Christians, heaven: eternal life with God. (Catholics also teach purgatory.)
  • Hell: Buddhists, hell realms are real but temporary; Christians, hell is traditionally eternal separation from God.
  • Shared: actions matter; the final goal lies beyond suffering; both offer hope.
Mission 1Evaluate

"Nibbana cannot be described, so it cannot be a useful goal."

  • Reasons for: the texts describe nibbana mostly by negatives (unborn, unmade). The Buddha refused to say what happens to an enlightened one after death. It is hard to aim at something you cannot picture.
  • Reasons against: nibbana is known by experience, like a taste, not by definition. Its effects can be described: freedom from greed, hatred and ignorance; peace; "the highest happiness" (Dhammapada 204). The path is described step by step, and each step brings some benefit now.
  • Another view: Mahayana and Zen stress awakening here and now, so the goal is not far away.
  • Judge it by: Must a goal be described to be worth pursuing? Can partial experience give confidence? Is the path more important than a description of the end?

For Buddhists, nibbana is the fires of craving going out: not a place, not nothingness, but release from suffering and rebirth.

Mission 2

The Fourth Noble Truth

  • The Four Noble Truths work like a doctor's visit:
  • Dukkha: the illness (suffering, unsatisfactoriness).
  • Samudaya: the cause (craving, tanha).
  • Nirodha: the cure is possible (craving can cease: nibbana).
  • Magga: the medicine, a path that leads there.
  • The Buddha taught all four in his first sermon at the Deer Park, Sarnath: the Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta ("Setting the Wheel of the Dhamma in Motion").
  • The Fourth Truth is the practical one: it says what to do.
Mission 2

The Middle Way

  • The Buddha had lived both extremes: a prince's luxury, then years of harsh fasting.
  • Neither brought awakening. He called the path between them the Middle Way.
  • First sermon (paraphrase): avoid devotion to sense pleasures (low and unprofitable) and devotion to self-torment (painful and unprofitable); the Middle Way, the Noble Eightfold Path, leads to calm, insight and nibbana.
  • A well-known image: a lute string too tight snaps; too loose, it makes no sound. Tuned just right, it plays.
  • Say: the Middle Way avoids both indulgence and self-punishment — not: the Middle Way means "halfway between good and bad".
Mission 2

Eight factors, one path

  • The Noble Eightfold Path (each factor starts samma, "right": complete, wholesome):
  • 1 Right view (understanding) · 2 Right intention (thought, resolve)
  • 3 Right speech · 4 Right action · 5 Right livelihood
  • 6 Right effort · 7 Right mindfulness · 8 Right concentration
  • Not eight steps climbed in order: they are practised together.
  • Symbol: the Dhamma wheel (dhammacakka) with eight spokes. Remove one spoke and the wheel is weaker.
Mission 2

What each factor asks

  • View: understanding the Four Noble Truths. Intention: letting go, goodwill, harmlessness.
  • Speech: no lies, divisive talk, harsh words or idle chatter.
  • Action: no killing, stealing or sexual misconduct.
  • Livelihood: work that does not harm (traditionally: no trade in weapons, living beings, meat, intoxicants or poisons).
  • Effort: stop unskilful states arising; let go of those present; grow and keep skilful ones.
  • Mindfulness: clear awareness of body, feelings, mind and mental objects.
  • Concentration: a calm, focused mind developed in meditation.
Mission 2

The Threefold Way

  • The eight factors fall into three groups, the Threefold Way:
  • Panna (wisdom): right view, right intention.
  • Sila (ethics): right speech, right action, right livelihood.
  • Samadhi (meditation): right effort, right mindfulness, right concentration.
  • They support each other: ethical living calms the mind; a calm mind sees clearly; clear seeing guides how to live.
  • Many teachers present them as a training order (sila → samadhi → panna), while the list starts with view, because some understanding is needed to set out at all.

Mission 2Read the source

The first sermon

Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta (Samyutta Nikaya 56.11), in summary (paraphrase):

  • The Buddha speaks to five ascetics who had practised with him.
  • He rejects the two extremes: chasing sense pleasures and tormenting the body.
  • The Fourth Truth: the way leading to the end of suffering is the Noble Eightfold Path.
  • He says he claimed full awakening only once he had seen each truth fully.

Q1 Why did the Buddha teach the Middle Way to these listeners first?

Show answer

They had been with him in harsh asceticism; he showed them it was an extreme.

Q2 What does the sermon name as the content of the Fourth Truth?

Show answer

The Noble Eightfold Path.

Mission 2Diversity

One path, lived differently

  • All Buddhist traditions accept the Four Noble Truths and the Eightfold Path. Emphases differ.
  • Theravada: the path to nibbana as an arahant. Monks and nuns give most time to meditation and study; lay Buddhists often stress sila and generosity (dana).
  • Zen (Mahayana): sitting meditation (zazen) at the heart of practice; wisdom seen directly in the present moment.
  • Tibetan (Mahayana): the path within the bodhisattva's six perfections; study and debate train wisdom.
  • Pure Land (Mahayana): many rely on the power of Amida Buddha rather than their own effort; the ethics of the path are a grateful response.
  • Triratna (UK-founded): lay and ordained members follow the path in daily jobs and families; some have run team-based right-livelihood businesses.
  • Today: right livelihood and right speech raise questions about jobs, online posting and gossip.
Quick check

Mission 2Evaluate

"Wisdom is the most important part of the Threefold Way."

  • Reasons for: right view comes first in the list. Ignorance is the root of suffering, so only wisdom finally removes it. Ethics and meditation serve wisdom.
  • Reasons against: without sila the mind is too restless to meditate or see clearly. Many lay Buddhists build their practice mainly on precepts and generosity. The wheel image: no spoke is "most important".
  • Another view: in Pure Land Buddhism, entrusting oneself to Amida matters more than one's own wisdom or effort.
  • Judge it by: Is the path a sequence or a whole? Whose practice are we describing: monastic or lay? Theravada or Mahayana?
Mission 2Worked answer

Belief, source, development

Question: Explain two Buddhist teachings about the Eightfold Path. Refer to sacred writings or another source of Buddhist belief and teaching.

  • Teaching 1: the path is the Middle Way. Development: it avoids indulgence and self-torment, which the Buddha had tried; so Buddhists aim for a simple, balanced life.
  • Teaching 2: the path leads to the end of suffering. Source: Dhammapada 191 calls it "the eightfold holy way that leads to the quieting of pain". Development: practising sila, samadhi and panna together removes craving, the cause of suffering.

The Fourth Noble Truth is a path, not a single act: ethics, meditation and wisdom, practised together.

Mission 3: If you are made of five changing parts, is anyone really in there?

Make a guess: If you are made of five changing parts, is anyone really in there?

Mission 3

Five heaps

  • The Buddha described a person as five khandhas ("heaps", aggregates). Sanskrit: skandhas.
  • Rupa: form, the body and the senses.
  • Vedana: feeling or sensation: pleasant, unpleasant or neutral.
  • Sanna: perception: recognising and labelling.
  • Sankhara: mental formations: intentions, habits, choices. This is where kamma is made.
  • Vinnana: consciousness: awareness through each sense.
Mission 3

The heaps at work

  • A dog barks behind you.
  • Form: ears and sound waves.
  • Feeling: unpleasant (a jolt).
  • Perception: "That's a dog."
  • Mental formations: fear, the urge to step away, an old habit of disliking dogs.
  • Consciousness: awareness of the hearing.
  • All five arise and pass in moments. Nowhere in the list is a sixth thing, an "owner".
Mission 3Read the source

Khandha Sutta

Khandha Sutta (Samyutta Nikaya 22.48), in summary (paraphrase):

  • The Buddha names five aggregates: form, feeling, perception, formations, consciousness.
  • Each covers every instance: past, future or present; inside or outside; coarse or subtle; far or near.
  • He then names the five aggregates subject to clinging: the same heaps when grasped as "me" or "mine".

Q1 What turns an aggregate into an "aggregate subject to clinging"?

Show answer

Grasping it, treating it as me or mine.

Q2 Why does this matter for dukkha?

Show answer

Clinging to the heaps, not the heaps themselves, brings suffering.

Mission 3

Heaps, marks and no owner

  • Anicca: every khandha is changing all the time.
  • Anatta: none of them, alone or together, is a fixed, lasting self. Like Nagasena's chariot: a name for parts working together.
  • Dukkha: clinging to changing heaps as "me" leads to dissatisfaction.
  • Say: no fixed, unchanging self — not: no person at all.

Mission 3

Sunyata: emptiness

  • Sunyata (Pali sunnata): emptiness, a key Mahayana teaching.
  • Your spec spells it sunnata.
  • Everything is empty of a fixed, independent nature: it exists only through causes and conditions.
  • Mahayana applies this to everything, even the khandhas and the Buddha's teachings themselves.
  • Image: a wave is real, but there is no wave apart from water, wind and tide.
  • Linked with the philosopher Nagarjuna (c. 2nd century CE) and the Perfection of Wisdom sutras.
  • Say: empty of fixed self-nature — not: nothing exists.
Mission 3Read the source

The Heart Sutra

The bodhisattva of compassion (Avalokiteshvara) looks deeply at the five heaps:

> "There are the five Skandhas, and these he considered as by their nature empty (phenomenal)." (Heart Sutra, Müller 1894)

> "form here is emptiness, and emptiness indeed is form. Emptiness is not different from form, form is not different from emptiness." (Heart Sutra, Müller 1894)

  • The same is then said of feeling, perception, formations and consciousness.

Q1 Which teaching from the Theravada list does the Heart Sutra apply emptiness to?

Show answer

The five khandhas.

Q2 Why does "emptiness is form" stop emptiness meaning "nothing"?

Show answer

Emptiness is how real things exist, not a void apart from them.

Mission 3

Buddha-nature

  • Tathagatagarbha: "womb" or "seed" of the Tathagata (a Buddha).
  • Many Mahayana Buddhists teach that every being has Buddha-nature: all can become enlightened.
  • Sutra images: gold hidden in dirt; a precious statue wrapped in rags. The gold is there; the dirt (greed, hatred, ignorance) needs removing.
  • In practice: Zen aims to realise one's Buddha-nature directly; Nichiren Buddhists chant to reveal it; all beings deserve respect.
Quick check
?A student writes: "Sunyata means that Mahayana Buddhists believe nothing really exists." Which correction is most accurate?
Mission 3Diversity

Theravada and Mahayana compared

  • Shared: the five khandhas; anicca; anatta; clinging causes dukkha.
  • Theravada: stresses analysing the khandhas, for example in vipassana (insight) meditation, watching each heap arise and pass.
  • Theravada: no doctrine of Buddha-nature; it teaches that any human who follows the path can reach nibbana, and speaks of the mind as "luminous" but covered by passing defilements.
  • Mahayana: extends no-self to all things (sunyata) and stresses Buddha-nature in all beings.
  • Mahayana schools differ too: some treat Buddha-nature as a potential to grow, others as already present, waiting to be uncovered.
Mission 3Evaluate

"Buddha-nature means Buddhists believe in a self after all."

  • Reasons for: it sounds like an inner essence in every being. Some Mahayana sutras even call it a "true self". Some Japanese Buddhist scholars (the "Critical Buddhism" movement, 1980s) argued it contradicts anatta.
  • Reasons against: most Mahayana teachers say Buddha-nature is itself empty, not a personal soul. The Lankavatara Sutra says it is taught so people who fear "no-self" are not put off the path. It is a potential, not a person.
  • Judge it by: What does "self" mean: a fixed soul, or a capacity? Does the idea help practice? Who decides what counts as authentic teaching?

Mission 4

The arhat

  • Arhat (Pali arahant): "worthy one", a perfected person.
  • Your spec spells it Arahant.
  • Has fully ended greed, hatred and ignorance by following the Buddha's teaching.
  • Has reached nibbana in this life; will not be reborn. At death: parinibbana, final nibbana.
  • The goal of the path in Theravada Buddhism.
  • The first arhats were the Buddha's early disciples. The Buddha sent them out to teach "for the welfare of the many".
Mission 4

Four stages to arhatship

  • Theravada describes four stages, as ten "fetters" (chains) are broken:
  • Stream-enterer: has seen the Dhamma; doubt and belief in a fixed self gone; at most seven more lives.
  • Once-returner: greed and ill-will much weaker; one more human life.
  • Non-returner: sense-desire and ill-will gone; no more human rebirth.
  • Arhat: all fetters broken, including conceit and ignorance.
Mission 4

The bodhisattva

  • Bodhisattva: "enlightenment being". The ideal of Mahayana Buddhism.
  • Moved by compassion, they vow to become a fully enlightened Buddha in order to free all beings.
  • The wish that starts this path is bodhicitta, "the mind of awakening".
  • Often described as delaying final nirvana to help others. Many Mahayana teachers put it differently: full Buddhahood is the best way to help everyone.
  • Theravada also uses the word (bodhisatta) for the Buddha before his awakening, as in the Jataka stories.
Quick check
?Which statement correctly contrasts the Theravada and Mahayana ideals?
Mission 4

Buddhahood

  • A Buddha ("awakened one") discovers the Dhamma when it has been lost, and teaches it.
  • An arhat follows a Buddha's teaching to the same freedom from craving.
  • Theravada: one Buddha in each long age: Gotama in ours; Metteyya (Maitreya) is expected next.
  • Mahayana: countless Buddhas in countless worlds, such as Amida.
  • Mahayana: all beings can become Buddhas, because all have Buddha-nature.
Mission 4

Bodhisattvas as a focus of devotion

  • Avalokiteshvara: compassion; many arms to help. Called Guanyin in China, Chenrezig in Tibet.
  • Manjushri: wisdom. Holds a flaming sword that cuts through ignorance and a book (the Perfection of Wisdom) on a lotus.
  • Tara: compassionate, swift help; especially loved in Tibet.
  • Many Tibetan Buddhists regard the Dalai Lama as an emanation of Avalokiteshvara.
Mission 4

Amida and the Pure Land

  • Pure Land Buddhists believe that Amida (Amitabha, "Infinite Light") is a Buddha who, after ages of practice, created a Pure Land: Sukhavati ("Land of Bliss").
  • His 18th vow, they believe: anyone who sincerely trusts him and calls his name will be born there.
  • Pure Land Buddhists recite the nembutsu: "Namu Amida Butsu" ("I take refuge in Amida Buddha").
  • Japan: Honen (1133–1212) founded Jodo Shu; his disciple Shinran (1173–1263), Jodo Shinshu, stressed total reliance on Amida's power (other-power) rather than one's own (self-power).
  • The Pure Land is not the final goal: it is the ideal place to reach enlightenment.

Mission 4Diversity

Different destinies, different readings

  • Theravada: the arhat ideal; Buddhahood is rare and Gotama is the Buddha of this age; no Pure Land doctrine.
  • Mahayana (general): the bodhisattva ideal for all; arhatship seen as a stage, not the final goal.
  • Pure Land: trust in Amida; birth in Sukhavati after death.
  • Zen and some other Mahayana teachers: the Pure Land is the purified mind, here and now.
  • History: the Pure Land sutras came from India; Pure Land devotion grew in China and became major schools in Japan from the 12th–13th centuries.
  • Many Theravada Buddhists today also admire the bodhisatta ideal; a few take bodhisatta vows.
Mission 4Evaluate

"The bodhisattva ideal is better than the arhat ideal."

  • Reasons for: it aims to free all beings, not some. The Mahayana monk Santideva (8th century) argued that suffering is suffering, whoever has it. Full Buddhahood can help more than arhatship.
  • Reasons against: arhats are also compassionate and teach others. The arhat goal is reachable, and is what the Buddha taught his first disciples. "Better" may be a Mahayana judgement read back into Theravada.
  • Another view: the two ideals answer different questions: what is the end of suffering, and how best to help the world.
  • Judge it by: Which texts count as the Buddha's word? Is helping others separate from ending one's own craving?

Mission 5

Kamma is intention

  • Kamma (Sanskrit karma): "action". For Buddhists it means intentional action, of body, speech or mind.
  • The Buddha (paraphrase): it is intention that I call kamma.
  • Actions are like seeds; their results (vipaka) are the fruit, in this life or later ones.
  • Buddhists teach that no god rewards or punishes: kamma works as a natural law of cause and effect.
  • Not everything that happens is kamma: the Buddha also named illness, weather and other people's actions as causes.
  • Say: kamma is action and its results — not: kamma is fate.
Mission 5

Skilful and unskilful

  • Buddhists judge actions as skilful (kusala) or unskilful (akusala), not as obeying or breaking a god's command.
  • Unskilful actions come from the three poisons: greed, hatred, ignorance (delusion).
  • Skilful actions come from their opposites: generosity, loving-kindness, wisdom.
  • Skilful action leads towards happiness and the path; unskilful action to suffering.
Mission 5Read the source

The teaching of all Buddhas

> "Not to commit any sin, to do good, and to purify one's mind, that is the teaching of (all) the Awakened." (Dhammapada 183, Müller 1881)

  • Müller's "sin" (1881): modern translations say "evil" or "unwholesome".
  • Dhammapada 181–187 (paraphrase): even gods envy the awakened (181); human birth and hearing the Dhamma are rare (182); patience is the highest practice; one who harms others is no true renunciant (184); restraint, moderation and meditation (185); wealth never satisfies craving (186–187).

Q1 What three things does verse 183 ask?

Show answer

Avoid evil; do good; purify the mind.

Q2 Why does "purify one's mind" matter for kamma?

Show answer

Kamma is intention: a purified mind makes skilful intentions.

Mission 5

Rebirth and samsara

  • Samsara: the endless cycle of birth, death and rebirth.
  • Buddhists believe kamma shapes the circumstances of rebirth, and of this life too.
  • The Wheel of Life shows six realms: gods, jealous gods, humans, animals, hungry ghosts, hell beings. None is permanent.
  • Not a soul moving house (anatta): like a flame lit from another, the new life is connected to the old but not identical.
  • Buddhists teach that ending craving ends rebirth: nibbana.
Mission 5

Merit

  • Merit (punna): the good kamma made by skilful action.
  • Three bases of merit: giving (dana), ethics (sila), meditation (bhavana).
  • Lay Buddhists make merit by giving food to monks and nuns, keeping the precepts, chanting and meditating.
  • Transfer of merit: many Buddhists share merit with others, for example with relatives who have died.
  • Khuddakapatha (Nidhikanda Sutta, paraphrase): buried treasure can be lost or stolen, but the treasure of merit, laid up by giving, virtue and self-control, goes with you.

Mission 5

Karuna: compassion

  • Karuna: compassion: feeling others' suffering as real and wishing, and working, to relieve it.
  • The Buddha taught for 45 years out of compassion for all beings.
  • Karuna and wisdom are like the two wings of a bird: both are needed to fly.
  • Mahayana places karuna at the heart of the bodhisattva ideal.
Mission 5

Metta: loving-kindness

  • Metta (Sanskrit maitri): loving-kindness, warm goodwill wishing all beings to be happy, enemies included.
  • > "For hatred does not cease by hatred at any time: hatred ceases by love, this is an old rule." (Dhammapada 5, Müller 1881)
  • Karaniya Metta Sutta (Sutta Nipata 1.8; also in the Khuddakapatha), paraphrase: as a mother would protect her only child with her life, so let a person cultivate a boundless heart towards all beings.
  • Metta and karuna are two of the four "divine abodes" (brahmaviharas), with sympathetic joy and equanimity.
  • Practised in metta bhavana meditation (see the Meditation mission).

Mission 5Diversity

Kamma and compassion in different traditions

  • Theravada: strong emphasis on merit-making, especially giving to the monastic Sangha, and transferring merit to the dead.
  • Mahayana: bodhisattvas dedicate all their merit to the awakening of all beings.
  • Pure Land: Amida's merit, not one's own, brings birth in the Pure Land.
  • Tibetan: compassion trained deliberately, e.g. seeing every being as having once been one's mother.
  • Some Western and secular Buddhists (e.g. Stephen Batchelor, Buddhism Without Beliefs, 1997) stay agnostic about rebirth, while keeping kamma as a guide to how actions shape character now.
Mission 5Evaluate

"Buddhists should be good out of compassion, not to gain good kamma."

  • Reasons for: merit-seeking can become a subtle form of craving for oneself. The bodhisattva gives without thought of reward. Karuna aims at others' good.
  • Reasons against: the Buddha encouraged making merit; it supports lay practice and the Sangha. Kamma is simply how actions work, not a bribe. Wanting a good rebirth to keep practising is itself wise.
  • Another view: the motives can mix: an act done from compassion is skilful kamma.
  • Judge it by: Does motive matter more than outcome in Buddhism? Theravada or Mahayana? Monastic or lay life?

Mission 6

The Five Precepts

  • Pancasila: the five precepts (sila = ethics).
  • Your spec spells it pancha sila.
  • Each begins: "I undertake the training rule to abstain from…"
  • 1. Taking life
  • 2. Taking what is not given
  • 3. Sexual misconduct (AQA: misusing the senses)
  • 4. False speech
  • 5. Intoxicants that cloud the mind
  • They are promises a person makes, not commands from a god. Breaking one is a reason to recommit, not to be punished.
Mission 6

Each precept has a positive side

  • Abstain from harm → practise loving-kindness (metta).
  • Abstain from stealing → practise generosity (dana).
  • Abstain from misusing the senses → practise contentment and respect.
  • Abstain from false speech → practise truthful, kind speech.
  • Abstain from intoxicants → practise mindfulness: a clear mind.

Mission 6

The precepts today

  • Buddhists apply the precepts in different ways:
  • 1st, food: many Mahayana Buddhists (e.g. in China, Vietnam, Korea) are vegetarian; Theravada monks eat what is given, but should not eat meat from an animal killed for them; many Tibetans eat meat.
  • 1st, life and death: many Buddhists see abortion and euthanasia as breaking the first precept; others argue compassion and intention can matter in hard cases.
  • 3rd: "misusing the senses" can be applied to overindulgence of any kind, not only sex.
  • 4th: truthful speech applies to online posts and gossip.
  • 5th: some abstain from alcohol completely; others drink little, aiming to keep the mind clear.
Mission 6

The six perfections

  • In Mahayana Buddhism, the paramitas ("perfections") are the qualities a bodhisattva develops on the way to Buddhahood.
  • 1 Generosity (dana): giving time, things, teaching, protection.
  • 2 Morality (sila): keeping precepts; helping others.
  • 3 Patience (ksanti): bearing hardship and insult without anger.
  • 4 Energy (virya): joyful effort that does not give up.
  • 5 Meditation (dhyana): a calm, focused mind.
  • 6 Wisdom (prajna): seeing sunyata, the emptiness of all things.
Mission 6

Growing a perfection

  • A virtue becomes a perfection when wisdom joins it: giving with no clinging to "me the giver", "the gift" or "the one who receives".
  • How individuals develop them (examples):
  • Generosity: giving regularly, starting small (dana to monks, volunteering).
  • Patience: pausing before reacting; reflecting on anger as harmful.
  • Energy: a steady daily routine of practice.
  • Meditation and wisdom: daily sitting; studying sutras; reflecting on emptiness.

Mission 6Diversity

How many perfections?

  • Mahayana (main list): six perfections.
  • Mahayana (longer list): ten, adding skilful means, vow, power and knowledge, matching the ten stages of the bodhisattva path.
  • Theravada: ten paramis: generosity, virtue, renunciation, wisdom, energy, patience, truthfulness, determination, loving-kindness, equanimity. The Jataka stories show the Buddha perfecting these in past lives.
  • Theravada stresses the precepts and these qualities for everyone; Mahayana frames the perfections as the bodhisattva's path.
Mission 6Evaluate

"A Buddhist should never break a precept, whatever the situation."

  • Reasons for: precepts are firm commitments; one exception invites others. Every intentional killing or lie has kammic results. Many Buddhists keep them strictly.
  • Reasons against: kamma is about intention, so the same act can be skilful or unskilful. Mahayana teaches skilful means (upaya): a bodhisattva may accept bad kamma to save others. Compassion and wisdom may guide hard cases.
  • Judge it by: Are the precepts rules or training? Theravada or Mahayana? Who decides what counts as "skilful means"?
Mission 6Worked answer

Belief, source, development

Question: Explain two ways in which the Five Precepts guide Buddhists today. Refer to a source of wisdom and authority.

  • Way 1: the first precept guides diet. Development: many Mahayana Buddhists are vegetarian, to avoid causing animals to be killed, an expression of loving-kindness.
  • Way 2: the fourth precept guides speech. Source: Dhammapada 246–247 lists the one "who speaks untruth" among those who, "even in this world", dig up their own root. Development: so Buddhists avoid lies and harmful gossip, online and offline.
  • 5-mark (c) tip: two ways, each developed, and a source of wisdom and authority.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The Third Noble Truth (nirodha): Buddhists teach that suffering ends when craving ends; samsara is the cycle of rebirth driven by craving and kamma.
  • Nibbana means "blowing out" the fires of greed, hatred and ignorance; reached in life, with parinibbana at death; Enlightenment (bodhi) is awakening to reality.
  • Theravada aims at arahantship; Mahayana at Buddhahood for all beings; Zen stresses sudden awakening, Pure Land faith in Amitabha.

Mission 2

  • The Fourth Noble Truth (magga): the Noble Eightfold Path, the Middle Way between indulgence and self-torment, leads to the end of suffering.
  • Eight factors in three groups: panna (view, intention), sila (speech, action, livelihood), samadhi (effort, mindfulness, concentration).

Mission 3

  • A person is five changing khandhas: form, feeling, perception, mental formations, consciousness; none is a fixed self (anatta), all change (anicca), and clinging to them brings dukkha.
  • Mahayana teaches sunyata (all things empty of fixed self-nature) and Buddha-nature (tathagatagarbha): all beings can become Buddhas.

Mission 4

  • Theravada ideal: the arhat (arahant), who reaches nibbana through four stages; a Buddha discovers and teaches the Dhamma.
  • Mahayana ideal: the bodhisattva, who vows to become a Buddha for all beings; all have the potential for Buddhahood.
  • Pure Land: trust in Amida Buddha's vow brings birth in Sukhavati, where enlightenment is reached.

Mission 5

  • In Buddhist teaching, kamma is intentional action; skilful actions (rooted in generosity, love, wisdom) and unskilful ones (rooted in greed, hatred, ignorance) shape this life and rebirth in samsara.
  • Merit comes from giving, ethics and meditation; Buddhists teach that nibbana ends rebirth altogether.
  • Karuna (compassion) and metta (loving-kindness) are central virtues; compassion needs wisdom.

Mission 6

  • The Five Precepts are training rules: abstain from harming life, stealing, misusing the senses, false speech, intoxicants; each has a positive side.
  • Mahayana's six perfections (generosity, morality, patience, energy, meditation, wisdom) are developed through practice and joined with wisdom.
  • Buddhists apply the precepts differently today; Theravada lists ten paramis.
