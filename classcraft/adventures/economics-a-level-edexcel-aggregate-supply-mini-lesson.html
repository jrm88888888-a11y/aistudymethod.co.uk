Mission 1: Why can a jump in oil prices make a whole economy produce less and charge more at once?

Make a guess: Why can a jump in oil prices make a whole economy produce less and charge more at once?

Mission 1

Aggregate supply in the short run

  • Aggregate supply (AS): the total real output that all firms in an economy plan to produce at each price level, in a given period.
  • Axes: Price level (vertical), Real national output (Y) (horizontal).
  • Short run: some costs, especially money wages (wages in cash terms, also called nominal wages), are fixed. Firms cannot change every cost straight away.
  • The SRAS curve slopes upwards: a higher price level goes with more real output.
  • Long run: all costs can adjust, so the long-run curve (LRAS) is drawn differently.
Mission 1

Why SRAS slopes upwards

  • Sticky wages: money wages are set in contracts and pay deals, often for a year. They do not rise straight away when prices rise.
  • So when the price level rises:
    • firms get more for each unit they sell
    • many costs per unit stay the same
    • profit per unit rises, so firms supply more
  • Rising costs near capacity: to produce more, firms pay overtime and use older, less efficient machines. Extra output costs more, so it needs a higher price level.
  • The reverse holds too: a lower price level squeezes profit per unit, so firms cut output.
  • Say: "a higher price level", "real output" — not: "a higher price", "quantity". This is the whole economy, not one market.
Mission 1

Worked example: a firm with a fixed wage

  • Illustrative data. A firm sells each unit for 20.
  • Costs per unit: wages 12 (fixed by contract for a year), materials 4.
  • Profit per unit: 20 − 12 − 4 = 4.
  • The price level rises by 10%. The firm's price and its materials both rise by 10%:
    • price: 20 × 1.1 = 22
    • materials: 4 × 1.1 = 4.4
    • wages: still 12
  • New profit per unit: 22 − 12 − 4.4 = 5.6, up 40%.
  • Producing more now pays, so output rises: a movement up along SRAS.
  • When wages are renegotiated up 10% (to 13.2), profit per unit is 22 − 13.2 − 4.4 = 4.4: the same as 4 once prices are 10% higher. The extra reason to produce disappears in the long run.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A firm sells its product for 50. Per unit, it pays 30 in wages, fixed by contract for the year, and 10 for materials. The price level then rises by 10%, and the firm's price and materials cost rise by 10% too. What is the firm's new profit per unit?
Mission 1

Movement along SRAS or shift?

  • A change in the price level → a movement along SRAS.
    • Price level rises → output rises: an extension of SRAS.
    • Price level falls → output falls: a contraction of SRAS.
  • A change in firms' costs (anything other than the price level) → a shift of SRAS.
    • Costs rise → SRAS shifts left (up): less output at each price level.
    • Costs fall → SRAS shifts right (down).
  • Say: "SRAS shifts left" — not: "SRAS falls" or "SRAS moves up".

Mission 1

Real world: the 1973–74 oil shock

  • In October 1973, Arab oil producers began an embargo on oil exports to the United States and other countries.
  • The price of oil rose from 2.90 dollars a barrel before the embargo to 11.65 dollars a barrel in January 1974: about four times as much.
  • Oil is an input to transport, power, plastics and fertiliser, so costs rose across the economy: SRAS shifted left.
  • The US was in recession from November 1973 to March 1975, while prices rose fast.
  • Rising prices and falling output together: stagflation.
  • Caution: US inflation was already rising before the embargo. The oil shock added to it; it was not the only cause.
Mission 1Evaluate

How much damage does a cost shock do?

It depends on:

  • Size and duration. A brief price spike may barely move SRAS; a lasting rise shifts it for years.
  • How much firms use the input. Energy-heavy industries (steel, chemicals, transport) are hit far harder than services.
  • Second-round effects. If workers win higher money wages to keep up with prices, SRAS shifts left again.
  • Firms' margins. Firms with high profits may absorb some of the cost rather than raise prices.
  • Policy. Subsidies or tax cuts can offset some of the rise in costs, at a cost to public finances.
Mission check

?New software lets workers in most firms produce 10% more each hour. Money wages and AD do not change. What is the most likely short-run effect?

Anything that changes firms' costs per unit shifts SRAS; only a change in the price level moves the economy along it. Higher productivity also raises capacity in the long run: a separate, long-run effect.

Mission 2

LRAS: the economy's capacity

  • Long run: long enough for money (nominal) wages and all other input prices to adjust fully to the price level.
  • Long-run aggregate supply (LRAS): the real output the economy can produce when all its resources are fully employed.
  • This is its productive capacity (potential output), sometimes called the full-employment level of output, Yfe.
  • In the classical (neo-classical, monetarist) view, LRAS is vertical at that output.
  • Capacity depends on the quantity and quality of land, labour, capital and enterprise, and on technology, not on the price level.
  • "Full employment" still leaves some unemployment (people between jobs, for example). It is not zero.
Mission 2

Worked example: when all costs catch up

  • Illustrative data. The same firm as before: price 20, wages 12, materials 4, profit per unit 4.
  • Short run: the price level rises 10%, wages are stuck at 12, so profit per unit rises to 5.6. Output rises.
  • Long run: the wage contract is renegotiated. Wages catch up: 12 × 1.1 = 13.2.
  • Now every price and cost is 10% higher: 22 − 13.2 − 4.4 = 4.4.
  • 4.4 is exactly 4 × 1.1: real profit per unit is unchanged.
  • So firms have no lasting reason to produce more. Output returns to capacity.
  • As wages rise, SRAS shifts left until the economy is back on LRAS: this is the link between SRAS and LRAS.
  • A higher price level, the same real output: the long-run curve is vertical.
Quick check
?Why is the long-run aggregate supply curve drawn vertical?
Mission 2

The Keynesian AS curve

  • Keynes argued an economy can stay below full employment for a long time: money wages rarely fall, even with mass unemployment.
  • The Keynesian AS curve has three sections (often drawn as a reverse L):
    • Horizontal: lots of spare capacity. Firms can hire idle workers and machines without raising costs. Output rises; the price level does not.
    • Upward-sloping: bottlenecks. Some industries and skills run short, so costs and prices start to rise.
    • Vertical at Yfe: full capacity. More output is impossible; only the price level rises.
  • Keynesian economists draw this as their long-run AS. The classical view draws LRAS vertical only.
Mission 2Graph lab

Keynesian or classical?

Illustrative data. Keynesian AS: flat at a price level of 100 up to output 800; vertical at Yfe = 1000.

  • Takeaway: with spare capacity, the Keynesian curve says more AD raises output (600 → 800) at the same price level; with a vertical LRAS, in the long run more AD only raises the price level.
Mission 2

Two views, two policy messages

  • Classical/monetarist view:
    • the economy returns to Yfe by itself, as wages and prices adjust
    • in the long run, more AD only raises the price level
    • to raise output for good, raise capacity: supply-side policies
  • Keynesian view:
    • an economy can stay below Yfe; adjustment is slow or does not happen
    • with spare capacity, more AD (e.g. government spending) raises output and employment without much inflation
    • near Yfe, more AD mostly raises prices
  • Output below capacity: a negative output gap. Above it: a positive output gap.
  • Both views agree that capacity limits real output. They disagree on whether, and how fast, an economy returns to Yfe by itself.
Quick check
?An economy is getting close to full capacity. Some industries cannot find enough skilled workers; others still have idle machines. The government raises its spending, shifting AD to the right. According to the Keynesian AS curve, what is the most likely result?
Mission 2

What shifts LRAS

  • LRAS shifts right when capacity grows:
    • Quantity of factors: a larger labour force (population growth, net migration, more people choosing to work), more capital from investment, new natural resources.
    • Quality of factors: education, training and health raise human capital; better machines.
    • Technology: new methods let the same inputs make more.
    • Productivity and efficiency: more output per worker or per unit of capital.
    • Institutions: secure property rights, rule of law, a sound financial system, low corruption.
  • Also: skills, regulation (less red tape), demographics, migration, competition policy.
  • LRAS shifts left if capacity shrinks: war or natural disaster destroys capital, emigration, an ageing population.
  • Some changes shift both SRAS and LRAS: higher productivity cuts costs now and raises capacity. A temporary rise in oil prices shifts SRAS only.
  • Supply-side policies aim to shift LRAS right: education and training, infrastructure, tax incentives, deregulation, competition policy.
Mission 2

LRAS and the PPC tell the same story

  • The PPC shows the most an economy can make of two goods with all resources fully used.
  • LRAS shows the same capacity as a single total of real output.
  • On the PPC → output at LRAS. Inside the PPC → output below capacity (unused resources).
  • PPC shifts outwards ↔ LRAS shifts right: long-run economic growth.

Mission 2

Real world: Japan's shrinking workforce

  • Japan's working-age population (aged 15–64) peaked in 1995 at about 86.9 million.
  • By 2024 it was about 73.7 million: around 15% lower.
  • Fewer people of working age, other things equal, means less capacity: a force pushing LRAS left.
  • But LRAS also depends on how many of those people work, on capital, skills and technology.
  • Question to weigh: which of these could offset a shrinking workforce, and how fast?
Mission 2Evaluate

Can policy shift LRAS?

It depends on:

  • Time lags. Education and infrastructure take years, sometimes decades, to raise capacity.
  • Cost. Supply-side spending has an opportunity cost and adds to government borrowing until it pays off.
  • Whether it works. Training may not match the skills firms need; tax cuts may not change effort much.
  • Where the economy is. With a large negative output gap, Keynesians argue that weak AD, not capacity, is the main problem.
  • Side effects. Deregulation or lower benefits may raise inequality; there are trade-offs to weigh.
Mission check

?An economy is producing at its full-employment level of output. A boom in export orders shifts AD to the right. Its resources and technology do not change. According to the vertical LRAS model, what happens in the long run?

Capacity sets real output in the long run. More spending alone cannot push past it.

Mission 3

Mission 3: If a slump ends by itself, why do some economists say we should not wait?

Make a guess: If a slump ends by itself, why do some economists say we should not wait?

Mission 3

Two views of aggregate supply

  • Classical view: LRAS is vertical at full-employment output, Yfe.
  • Keynesian view: AS is reverse-L shaped.
    • flat with large spare capacity, rising as bottlenecks appear, vertical only at full capacity
  • The two views agree at full capacity: more AD there only raises prices.
  • They disagree about below full capacity: does the economy get back to full-employment output on its own, and how fast?
Mission 3

A recessionary gap closes itself (in theory)

  • Start: AD has fallen. Output is below full employment; unemployment is above its natural rate.
  • With many people looking for work, nominal wages fall (and other input prices fall).
  • Lower costs → SRAS shifts right.
  • The price level falls, so more real output is bought (a movement down along AD).
  • This continues until SRAS meets AD on LRAS.
  • Long run: output back at full employment, unemployment back at its natural rate, price level lower.
  • Adjustment can be slow. Until it happens, output is lost and unemployment stays high, so governments and central banks may act instead.
Mission 3

An inflationary gap closes itself

  • Start: AD has risen. Output is above full employment; unemployment is below its natural rate.
  • Labour is scarce, and workers expect prices to keep rising: they win higher nominal wages.
  • Higher costs → SRAS shifts left.
  • The price level rises again; real output falls back.
  • Long run: output back at full employment, unemployment at its natural rate, price level higher than before the boom.
  • So a lasting rise in AD has no lasting effect on real output: only the price level is higher.
  • Say: "SRAS shifts", not "LRAS shifts": capacity has not changed.
Quick check
?An economy has an inflationary (positive output) gap. There is no change in policy, and wages and prices are flexible. Which describes how the economy returns to full employment?

Mission 3

Why a slump can last

  • Why nominal wages may not fall:
    • wage contracts last a year or more
    • trade unions resist pay cuts
    • minimum wage laws set a floor
    • firms prefer to lay off some workers than cut everyone's pay and damage morale
  • Prices can be slow to fall too: changing them has costs.
  • Keynes's second point: even if wages fall, workers' incomes fall. Spending falls, so AD may shift left again.
  • Falling prices raise the real burden of debts: households and firms may cut spending further.
  • Result: the economy can sit on the flat part of Keynesian AS, below full-employment output, for years.
  • A negative output gap with spare capacity that persists.
  • Keynes (1923): "In the long run we are all dead."
Quick check
?In the Keynesian view, why might a negative (recessionary) output gap last for years without government action?
Mission 3

Real world: the Great Depression

  • US, 1929–33: real GDP fell by about a quarter; consumer prices fell by about a quarter.
  • Unemployment reached about 25% in 1933.
  • Wages and prices did fall, but output stayed far below capacity for years.
  • Keynes (The General Theory, 1936): an economy can be stuck with too little demand; government spending can lift it.
  • Friedman and Schwartz (1963): the US money supply fell by about a third from 1929 to 1933. The central bank let it happen: a policy mistake, not proof that markets cannot adjust.
  • Both views agree the slump was deep and long. They disagree on why, and so on the cure.
Mission 3

What each view implies for policy

  • Keynesian: in a deep slump, use expansionary fiscal policy (more government spending, lower taxes).
    • AD shifts right on the flat part of AS: real output and jobs rise, with little rise in the price level.
  • Classical: expansionary policy at full employment raises output only for a while.
  • Contractionary fiscal policy (less spending, higher taxes) closes an inflationary gap in both models: AD shifts left.
  • Today most economists accept parts of both. The real argument: how slowly does the economy adjust, and how well can policy be timed?
Mission 3Graph lab

Mission 3Evaluate

How long is the long run?

It depends on:

  • Wage and price flexibility. Contracts, unions, minimum wages and labour-market rules decide how fast SRAS shifts back towards LRAS.
  • Expectations. If people expect low, stable inflation, wages may adjust faster; if confidence collapses, spending may keep falling.
  • Size of the gap. A small gap may close in a few quarters; a deep slump with falling prices and heavy debts can take years.
  • Policy timing. Policy acts with lags; badly timed stimulus can add to inflation once recovery has begun.
Mission check

?An economy is in a recessionary gap after a fall in AD. Wages and prices are flexible, but take three years to adjust fully. Path 1: no policy. Path 2: an expansionary policy shifts AD back to the right straight away. How do the two paths compare?

The slump does end by itself. The case for acting is the output and jobs lost while wages and prices adjust.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • SRAS slopes upwards because money wages and some other costs are sticky in the short run.
  • A change in the price level → a movement along SRAS. A change in costs (wages, raw materials, energy, exchange rate, taxes, subsidies, productivity) → a shift.

Mission 2

  • LRAS shows the economy's capacity: the output possible with all resources fully employed.
  • Classical/monetarist: LRAS vertical. Keynesian: AS horizontal with spare capacity, then rising, then vertical at Yfe.
  • LRAS shifts with the quantity and quality of factors, technology and institutions.

Mission 3

  • Without policy, flexible wages shift SRAS until the economy is back on LRAS: output at full employment, unemployment at its natural rate.
  • Keynesians argue wages are sticky downwards and falling incomes cut spending, so a negative gap can persist; the vertical-LRAS view expects fairly quick self-correction.
  • Policy follows: Keynesians favour fiscal stimulus in a slump; the vertical-LRAS view favours supply-side policy and stable inflation.
