Mission 1

Mission 1: Why does salt vanish into water while oil sits on top in a separate layer?

Make a guess: Why does salt vanish into water while oil sits on top in a separate layer?

Mission 1

The medium for life

  • Water is the most abundant molecule in cells: about 70% of a typical cell's mass.
  • Most reactions of life happen in aqueous solution (dissolved in water).
  • Every one of those properties starts with the shape and charges of one small molecule.
Mission 1

A polar molecule

  • Each O–H bond is covalent: a pair of electrons is shared.
  • Oxygen attracts the shared electrons more strongly than hydrogen does.
  • Unequal sharing makes a polar covalent bond: oxygen is δ−, each hydrogen δ+.
  • The molecule is bent (about 104.5°), so the charges do not cancel. Water is a dipole: a δ− end and a δ+ end.
  • Overall, the molecule is neutral.
  • Say: "partial charges (δ+, δ−)" — not: "water is made of ions".
Mission 1

Hydrogen bonds

  • The δ+ hydrogen of one molecule is attracted to the δ− oxygen of another molecule: a hydrogen bond.
  • Hydrogen bonds are between molecules. The O–H bonds within a molecule are covalent.
  • Each hydrogen bond is weak, much weaker than a covalent bond. In liquid water they constantly break and re-form.
  • One molecule can hydrogen-bond to up to four neighbours. Millions of weak bonds add up to a strong effect.
  • Say: "hydrogen bond between water molecules" — not: "the hydrogen bond between H and O in the molecule".

Mission 1

Why some things dissolve and others don't

  • Ions (e.g. Na⁺, Cl⁻): water's δ− oxygens face cations; its δ+ hydrogens face anions. Each ion is surrounded by water and separates from the crystal.
  • Polar molecules (e.g. glucose, with many –OH groups): form hydrogen bonds with water.
  • Charged and polar substances are hydrophilic: they dissolve.
  • Non-polar molecules (e.g. oils): no charges to attract water. Water molecules stay hydrogen-bonded to each other and exclude them. They are hydrophobic.
  • Dissolving is physical: molecules stay whole; no covalent bonds break.
  • Say: "ions are attracted to the partial charges of water" — not: "ions form hydrogen bonds with water".
Mission 1

The solvent at work

  • Metabolism: reactants dissolved in the cytoplasm can move and collide. Most enzymes catalyse reactions in aqueous solution.
  • Transport: dissolved substances are carried in a moving stream of water.
  • Animals: blood plasma carries glucose, amino acids, ions and urea.
  • Plants: xylem sap carries mineral ions; phloem sap carries sucrose.
Mission 1

Water as a metabolite

  • A metabolite takes part in metabolic reactions as a reactant or product.
  • Condensation joins two molecules and releases water.
  • Hydrolysis breaks a bond using water.
  • Water is also a reactant in photosynthesis and a product of aerobic respiration.
  • More in a later lesson.
Quick check

Mission check

?In blood plasma, glucose travels freely dissolved. Cholesterol does not: it travels inside lipoprotein particles whose outer surface carries polar and charged groups. Why does cholesterol need this carrier?

Like oil in the hook, cholesterol is non-polar, so water holds onto itself instead. Like salt, anything charged or polar is pulled into solution.

Mission 2

Ions: small, charged, specific

  • An ion is an atom, or a group of atoms, with an electric charge.
  • Cation: positive, e.g. Na⁺. Anion: negative, e.g. Cl⁻.
  • Inorganic ions dissolve in water: cytoplasm, blood, tissue fluid, plant sap.
  • Each type of ion has a specific role, set by its properties: its charge, its size, what it binds to.
  • One ion cannot stand in for another.
Mission 2

Names and symbols

  • Magnesium Mg²⁺, iron Fe²⁺, calcium Ca²⁺, phosphate PO4³⁻.
  • The superscript gives the charge: ²⁺ means two positive charges; ³⁻ three negative.
  • Ammonium and nitrate both contain nitrogen, but carry opposite charges.
Quick check
?Which ion is given with its correct symbol?
Mission 2

Four ions, four jobs

  • Iron ions (Fe²⁺): one at the centre of each haem group in haemoglobin. Each haem binds one oxygen molecule.
  • Phosphate (PO4³⁻): part of DNA, RNA and ATP.
Mission 2

More ions, more jobs

  • Ca²⁺: in bones and teeth (as calcium phosphate); triggers muscle contraction.
  • Mg²⁺: at the centre of each chlorophyll molecule.
Quick check

Quick check
?Which molecules need phosphate ions to be built?
Mission 2

What plants need from the soil

  • Roots absorb water and ions from the soil through root hairs.
  • Nitrate (NO3⁻): nitrogen for amino acids (→ proteins), nucleotides (→ DNA, RNA, ATP) and chlorophyll.
  • Magnesium (Mg²⁺): at the centre of every chlorophyll molecule.
  • Lack nitrate: stunted growth; older leaves turn yellow (chlorosis).
  • Lack magnesium: yellowing between the veins, older leaves first.
  • Say: "ions are raw materials" — not: "plant food" (they supply no energy).
Mission 2Practical

Mineral deficiency

  • Specified practical: investigation into the role of nitrogen and magnesium in plant growth.
  • Grow equal starting numbers of duckweed (Lemna) plants, or seedlings, in culture solutions:
  • complete (the control) · lacking nitrate · lacking magnesium
  • Keep volume, light, temperature and starting size the same. Cover the solutions to stop algae.
  • Over 2–3 weeks: count fronds (or measure mass, height, leaf number). Record leaf colour.
  • Calculate: mean per solution; each mean as a percentage of the control mean.

Mission 3

Mission 3: How can weak attractions between molecules hold up an insect and keep a lake's temperature steady?

Make a guess: How can weak attractions between molecules hold up an insect and keep a lake's temperature steady?

Mission 3

Cohesion and surface tension

  • Cohesion: water molecules attract each other by hydrogen bonds.
  • At the surface, molecules are pulled inwards and sideways, not outwards.
  • Result: surface tension. The surface resists breaking, like a stretched film.
  • A pond skater stands on the surface without breaking it, held up by surface tension: a habitat on the water surface.
  • Say: "cohesion" for water attracted to water — not: for water attracted to anything else.
Quick check
?A pond skater rests on a pond. Its legs make small dents in the water surface but do not pass through it. What allows the surface to support the insect?
Mission 3

A temperature buffer

  • Specific heat capacity: energy needed to raise 1 kg by 1 °C. Water: about 4200 J kg⁻¹ °C⁻¹.
  • That is high: about 4 times that of air, per kilogram.
  • Why: much of the energy goes into breaking hydrogen bonds, not into making molecules move faster.
  • So water's temperature changes slowly. Lakes and seas are stable habitats.
  • Bodies are mostly water, so body temperature is buffered too: enzymes stay near their optimum.
  • Say: "temperature changes slowly / stays stable" — not: "water keeps things warm".
Mission 3

Worked example: cooling by evaporation

  • To evaporate, a molecule must break all its hydrogen bonds. That takes a lot of energy.
  • Latent heat of vaporisation at body temperature: about 2400 J g⁻¹.
  • Energy comes from the skin, so the skin cools. Sweating (mammals) and transpiration (plants) use this.
  • Example (illustrative data): a runner must lose 1200 kJ of heat by sweating.
  • Mass of sweat = 1 200 000 J ÷ 2400 J g⁻¹ = 500 g, about half a litre.
  • Compare: warming 1 g of water by 1 °C takes only 4.2 J. Evaporating it takes 2400 J: about 570 times as much.
  • Big cooling effect, little water lost.
Quick check
?(Illustrative data.) Over a clear spring day and night, the air above a lake varies between 2 °C and 18 °C, but the lake water 1 m below the surface stays between 7 °C and 8 °C. Which explanation of the stable water temperature is correct?
Mission check

?Dogs lose little heat by sweating. On a hot day a dog pants: air moves quickly over its wet tongue and airways. How does panting cool the dog?

The same hydrogen bonds that steady the hook's lake make evaporation a powerful way to cool.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Glucose and fructose are built from exactly the same atoms. So what makes them different sugars?

Make a guess: Glucose and fructose are built from exactly the same atoms. So what makes them different sugars?

Mission 4

Single sugars

  • A monosaccharide is a single sugar unit: the monomer of carbohydrates.
  • Named by number of carbon atoms: triose (3 C), pentose (5 C), hexose (6 C).
  • Triose example: glyceraldehyde, C3H6O3.
  • Hexoses: glucose, fructose, galactose. Pentoses: ribose (in RNA and ATP), deoxyribose (in DNA).
  • Glucose and fructose are both C6H12O6: same atoms, arranged differently. They are isomers.
Mission 4

Rings: count every carbon

  • In water, pentoses and hexoses exist mostly as rings.
  • Glucose: ring of 5 C + 1 O. Carbon 6 sits outside the ring, in a CH2OH group.
  • Ribose: ring of 4 C + 1 O. Carbon 5 sits outside.
  • Fructose (as in sucrose): ring of 4 C + 1 O, with two carbons outside.
  • So ring size does not tell you the sugar type. Count all the carbons.
  • In glucose, number the carbons from the one to the right of the ring O: C1, C2 … (In fructose, C1 is outside the ring.)
Quick check
?Two monosaccharides, P and Q, are drawn in their ring forms. In P the ring is made of four carbon atoms and one oxygen atom, with one further carbon atom (in a CH2OH group) attached outside the ring. In Q the ring is also made of four carbon atoms and one oxygen atom, but two further carbon atoms (each in a CH2OH group) are attached outside the ring. How should P and Q be classified?
Mission 4

α-glucose and β-glucose

  • Two forms of glucose: same formula, same ring.
  • They differ only at carbon 1.
  • α-glucose: –OH on C1 points below the ring.
  • β-glucose: –OH on C1 points above the ring.
  • A tiny difference with large effects: α-glucose builds starch and glycogen; β-glucose builds cellulose (see Mission 5).
  • Eduqas calls this "alpha and beta structural isomerism".
  • Say: "the –OH on carbon 1 is inverted" — not: "β-glucose has different atoms".
Quick check
?Which statement about α-glucose and β-glucose is correct?
Mission 4

Two sugars make a disaccharide

  • Two monosaccharides join by condensation: a glycosidic bond forms and water is released.
  • Maltose = glucose + glucose. Two α-glucose, joined C1 to C4: a 1,4-glycosidic bond.
  • Sucrose = glucose + fructose.
  • Lactose = glucose + galactose.
  • Hydrolysis splits a disaccharide back into its monosaccharides, using water.
  • Given two ring formulae: circle the –OH on C1 of one and the –OH on C4 of the other. H2O leaves; –O– links C1 to C4.
Quick check
?The enzyme maltase, in the small intestine, acts on maltose. Which statement describes the reaction?
Mission check

?Galactose and glucose both have the formula C6H12O6 and both form a ring of five carbons and one oxygen. They differ only in which way the –OH on carbon 4 points. Which statement is correct?

Same atoms, different arrangement: that is all it takes to make a new sugar, from glucose and fructose to α and β.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Starch and cellulose are both chains of glucose. Why can one feed you while the other builds a tree?

Make a guess: Starch and cellulose are both chains of glucose. Why can one feed you while the other builds a tree?

Mission 5

One monomer, three polymers

  • Starch: energy store in plants.
  • Glycogen: energy store in animals and fungi.
  • Cellulose: strong walls of plant cells.
  • All three are polymers of glucose, built by condensation.
  • Units are joined by glycosidic bonds.
  • What differs: which form of glucose, and how the units are connected.
Mission 5

Starch: amylose and amylopectin

  • Made of α-glucose.
  • Amylose: 1,4-glycosidic bonds only. Unbranched. Coils into a helix: compact.
  • Amylopectin: 1,4 bonds plus 1,6-glycosidic bonds at branch points, about every 25 units.
  • Starch grains in plant cells, e.g. potato tubers and seeds, contain both.
Mission 5

Glycogen

  • Made of α-glucose, like amylopectin.
  • 1,4 bonds, with 1,6 branches about every 10 units: more branched than amylopectin.
  • Very compact granules in liver and muscle cells.
  • Enzymes remove glucose only from chain ends. More branches = more ends = faster release.
  • Fits animals: they can need glucose for respiration very quickly.
Mission 5

What makes a good store

  • Compact: coiling and branching pack many glucose units into little space.
  • Relatively insoluble because the molecules are so large. So they do not draw water into the cell by osmosis.
  • Too large to leave the cell.
  • Easily hydrolysed to α-glucose when energy is needed; easily built up again by condensation.
  • Say: "relatively insoluble because they are so large" — not: "insoluble because they are non-polar".

Quick check

Mission 5

Cellulose

  • (1) Made of β-glucose. To form each 1,4-glycosidic bond, every other unit is inverted (rotated 180°).
  • (2) The alternating units give straight, unbranched chains.
  • (3) Straight chains lie side by side, linked by many hydrogen bonds between their –OH groups: microfibrils.
  • Microfibrils group into fibres with high tensile strength.
  • Cell walls resist stretching, so plant cells can become turgid without bursting.
  • Say: "hydrogen bonds between chains" — not: "covalent cross-links" or "coiled like starch".
Quick check
?Cellulose gives plant cell walls high tensile strength. Which feature of cellulose is responsible for this?
Mission 5

Chitin

  • A structural polysaccharide: exoskeletons of insects and crustaceans; cell walls of fungi.
  • Its monomer is a β-glucose with a nitrogen-containing group (–NHCOCH3) in place of the –OH on carbon 2.
  • Like cellulose: every other unit inverted, 1,4 bonds, straight chains.
  • Parallel chains hydrogen-bond into strong microfibrils.
  • Unlike cellulose: chitin contains nitrogen.
Quick check

Mission 6

What makes a lipid

  • Lipids dissolve in non-polar solvents but only sparingly in water.
  • Mostly non-polar C–H bonds: few δ+ or δ− regions to attract water. They are hydrophobic.
  • Lipids include fats, oils, waxes, phospholipids and steroids.
  • Say: "insoluble because non-polar" — not: "because the molecules are large" or "because they are lighter than water".
Mission 6

Building a triglyceride

  • (1) Glycerol has three –OH groups. A fatty acid is a hydrocarbon chain (R) with a –COOH group: RCOOH.
  • (2) Condensation: the –COOH of a fatty acid reacts with an –OH of glycerol. An ester bond forms; water is released.
  • (3) Three times: one glycerol + three fatty acids → one triglyceride + 3 H2O.
  • Hydrolysis breaks the ester bonds, releasing glycerol and three fatty acids (e.g. lipase in digestion).
  • A triglyceride is not a polymer.
  • Given formulae: the –OH of the fatty acid's –COOH and the H of glycerol's –OH leave as H2O.
  • Say: "one glycerol and three fatty acids" — not: "three glycerols and one fatty acid".
Quick check
?One glycerol molecule and three fatty acid molecules react by condensation to form one triglyceride. How much water is produced?
Mission 6

Saturated and unsaturated

  • Saturated: only single C–C bonds. Every carbon holds as many H atoms as it can: saturated with hydrogen.
  • Unsaturated: at least one C=C double bond.
  • In natural fats, each C=C puts a kink in the chain. Saturated chains are straight.
  • Only C=C in the chain counts. The C=O in –COOH does not make a fatty acid unsaturated.
  • Say: "saturated with hydrogen" — not: "full of double bonds".
Mission 6

Kinks and melting points

  • Straight saturated chains pack closely: strong attractions between molecules → higher melting point.
  • Kinked unsaturated chains cannot pack closely: weaker attractions → lower melting point.
  • Rich in saturated fatty acids: fat, solid at room temperature (butter).
  • Rich in unsaturated fatty acids: oil, liquid at room temperature (olive oil).
  • Melting breaks no covalent bonds. Molecules just move apart.
Quick checkRead the data
?The graph shows the approximate melting points of four fatty acids. Each has a chain of 18 carbon atoms; they differ only in the number of double bonds between carbon atoms in the chain. Which statement is best supported by the data?
Mission 6

A dense energy store

  • Oxidising triglycerides releases about 37 kJ g⁻¹; carbohydrate about 17 kJ g⁻¹. About twice as much per gram.
  • Why: long hydrocarbon chains, rich in C–H bonds, with little oxygen.
  • Insoluble: no osmotic effect, and stored without the water that glycogen holds.
  • So a lot of energy fits in a small, light store: adipose tissue in animals, oil droplets in seeds.
  • Say: "energy is released when fatty acids are oxidised in respiration" — not: "energy is stored in the ester bonds".
Mission 6

Saturated fat and health: the evidence

  • Controlled feeding trials: replacing saturated fat with unsaturated fat lowers blood LDL cholesterol.
  • Raised LDL cholesterol is a risk factor for atherosclerosis and cardiovascular disease (CVD).
  • Randomised trials (Cochrane review, 2020; 15 trials, about 57 000 people, at least 2 years each):
  • Cutting saturated fat: about 17% fewer combined cardiovascular events.
  • Little or no difference in deaths from all causes or from CVD.
  • Replacing saturated fat with polyunsaturated fat or with carbohydrate both appeared useful.
  • Observational studies alone can show only a correlation; randomised trials test cause.
Quick check
?In the trials on the last screen, people who cut saturated fat had about 17% fewer cardiovascular events, but little or no difference in deaths from all causes. Which conclusion is best supported?
Mission check

?To make a spread, manufacturers react a liquid vegetable oil with hydrogen, using a catalyst. Some C=C double bonds in its fatty acid chains become C–C single bonds. How does the oil change?

Same molecule type, different tails: straight tails pack into a solid; kinked tails slide past each other as a liquid.

Mission 7: Oily molecules avoid water, yet they wrap every cell in a thin film. How does that film build itself?

Make a guess: Oily molecules avoid water, yet they wrap every cell in a thin film. How does that film build itself?

Mission 7

Lipids are more than fat

  • Lipids dissolve in non-polar solvents but only sparingly in water: they are hydrophobic.
  • Here: phospholipids, the lipids of membranes.
  • Not every lipid is an energy store.
Mission 7

Building a phospholipid

  • Start with a triglyceride: glycerol + three fatty acids.
  • Swap one fatty acid for a phosphate-containing group.
  • So: glycerol + two fatty acids + phosphate group.
  • Given the formulae: water leaves from each –OH and the group it joins.
  • Result: a head (phosphate) and two tails (fatty acids).
Mission 7

Amphipathic: two personalities

  • Head: phosphate group, charged. It is hydrophilic: it interacts with water.
  • Tails: hydrocarbon chains, non-polar. They are hydrophobic.
  • This is called amphipathic.
  • Say: "the tails are excluded from water" — not: "the tails repel water".
Quick check
?Which components are linked together by condensation reactions to form one phospholipid molecule?
Mission 7

The bilayer builds itself

  • (1) On water, phospholipids form a single layer: heads in the water, tails in the air.
  • (2) Under water, tails cannot stay in the air. They hide by pairing up tail to tail.
  • (3) Result: a phospholipid bilayer. Heads face the water on both sides; tails form a non-polar core.
  • No enzymes, no energy from the cell: water molecules hydrogen-bond to each other and exclude the tails.
  • A sheet's edges still expose tails, so it closes into a sphere.
  • The molecules are not covalently bonded to each other, so the bilayer is fluid.
Quick check
?Phospholipids are shaken with water in a test tube, with no cells or enzymes present. What happens, and why?

  • Phospholipid: the bilayer's non-polar core stops water-soluble molecules and ions crossing freely.
Mission check

?Lipoproteins carry cholesterol and triglycerides in blood plasma, which is mostly water. Each lipoprotein has an oily core of cholesterol and triglyceride, surrounded by a single layer of phospholipids (with some proteins). How are the phospholipids in this layer most likely arranged?

One rule builds membranes, vesicles and lipoproteins: heads to the water, tails away from it.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Unequal sharing of electrons makes water polar (δ− O, δ+ H); hydrogen bonds form between molecules.
  • Charged and polar substances dissolve (hydrophilic); non-polar ones are excluded (hydrophobic). That makes water the medium for metabolism and transport.

Mission 2

  • Each inorganic ion has a specific role that no other ion can fill.
  • Plants need nitrate for proteins, nucleic acids and chlorophyll, and magnesium for chlorophyll.

Mission 3

  • Hydrogen bonds give cohesion (surface tension; unbroken xylem columns) and a high specific heat capacity (stable temperatures).
  • Evaporation must break hydrogen bonds: a large cooling effect for little water lost.

Mission 4

  • Monosaccharides are named by their carbons (triose, pentose, hexose); isomers share a formula but not an arrangement; α- and β-glucose differ only at carbon 1.
  • Condensation of two monosaccharides forms a glycosidic bond: maltose (glucose + glucose), sucrose (glucose + fructose), lactose (glucose + galactose).

Mission 5

  • α-glucose → starch (amylose helix; branched amylopectin) and glycogen (highly branched): compact, insoluble stores. β-glucose → straight cellulose chains hydrogen-bonded into microfibrils: strength.

Mission 6

  • A triglyceride is one glycerol + three fatty acids joined by ester bonds (three condensations); hydrophobic, energy-dense store.
  • Saturated chains are straight and pack closely (fats, higher melting point); C=C kinks unsaturated chains (oils, lower melting point).

Mission 7

  • Phospholipids are amphipathic: a hydrophilic phosphate head and two hydrophobic tails; in water they form bilayers by themselves.
