1896–1914 · liberal Italy

A state without a nation

Italy was unified in 1870 but not integrated. The liberal state was narrow, corrupt-looking and — crucially — never legitimised in the eyes of large parts of the population.

  • Adowa, 1 March 1896: an Italian army was destroyed by Ethiopian forces — a colonial humiliation without parallel among the European powers. It brought down Crispi and left a lasting appetite for imperial redemption.
  • The Church: since 1870 the papacy had refused to recognise the Italian state, and Catholics were long discouraged from participating in national politics. A state at odds with the faith of most of its citizens starts with a legitimacy deficit.
  • The South: the questione meridionale — poverty, illiteracy, mass emigration — meant national unity was a fiction over much of the peninsula.
  • Giolitti, dominant from 1903 to 1914, governed by trasformismo: buying off opponents and manufacturing majorities. He extended the franchise to near-universal manhood suffrage in 1912 and fought the Libyan War (1911–12). But his methods bred contempt for parliamentary politics itself.

The examinable link: Fascism did not have to destroy respect for liberal parliamentary government in Italy. Trasformismo, the Southern question and the papal breach had already done a great deal of that work.

1915–1922 · war and the mutilated victory

How the war made Fascism possible

Italy entered the war in May 1915 on the Allied side, having been promised territory in the secret Treaty of London (April 1915). It was a war chosen by a small elite against the wishes of most of the country.

  • Cost: hundreds of thousands of dead, catastrophic defeat at Caporetto (October 1917), eventual victory at Vittorio Veneto (1918) — and economic ruin.
  • The 'mutilated victory' — the phrase was Gabriele D'Annunzio's — expressed nationalist fury that the peace settlement withheld promised gains. In September 1919 D'Annunzio seized Fiume with a private army and held it for over a year while the government dithered. He also supplied Fascism with its political theatre: the balcony speech, the salute, the chanted dialogue with the crowd.
  • The Biennio Rosso (1919–20): strikes, land occupations and factory occupations terrified the propertied classes. When the liberal state failed to crush them, landowners and industrialists began funding squadristi — Fascist paramilitary squads — to do it instead.
  • Mussolini founded the Fasci di Combattimento in March 1919. It won no seats that year. Its transformation came from squadrismo in the countryside, and from Mussolini's decision to move sharply rightwards.

1922 · the March on Rome

How Mussolini actually took power

The myth is an insurrection. The reality is a handover.

  • By 1922 Fascist squads controlled large areas of the north and centre through violence, while Mussolini negotiated with the establishment and dropped his early republican and anti-clerical positions.
  • The March on Rome was launched on 28 October 1922. It was ill-armed and could almost certainly have been stopped: the army was ready, and the prime minister Facta asked King Victor Emmanuel III to declare martial law.
  • The King refused to sign. On 29–30 October 1922 he invited Mussolini to form a government. Mussolini arrived in Rome by train, and was appointed — legally — as Prime Minister of a coalition in which Fascists were a small minority.

Examiner's favourite: 'Mussolini seized power in 1922.' He did not. He was given it, by a king who feared civil war, army disloyalty and the left more than he feared Fascism. The interesting question is not how the Fascists marched, but why the elites let them in.

1923–1929 · building the dictatorship

Matteotti, the fascistissime and the Church

Dictatorship was built in stages over roughly three years.

  • The Acerbo Law (1923) awarded two-thirds of the seats to the party winning the largest share of the vote. The Fascists duly won the violent election of April 1924.
  • The Matteotti crisis (June 1924): the socialist deputy Giacomo Matteotti, who had denounced Fascist violence in parliament, was abducted and murdered. Outrage nearly destroyed Mussolini. The opposition withdrew from parliament — the Aventine Secession — which removed the only body that could have censured him. In January 1925 Mussolini accepted responsibility in parliament and dared his enemies to act. They did not.
  • The leggi fascistissime (1925–26): other parties banned, a free press abolished, elected local government replaced by appointed podestà, the secret police (OVRA) and the Special Tribunal established.
  • The Lateran Pacts (11 February 1929): Mussolini settled the sixty-year quarrel with the papacy — recognition of Vatican City, Catholicism as the state religion, compensation. It was his single greatest domestic coup, and it made him respectable to millions of Catholic Italians.

How totalitarian was it? Mussolini never controlled the monarchy, the army, the Church or big business as Hitler controlled their German equivalents. The regime's own slogan of a total state outran the reality. Keep that distinction: ambition is not achievement.

The regime · policy and reality

The battles, the corporate state, and empire

Fascist policy was announced as a series of 'battles'. Judging it means separating propaganda from outcome.

  • The Battle for Grain raised wheat production — by diverting land from more suitable crops such as olives and vines, damaging the agricultural economy overall.
  • The Battle for the Lira (1927) revalued the currency at 'quota 90' for reasons of prestige. It made exports uncompetitive and forced wage cuts.
  • The Battle for Births aimed to raise the population for military strength; the birth rate did not respond as intended.
  • The corporate state claimed to abolish class conflict by fusing employers and workers in state-run corporations. In practice independent unions were destroyed and employers did well.
  • Empire: the invasion of Abyssinia (October 1935) — with poison gas and aerial bombing — brought League sanctions and huge domestic popularity. It also pushed Mussolini towards Hitler: the Rome–Berlin Axis (1936), intervention in Spain, the racial laws of 1938 and the Pact of Steel (May 1939).
  • The fall: Italy entered the war in June 1940 hopelessly unprepared; defeats followed in Greece, North Africa and the Mediterranean. After the Allies landed in Sicily (July 1943), the Fascist Grand Council voted against Mussolini on 24–25 July 1943 and the King dismissed and arrested him. The regime ended as it had begun — by royal decision.

Interpretations · a bitterly contested field

De Felice, Mack Smith, Gentile, Bosworth

Italian Fascism is argued over more fiercely than almost any topic on the specification — partly because the argument is still political in Italy.

Renzo De Felice, author of a vast multi-volume biography of Mussolini, distinguished the Fascist movement from the Fascist regime, and controversially argued that the regime enjoyed genuine consensus among Italians in the 1930s, especially after Abyssinia. His critics accused him of normalising the dictatorship; his defenders point to the archival scale of the work.
Denis Mack Smith took the opposite line: Mussolini as a bluffer and improviser, a propagandist of genius and a statesman of little substance, whose regime was hollow and whose foreign policy was vanity. The corrective to De Felice — and, some argue, an over-correction.
Emilio Gentile reframed Fascism as a political religion: the sacralisation of politics through myth, ritual, liturgy and the cult of the Duce. On this view its seriousness lies not in policy outcomes but in its attempt to remake the Italian as a person.
R.J.B. Bosworth (Mussolini, 2002) is sceptical of totalitarian claims made both by the regime and about it, stressing how much of old Italy — Church, monarchy, family, the South — survived Fascism untouched.

The evaluative move: notice that these historians are often answering different questions. De Felice asks what Italians believed; Mack Smith asks whether the regime worked; Gentile asks what Fascism was for. Saying so, and then judging on evidence, is Level 5.

Exam technique · Unit Group 2

The non-British period study

OCR Unit Group 2: 1 hour, 30 marks, 15% of the A-level — assessed by essay. No sources; nothing but your knowledge and your argument.

  • Refuse the regime's own version of events. Almost every myth in this unit — the heroic March on Rome, the totalitarian state, the drained marshes, the punctual trains — was manufactured by Fascist propaganda and then repeated by careless textbooks. The examiner is delighted by a student who says so, and evidences it.
  • Separate rhetoric, policy and outcome. The Battle for Grain succeeded in raising wheat output and damaged Italian agriculture. Both are true; a good answer says both.
  • Every question is a judgement. Decide, structure by reasons, and give the counter-case its strongest form before defeating it.

Key question · was Italy totalitarian?

Comparing Mussolini and Hitler

Mussolini coined the word totalitario. Judging whether the regime deserved it is a set-piece essay.

  • What it achieved: a one-party state, a secret police, the destruction of free trade unions and a free press, the cult of the Duce, and the fascistisation of youth organisations and leisure.
  • What it never achieved: the monarchy survived and retained the constitutional power to dismiss him — and used it in 1943. The army kept its own oath and its own culture. The Church, after the Lateran Pacts (1929), held a protected sphere of influence over education and family life that no totalitarian state would tolerate. Big business retained real autonomy.
  • The comparison: the Nazi regime destroyed or absorbed all four of those centres of power. Italian Fascism negotiated with them. The repression was also on a wholly different scale — the Special Tribunal's death sentences numbered in the dozens, not the millions.

The correct answer is a distinction, not a verdict: Fascist Italy was totalitarian in aspiration and authoritarian in practice. Say that, evidence it with the monarchy and the Church, and add that this is precisely why the regime could be ended by a royal decree in July 1943 — while the Third Reich had to be destroyed by armies.
