Mission 1

What "rational" means

  • Economics models choices as rational: agents weigh benefits against costs and pick the option that best meets their objective.
  • Consumers aim to maximise utility (satisfaction).
  • Firms aim to maximise profit (total revenue − total cost).
Mission 1

Total and marginal utility

  • Total utility (TU): the whole satisfaction from all units consumed.
  • Marginal utility (MU): the change in TU from one more unit. MU = ΔTU ÷ ΔQ.

  • TU still rises while MU is positive. TU is at its maximum where MU = 0.

Mission 1

From diminishing MU to the demand curve

  • A consumer buys one more unit only if its extra satisfaction is worth at least the price.
  • MU falls with each unit, so the most the consumer will pay for each extra unit falls too.
  • To persuade them to buy more, the price must be lower.
  • So quantity demanded rises as price falls: the demand curve slopes downward.
  • Say: "a fall in price causes an extension of demand" (a movement along D) — not: "demand increases".

Mission 1

Water and diamonds

  • The paradox of value: water is vital, diamonds are not, yet diamonds cost far more.
  • Water: plentiful. People already use a lot, so the MU of one more litre is tiny.
  • Diamonds: scarce. Few are owned, so the MU of one more diamond is high.
  • Price reflects marginal utility (what the next unit is worth), not total utility.
  • TU from water is enormous. MU of the last litre is small.
Mission 1Evaluate

How useful is the rational model?

  • Utility cannot be measured directly. "Utils" are a teaching device; economists infer preferences from choices.
  • It depends on information: real consumers rarely know every option and price.
  • It depends on effort and stakes: people use habits and shortcuts for small, frequent buys.
  • The model still predicts the direction of many responses well, e.g. buying less when price rises.
  • More on the limits: Mission 2.

Mission 2

The rational benchmark

  • Standard theory assumes consumers:
    • are rational: they weigh costs and benefits consistently;
    • maximise utility;
    • have perfect information;
    • are self-interested.
  • Behavioural economics uses psychology to test these assumptions against how people really choose.
  • Where people depart from the benchmark in predictable ways, economists call it a bias.
Mission 2

Bounded rationality

  • Bounded rationality: people try to choose well, but are limited by the information they have, the time they have and the brainpower they can spare.
  • So they often satisfice: choose an option that is "good enough" rather than the best.
  • Say: "bounded rationality" (limited ability to optimise) — not: "irrational" (no reasoning at all).
Mission 2

Rules of thumb and habit

  • A rule of thumb (heuristic): a mental shortcut that saves time.
    • "Buy the brand I bought last time."
    • "Pick the second-cheapest item on the menu."
  • Shortcuts are often sensible, but they can lead to choices a fully informed optimiser would not make.
  • Habits: repeated choices made with little thought, e.g. the same coffee every morning. A price rise may barely change behaviour in the short run.
Mission 2

Anchoring

  • Anchoring: judgements are pulled towards an initial number (the anchor), even an irrelevant one.
  • Classic experiment (Tversky and Kahneman, 1974): people saw a "random" number, then estimated the percentage of African countries in the United Nations.
    • Group shown 10: median estimate 25%.
    • Group shown 65: median estimate 45%.
  • In shops: "Was 200, now 120" makes 120 look cheap.
Mission 2

Availability

  • Availability bias: people judge how likely an event is by how easily examples come to mind.
  • Vivid, recent or heavily reported events seem more likely than they are.
  • Example: after a widely reported plane crash, some travellers switch to driving, which is riskier per kilometre travelled.
  • Insurance demand often rises just after a disaster nearby, then fades.
Quick check
?A sofa shop displays a "Was 1,500" label above each sofa, crossed out, with the sale price of 900 underneath. Shoppers rate 900 as a bargain, even though similar sofas elsewhere cost 850. Which bias is the shop using?
Mission 2

Bounded self-control

  • Bounded self-control: people make plans for their future selves, then fail to stick to them.
  • Present benefits and costs loom larger than future ones (present bias).
  • Evidence (DellaVigna and Malmendier, 2006, US gyms):
    • Members on monthly contracts paid over $70 a month.
    • They went on average 4.3 times a month.
    • Cost per visit: more than $17, though a 10-visit pass cost $10 per visit.
  • When signing up, people overestimate how often their future self will go.
  • Other examples: saving too little for retirement; snacking against a diet plan.
Mission 2

Others' behaviour and social norms

  • Social norms: unwritten rules about what people around us do and approve of.
  • People copy others (herd behaviour): a long queue outside a restaurant signals "it must be good".
  • Fashion, smartphone choices and saving behaviour are all shaped by peers.
  • Demand then depends on what others buy, not only on price, income and own tastes.
Mission 2

Bounded selfishness: altruism and fairness

  • Bounded selfishness: people care about others, not only themselves.
  • Altruism: giving to charity, donating blood, tipping in a café you will never revisit.
  • Fairness: the ultimatum game.
    • Player 1 splits a sum of money; Player 2 accepts (both get paid) or rejects (both get nothing).
    • A purely self-interested Player 2 accepts any amount above zero.
    • In experiments, offers are typically 40–50% of the sum, and offers under 20% are often rejected.
  • Rejecting money to punish unfairness is not self-interested in the narrow sense.
Quick check
?In an ultimatum game, Player 1 splits 100 and offers Player 2 just 5. Player 2 rejects, so both get nothing. What does the standard rational, self-interested model predict, and what does the rejection suggest?
Mission 2Evaluate

Should economists drop rationality?

  • It depends on stakes and repetition: with large stakes, practice and feedback, people learn and biases shrink.
  • It depends on market forces: firms that misprice lose to rivals, so some biases are offset at market level. Others are exploited (e.g. gym contracts).
  • It depends on predictability: biases that are systematic (anchoring, present bias) can be built into models and policy.
  • Policy uses of these biases: a later lesson.
  • Some behavioural results have been hard to replicate; others, such as anchoring and present bias, are well established.
  • The rational model remains a useful benchmark: it shows what to compare real behaviour against.
Quick check
?A supermarket offers "3 for the price of 2" on cereal boxes that cost 3 each. What percentage discount does a shopper who buys 3 boxes get on the usual price?

Mission 3

The budget line

  • Two goods, X and Y. A fixed money income, fixed prices.
  • Budget line: all combinations of X and Y the consumer can just afford, spending all income.
  • Illustrative data: income 60; price of X = 4; price of Y = 6.
  • All on X: 60 ÷ 4 = 15 units. All on Y: 60 ÷ 6 = 10 units.
  • Join the two intercepts: a straight line. Check (6, 6): 6 × 4 + 6 × 6 = 60. ✓
  • Slope = −(price of X ÷ price of Y) = −4/6 = −2/3: to get one more X, give up 2/3 of a unit of Y.
  • Inside the line: affordable, income left over. Outside: unaffordable.
Mission 3

Shifting the budget line

  • (1) Income rises 60 → 72: both intercepts rise (X 18, Y 12). Parallel shift outwards; slope unchanged.
  • (2) Price of X rises 4 → 5: X intercept falls to 12; Y intercept stays 10. The line pivots inwards on the Y axis.
  • (3) Price of X falls: the line pivots outwards on the Y axis.
  • Income falls → parallel shift inwards. Both prices change by the same % → parallel shift (like an income change).
  • Income and both prices change by the same % → no change in the budget line.
  • Say: "a price change pivots the budget line" — not: "it shifts it parallel".
Quick check
?Income is 60, the price of X is 4 and the price of Y is 6. The price of X falls to 3. What are the new intercepts of the budget line?
Mission 3

Indifference curves

  • Indifference curve (IC): all combinations of two goods that give the consumer the same satisfaction.
  • The consumer is indifferent between any two points on one curve.
  • Assumptions: more is preferred to less; preferences are consistent (rational); goods are divisible.
  • Slopes down: to keep satisfaction the same, more X must mean less Y.
  • Convex to the origin: the more X you already have, the less Y you will give up for one more X. This is a diminishing marginal rate of substitution (MRS).
  • Higher IC = more satisfaction. A set of ICs is an indifference map.
  • ICs never cross: one point would then give two different levels of satisfaction.
  • Only the ranking matters (ordinal). No need to measure utility in units, unlike marginal utility theory (see Mission 1).
Mission 3

The best affordable choice

  • The consumer wants the highest IC they can reach on their budget line.
  • That is where the budget line is a tangent to an IC: point W.
  • At W: slope of IC = slope of budget line, so MRS = price of X ÷ price of Y.
  • Points where the budget line cuts a lower IC: a higher IC is still reachable by moving along the line.
  • Higher ICs beyond the line: preferred, but unaffordable.
Quick check

Mission 3

Splitting the price effect

  • Price effect: the total change in quantity when a price changes. It has two parts.
  • Substitution effect: X is now relatively cheaper, so the consumer swaps Y for X, holding satisfaction constant.
  • Income effect: the price fall raises real income, so the consumer can reach a higher IC.
  • Price of X falls.
  • (1) Start at A: BL1 tangent to IC1.
  • (2) BL2 pivots outwards; new choice C on higher IC2.
  • (3) Draw an imaginary budget line parallel to BL2 but tangent to the old IC1, at B. It removes the gain in real income.
  • (4) A → B = substitution effect (always more X when X gets cheaper). B → C = income effect.
  • Price effect = substitution effect + income effect.

  • Takeaway: the substitution effect always points the same way; the income effect decides the good type.

Normal, inferior and Giffen goods

  • Price of X falls:

  • A Giffen good is an inferior good with an income effect so strong that it outweighs the substitution effect. Its demand curve slopes upwards.
  • Likely when the good is a staple that takes a large share of a poor household's budget, with few substitutes.
  • Real case: in 2006, a study gave poor households in Hunan, China, vouchers that cut the price of rice for five months. Many bought less rice: cheaper rice freed income for meat, a dearer food they preferred.
  • The very poorest, who relied on rice for almost all their calories, did not: like normal buyers, they bought more when it was cheaper.
  • The researchers called this the first real-world evidence of upward-sloping demand. Evidence for wheat in Gansu was weaker.
  • Say: "a Giffen good is a strongly inferior necessity" — not: "a luxury people buy more of because it is expensive" (that is a Veblen or status good, a different idea).

Mission 3Evaluate

Limits of indifference curves

  • Preferences are not observable. No one can draw a real person's IC map; the curves are assumed, not measured.
  • Only two goods. Real choices involve thousands. (Fix: let Y stand for "all other goods".)
  • Rational, consistent, fixed tastes are assumed. Habit, impulse, framing and advertising break these (see Mission 2).
  • Divisible goods and perfect information are assumed; many choices are lumpy (a car, a holiday) and uncertain.
  • Giffen goods are rare and hard to show with real data.
  • But: the model needs only a ranking, not measured utility, and it separates income and substitution effects clearly.
  • It depends on: how stable tastes are, how many goods matter, and how well informed the consumer is.
Mission check

Mission 4

What a free market gets right

  • Each point on the demand curve shows the marginal benefit (MB) of one more unit to buyers.
  • Each point on the supply curve shows the marginal cost (MC) of one more unit to sellers.
  • At competitive equilibrium, P = MB = MC.
  • Consumer surplus: what buyers would pay minus what they do pay.
  • Producer surplus: what sellers receive minus the least they would accept.
  • Consumer + producer surplus = community surplus.
  • If there is no market failure, equilibrium makes this total as large as possible: allocative efficiency.
Mission 4

Market equilibrium vs the social optimum

  • Buyers and sellers weigh only their private benefits and costs: market equilibrium is where MPB = MPC.
  • Society counts all benefits and costs: the social optimum is where MSB = MSC.
  • Allocative efficiency: resources make the goods society values most; the value of the last unit equals its full cost, MSB = MSC (for a single firm: P = MC).
  • No outside effects: MPB = MSB and MPC = MSC, so the market reaches the social optimum.
  • Outside effects: the market equilibrium and the social optimum are at different quantities.
  • Say: "the market equilibrium is not the socially optimum position" — not: "the market is not in equilibrium". A market can be in equilibrium and still be inefficient.
Mission 4

Other kinds of efficiency

  • Productive efficiency: output made at the lowest possible average cost (the bottom of the AC curve); no resources wasted.
  • Allocative efficiency: P = MC; society gets the mix of goods it values most.
  • Dynamic efficiency: efficiency over time, from investment and innovation that cut costs or create better products.
  • Pareto optimality: no one can be made better off without making someone else worse off.
  • A Pareto optimal economy is allocatively and productively efficient. Market failure means it is not Pareto optimal: some change could help one person and harm no one.

Mission 4

Worked example: measuring the welfare loss

  • Illustrative data: MSB = 120 − Q; MSC = 20 + Q (price in $, quantity in units per week).
  • Optimum: 120 − Q = 20 + Q, so Q = 50, value 70.
  • Output is 65 instead. At Q = 65: MSC = 20 + 65 = 85; MSB = 120 − 65 = 55.
  • Each extra unit from 50 to 65 costs society more than it is worth.
  • Welfare loss = triangle between MSC and MSB, from Q = 50 to 65.
  • Area = ½ × base × height = ½ × (65 − 50) × (85 − 55) = ½ × 15 × 30 = 225.
  • Total surplus at the optimum = ½ × 50 × (120 − 20) = 2,500. At 65 it falls to 2,500 − 225 = 2,275.

Mission 4

What market failure means

  • Market failure: the price mechanism fails to allocate resources efficiently, so the market outcome is not the social optimum.
  • The result is too much or too little of a good, and a welfare loss.
  • Complete market failure: no market exists at all: a missing market (e.g. street lighting).
  • Partial market failure: a market exists, but at the wrong price or quantity (e.g. too much tobacco, too little vaccination).

Mission 4

Private, external, social

  • Externality: a cost or benefit to a third party, not paid for or received through the market.
  • Social cost = private cost + external cost. At the margin: MSC = MPC + MEC.
  • Social benefit = private benefit + external benefit. At the margin: MSB = MPB + MEB.
  • External cost from production (e.g. pollution): MSC above MPC, so the market overproduces.
  • External benefit from consumption (e.g. vaccination): MSB above MPB, so the market underproduces.
  • Either way: a welfare (deadweight) loss triangle that points to the social optimum.

Mission 5

Two questions about waste

  • Efficiency: getting the most out of scarce resources.
  • Ask two separate questions:
    • How is it made? At the lowest possible cost per unit? → productive efficiency
    • What is made, and how much? The goods society values most? → allocative efficiency
  • One can hold without the other.
  • Both are static efficiency: efficiency at a point in time, with today's technology.
  • Dynamic efficiency asks a third question: are costs and products improving over time?
Mission 5

Productive efficiency

  • Productive efficiency: output where average cost (AC) is at its minimum.
  • At that output, MC = AC: the MC curve cuts the AC curve at its lowest point.
  • Why? If the next unit costs less than the average (MC < AC), it pulls AC down. If it costs more (MC > AC), it pushes AC up.
  • Say: "productively efficient at minimum AC" — not: "at the lowest total cost" (that is zero output).

Mission 5

Allocative efficiency

  • Allocative efficiency: resources make the goods and services society values most, in the right amounts.
  • Condition: P = MC.
    • Price shows what buyers will pay for the last unit: its marginal benefit (MB) to them.
    • MC shows the cost of the resources used for it: the value of what else they could have made.
  • P > MC: buyers value one more unit above its cost → too little is made.
  • P < MC: the last unit costs more than it is worth to buyers → too much is made.
  • Say: "allocatively efficient where P = MC" — not: "where P = AC" (that is normal profit).

Mission 5

Pareto efficiency

  • Pareto efficiency (Pareto optimality): no one can be made better off without making someone else worse off.
  • It needs both: productive efficiency (no waste in how goods are made) and allocative efficiency (the right goods are made).
  • A Pareto improvement: someone gains and no one loses.
  • Pareto efficiency says nothing about fairness: a very unequal outcome can still be Pareto efficient.
Mission 5

Dynamic efficiency

  • Dynamic efficiency: efficiency over time, through innovation.
    • Process innovation: new methods lower costs → the whole AC curve shifts down.
    • Product innovation: new and better products.
  • It needs investment, R&D and new technology.
  • Funding: retained supernormal profit is a major source.
  • Incentive: firms innovate to win or protect profit.
Mission 5

X-inefficiency

  • X-inefficiency: a firm produces at a cost above the lowest possible cost for its output.
  • On the diagram: a point above the AC curve, not on it.
  • Causes: organisational slack, overstaffing, overpaying suppliers, managers not pushed to cut costs.
  • Most likely where competition is weak, e.g. a protected monopoly.
  • Competition (or the threat of entry) removes it: high-cost firms lose sales.
  • Say: "X-inefficient" only for costs above the AC curve — not: for every firm away from minimum AC. A point on the curve, away from its minimum, is productively inefficient but not X-inefficient.

Mission 5

Case: patents

  • A patent gives an inventor the sole right to sell an invention for a set time.
  • WTO members must offer patent protection for at least 20 years from the filing date.
  • Static cost: a monopoly → P > MC → allocatively inefficient.
  • Dynamic gain: the expected profit pays for costly R&D (e.g. new medicines).
  • A deliberate trade: less static efficiency now for more dynamic efficiency later.
Mission 5Evaluate

Static or dynamic: which matters more?

  • It depends on whether profit is reinvested: supernormal profit may fund R&D, or be paid out to owners.
  • It depends on competitive pressure: a protected monopoly may stop innovating and let costs drift up.
  • It depends on economies of scale: one large firm's minimum AC may be far below that of many small firms.
  • It depends on time: static losses happen now; dynamic gains, if any, come later and are uncertain.

Mission 6

The cost nobody pays for

  • A firm pays for labour, materials, energy: its private costs.
  • Its production may also harm third parties, who are outside the trade: polluted air and rivers, noise, carbon emissions.
  • That harm is an external cost. Nobody pays the firm's victims through the market.
  • Social cost = private cost + external cost.
  • At the margin: MSC = MPC + MEC (marginal external cost).
  • A negative production externality = an external cost created by producing a good.
  • Examples: a chemical plant's waste in a river harms fishing downstream; an airport's noise lowers the value of nearby homes; a coal power station's emissions harm health.
Mission 6

Worked example: adding up social cost

  • Illustrative data: a paper mill dumps waste in a river. Per tonne of paper:
  • Marginal private cost (wood, labour, energy) = 300.
  • Marginal external cost (lost fishing, water cleaning downstream) = 80.
  • MSC = MPC + MEC = 300 + 80 = 380.
  • Buyers value the last tonne at 360 (MPB). No external benefit, so MSB = 360.
  • The mill makes the tonne: to it, 360 > 300. To society: 360 − 380 = −20. Society loses 20 on that tonne.
  • If all 1,000 tonnes cost the same: private cost 300,000; external cost 80,000; social cost 380,000.
  • The same rule for benefits: social benefit = private benefit + external benefit. Here external benefit = 0.

Mission 6

The diagram

  • Step 1: the market. Supply = MPC; demand = MPB = MSB (no external benefit). Market equilibrium at Qm, Pm.
  • Step 2: add the external cost. MSC lies above MPC; the vertical gap = MEC.
  • Step 3: the social optimum is where MSB = MSC: Q* (less), P* (higher).
  • Firms respond to private costs only, so the market produces Qm > Q*: overproduction.
  • The price Pm is too low: it does not include the external cost.
  • Say: "the market overproduces compared with the social optimum" — not: "the market is in disequilibrium".
Mission 6

The welfare loss

  • For each unit from Q* to Qm: MSC > MSB. It costs society more than it is worth.
  • Adding up these losses gives a triangle:
  • corners: the social optimum (MSB = MSC); MSC at Qm; MSB at Qm.
  • The triangle points towards the social optimum.
  • Called the welfare loss.
  • Say: "welfare loss = the triangle between MSC and MSB, from Q* to Qm" — not: "the whole area between MSC and MPC". That bigger area is the external cost of all output, most of which is still worth bearing.
Mission 6Graph lab

Turn up the pollution

Illustrative data: MPB = MSB = 100 − Q; MPC = 20 + Q. The marginal external cost (MEC) is the same for every unit.

  • Takeaway: a bigger MEC does not change what the market produces; it lowers the social optimum and widens the welfare loss.
Mission 6

Worked example: calculating the welfare loss

  • Illustrative data: MPB = MSB = 100 − Q; MPC = 20 + Q; MEC = 20 per unit, so MSC = 40 + Q.
  • Market: 100 − Q = 20 + Q, so Qm = 40, Pm = 60.
  • Social optimum: 100 − Q = 40 + Q, so Q* = 30, P* = 70.
  • At Qm = 40: MSC = 80; MSB = 60. Height = 80 − 60 = 20 (= MEC).
  • Base = Qm − Q* = 40 − 30 = 10.
  • Welfare loss = ½ × 10 × 20 = 100.
  • Compare: total external cost at Qm = 20 × 40 = 800. The welfare loss is only 100: most of the external cost (20 × 30 = 600) falls on units that are still worth producing.

Mission 6

Putting a number on the external cost

  • Carbon dioxide is a global external cost: emissions anywhere add to climate damage everywhere.
  • The social cost of carbon estimates the damage from one more tonne of CO₂, in today's money.
  • Two US government estimates for emissions in 2020: $51 per tonne (2021) and $190 per tonne (2023).
  • The gap comes mostly from assumptions, above all how much weight to give damage to people in the future.
  • So the MEC used for policy depends on value judgements, not only on science.
Mission 6Evaluate

How big is the problem?

  • It depends on measuring MEC. Health, nature and future harm have no market price, so estimates vary widely.
  • It depends on elasticity. For a given MEC, the more price elastic demand and supply are, the bigger the overproduction (Qm − Q*) and the welfare loss.
  • It depends on who and where. Many small polluters, or harm across borders, make the external cost hard to trace and charge.
  • It depends on time. Some damage builds up over years (carbon, soil), so today's MEC may understate the harm.
  • Ways to correct it (taxes, regulation, permits, property rights) are in an earlier lesson.

Mission 7

Private and social costs and benefits

  • A firm counts its private costs and benefits: what it pays and what it earns.
  • Society also counts external costs and benefits: effects on third parties.
  • Social cost = private cost + external cost (SC = PC + EC).
  • Social benefit = private benefit + external benefit (SB = PB + EB).
  • A new road: building costs (private); noise for nearby homes (external cost); faster journeys and fewer crashes (benefits).
  • A project can lose money for its builder yet still be worth it to society, or the other way round.
Mission 7

The method of cost-benefit analysis

  • Cost-benefit analysis (CBA): comparing all the social costs and social benefits of a project or policy, valued in money, to decide whether to go ahead.
  • Steps:
    • Identify every cost and benefit: private and external, now and in future.
    • Value each in money. Where there is no market price, estimate one (a shadow price).
    • Compare: net social benefit = social benefits − social costs.
    • Decide: go ahead if social benefits exceed social costs. Between options, pick the highest net social benefit.
    • Test the result: does the decision change if an estimate is wrong?
  • Benefits arriving years from now count for less than benefits today. You will not be asked to calculate this.
  • Say: "net social benefit" — not: "profit". A CBA can approve a project that makes a loss.
Mission 7

  • Illustrative data, in millions, all years added together.

Mission 7

Valuing things that have no price

  • Clean air, quiet and travel time are not sold in shops. Economists estimate their value in money:
  • Market prices: use a related price, e.g. the cost of cleaning up a river, or of treating an illness.
  • Revealed preference: watch what people actually pay.
    • Similar houses sell for less under a flight path: the gap values the noise.
    • Workers in riskier jobs are paid more: the extra pay values the risk.
  • Stated preference: ask people in a survey what they would be willing to pay.
  • Value of a statistical life (illustrative data): 100,000 people each pay 100 to cut their risk of dying this year by 1 in 100,000.
    • Expected deaths fall by 1.
    • Total paid = 100,000 × 100 = 10 million: the value of one statistical life.
  • It is not the value of any one named person's life.

Mission 7

Why externalities are hard to measure

  • Who is affected? Harm can spread far, across borders and to future generations.
  • How much harm? Scientists may not know the effect of a pollutant for years.
  • What money value? Different methods give different numbers for the same harm.
  • Survey problems: people may overstate what they would pay, or refuse to put a price on nature.
  • So a tax or subsidy set equal to the "external cost" rests on an estimate.
Mission 7Graph lab

Getting the tax wrong

Illustrative data: the true external cost is 20 per unit. The government sets a tax per unit equal to its estimate.

  • Takeaway: the tax corrects the market only if it matches the true external cost, so measurement matters.
Mission 7

How much clean-up is efficient?

  • Policymakers rarely choose "all or nothing". They choose how much.
  • For each extra unit of clean-up compare:
    • marginal social benefit (MSB): the extra harm avoided
    • marginal social cost (MSC): the extra cost of cleaning up
  • Keep going while MSB ≥ MSC. Stop when the next unit costs more than it is worth.
  • At MSB = MSC, net social benefit is as large as it can be: there is no deadweight loss.
  • Zero pollution is usually not efficient: the last units are very costly to remove.
  • Say: "choose the quantity where MSB = MSC" — not: "where total benefit is largest".

Mission 7

CBA in use

  • Transport: governments value time saved, accidents avoided, noise and emissions before building roads and railways.
  • Health: some health services compare a treatment's cost with the quality-adjusted life years (QALYs) it adds.
  • Flood defence: a 1936 US law allowed federal flood projects if benefits "to whomsoever they may accrue" exceed the estimated costs.
  • Regulation: the US Environmental Protection Agency recommends a value of a statistical life of US$7.4 million (in 2006 dollars), updated to the year of each analysis, when it weighs rules that cut deaths from pollution.
Mission 7Evaluate

How far can we trust a CBA?

It depends on:

  • The estimates: the money values put on life, time and nature can differ widely. Small changes can flip the decision.
  • Who wins and who loses: CBA adds up gains and losses. A project can pass while one group (e.g. nearby residents) loses heavily and is not compensated.
  • Time and uncertainty: long-lived projects and climate harms reach far into the future, and forecasts can be optimistic.
  • Who does the analysis: interest groups and politicians may push for favourable numbers.
  • Still, a CBA makes assumptions visible, so they can be challenged.
Mission check

CBA counts everyone's costs and benefits, so a profitable project can fail and a loss-making one can pass.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Marginal utility falls as consumption rises; this explains a downward-sloping demand curve.
  • Rational choice: keep going while MB ≥ MC; the best choice is where MB = MC.
  • Utility is maximised when MU/P is equal across goods and all income is spent.

Mission 2

  • Bounded rationality, bounded self-control and bounded selfishness are the three main limits to the rational model.
  • Biases such as rules of thumb, anchoring and availability are predictable departures from rational choice.

Mission 3

  • The budget line shows what is affordable (income shifts it parallel, a price change pivots it); ICs show preferences; the best choice is at the tangency.
  • Price effect = substitution effect (always towards the cheaper good) + income effect (normal: same way; inferior: opposite; Giffen: opposite and larger).
  • The model is clear but assumes observable, rational, stable preferences over two goods.

Mission 4

  • At the social optimum MSB = MSC and total surplus is as large as possible: allocative efficiency.
  • Market failure (for example externalities and public goods) leaves output above or below that point, causing a welfare loss.

Mission 5

  • Productive efficiency: minimum AC (MC = AC).
  • Allocative efficiency: P = MC.
  • Dynamic efficiency: costs and products improve over time through R&D and investment.
  • X-inefficiency: costs above the AC curve, often where competition is weak.

Mission 6

  • A negative production externality makes MSC = MPC + MEC lie above MPC.
  • The market produces where MPB = MPC: more than the social optimum where MSB = MSC.
  • The welfare loss is the triangle between MSC and MSB from Q* to Qm, pointing to the social optimum.

Mission 7

  • Social cost = private cost + external cost; social benefit = private benefit + external benefit.
  • CBA: identify, value, compare, decide. Go ahead if social benefits exceed social costs; choose the quantity where MSB = MSC.
  • Values for things with no price come from related markets, revealed preference and surveys, so they are estimates.
