Changing Places

Section 3.2.2 of AQA A-level Geography — and the most conceptual topic on the course. It asks what a place actually is, how places acquire meaning, who gets to decide that meaning, and how flows from far outside a place reshape it from within.

Where this sits in AQA 7037. This topic is COMPULSORY (as are Water and carbon cycles and Global systems and global governance). You then choose ONE of Hot desert / Coastal / Glacial systems and landscapes; ONE of Hazards / Ecosystems under stress; and ONE of Contemporary urban environments / Population and the environment / Resource security.

Work through each screen, answer the questions (some analytical, two calculations) and collect ⭐ stars. Press Start when you're ready.

The concept of place

Location, locale, sense of place

Geographers distinguish space — abstract, measurable, interchangeable — from place — space that has been given meaning by the people who use it. The classic three-part definition (after Agnew) is the backbone of this whole unit:

  • Location — the where. Absolute (coordinates, grid reference) or relative (near the coast, on the main line to London). It is the objective, mappable fact of a place.
  • Locale — the setting for social relations. The material stage on which everyday life is performed: the pubs, mosques, workplaces, school gates and street corners, and the practices and behaviours they shape. Locale is why a courtroom and a nightclub produce different behaviour from the same people.
  • Sense of place — the subjective, emotional attachment a person or group holds towards a place. It is felt, not measured, and it differs between individuals: the same street is home, workplace, memory or hostile territory depending on who is standing in it.

Two more terms complete the vocabulary. Placelessness (Relph) describes landscapes so standardised that they could be anywhere — the identikit retail park, the airport concourse — and clone town is its British shorthand. Topophilia (Tuan) is the affective bond between a person and a place; its opposite, topophobia, is fear or aversion.

Examiner's eye: the single most common weak answer describes a place instead of conceptualising it. "Bristol is a city in the south-west with a population of about half a million" is location only. "Bristol's docks are a locale whose meaning has been actively contested — a source of civic pride, and a site of the transatlantic slave trade whose commemoration divides the city" is A-level.

Categories of place

Near and far · experienced and media

AQA asks you to distinguish how we come to know places — because that determines how accurate, and how manipulable, our knowledge of them is.

  • Near places vs far places. Crucially, this is not just physical distance. Nearness is also felt: a place can be geographically distant yet emotionally near (a family's ancestral village, thousands of miles away, known intimately through stories and visits), while a district two miles down the road can feel utterly foreign. This is the difference between absolute and relative/perceived distance.
  • Experienced places — places you have actually been, whose meaning you have built out of first-hand encounter, over time, with your own body and senses.
  • Media places — places you have never been, and whose meaning has been assembled entirely from representations: news footage, film, novels, tourist advertising, social media. Your entire sense of them is second-hand — and therefore constructed by whoever produced those representations.

Place as a social construct. The deep claim of this unit is that place-meaning is not a natural property of the ground. It is made — by people, through practice and representation — and it can therefore be re-made, contested, and imposed. Places do not have one true meaning waiting to be discovered; they have competing meanings, produced by groups with unequal power to make theirs stick.

Why it matters: if most people's knowledge of most places is a media place, then whoever controls the representation controls the place — for investors deciding where to put money, for governments deciding where to intervene, and for residents deciding whether to feel proud or ashamed of where they live.

Insight · what shapes a place

Endogenous and exogenous factors

This is the analytical engine of the topic. Every place is produced by the interaction of what is inside it and what flows into it.

  • Endogenous factors — the internal characteristics that originate within the place: location and site, topography and physical geography, land use, the built environment and its architecture, infrastructure, and the demographic and economic characteristics of its population.
  • Exogenous factors — a place's relationships with other places: the flows of people (migration, commuting, tourism), money (investment, remittances, government funding, TNC decisions), ideas (culture, policy, fashion, technology) and resources that arrive from outside and reshape the place from within.

The mark-winning move: never treat these as separate lists. Explain the interaction. A deep-water estuary (endogenous) only became a great port because of colonial trade routes (exogenous); when those routes shifted to container terminals elsewhere, the same estuary became a liability. The physical geography did not change — its relationship to elsewhere did.

Past and present connections

The landscape as a palimpsest

Places carry their history in their fabric. A palimpsest was a manuscript scraped clean and written over, with the earlier text still faintly visible — and it is the perfect metaphor for a landscape in which every past connection has left a trace that the present has only partly erased.

  • Relict features — the physical residue of past economic and social relationships: a disused dock basin, pit-head gear, a mill chimney on a skyline, a canal built to move coal that no longer exists, a Victorian workhouse now converted into flats.
  • Traces in the street pattern — medieval burgage plots still visible in the depth of shop frontages; the curve of a road following a filled-in river; the grid of a planned company town.
  • Traces in the population — communities established by past labour migration, and the places of worship, shops, festivals and languages that came with them, long outlasting the industry that first recruited them.
  • Traces in meaning — and this is the sharpest point: past connections shape present identity and present conflict. Wealth built on empire and slavery is written into civic architecture, street names and museum collections, and the argument over what to do with those traces is itself a live contest over what the place means.

Use this in essays: a relict feature is never just a ruin. It is evidence of a former exogenous relationship. Read the landscape backwards: this warehouse implies a trade route; this terrace implies a workforce; this branch line implies a market that no longer exists. That reading is exactly the analytical skill AQA is testing.

Changing flows · changing character

How shifting flows change a place

Places change when the flows running through them change. AQA wants the mechanism traced from the flow, through the demography and economy, to the cultural character and meaning of the place.

  • Investment arrives → new employment in different sectors → in-migration of a different demographic (often younger, more qualified) → rising house prices → displacement of existing residents who can no longer afford to stay → new shops, bars and services aimed at the incomers → the place looks and feels different, and its meaning to the original community may become one of loss. This is gentrification, and its geography is a conflict, not a transformation everyone welcomes.
  • Investment leaves → job losses concentrate in one sector → out-migration of the young and mobile → an ageing, less affluent residual population → falling demand → shop vacancy, service withdrawal, environmental decline → a spiral of decline, and an external reputation that then deters the very investment that could reverse it.
  • People arrive → new languages, faiths, foods, festivals and businesses → visible change to the high street and the soundscape → cultural enrichment for some residents and perceived threat to others → the place's identity becomes actively contested.
  • Structural economic shift — from primary and secondary employment towards services, knowledge and the "experience economy" — has rewritten the character of whole regions, not just individual towns.

The A-level nuance: resist the words "improved" and "declined". Ask improved for whom? A regenerated waterfront may raise average incomes in a ward simply because poorer residents have been displaced out of it and richer ones have moved in — the statistics improve while the original community is worse off. Being alert to that is precisely what distinguishes a top-band answer.

%
Hint: (2100 ÷ 15000) × 100. In 2011 the figure was (2500 ÷ 12500) × 100 = 20% — so the ward has grown and got markedly younger.
Meaning and representation

How places are represented

A representation is any portrayal of a place — and every representation is a selection. Something is included, something is left out, and someone made that choice. AQA requires you to work with both families of source:

  • Quantitative sources — the census, indices of deprivation, house prices, employment and health statistics, crime figures, geospatial data. Strengths: comparable between places, reproducible, good for establishing pattern and trend. Limits: they measure what is easy to count, not what matters most; they are aggregated to arbitrary boundaries that can hide sharp internal contrasts (a "mixed" ward may be two segregated halves); they date quickly; and they say nothing whatsoever about how a place feels.
  • Qualitative sources — photographs, film and TV, art, literature and poetry, music, graffiti, oral history and interviews, blogs and social media, tourist board copy, estate agents' particulars. Strengths: they capture meaning, emotion, identity and lived experience — the very things statistics cannot reach. Limits: they are partial and positioned. They tell you as much about the producer as the place.

Representation is contested. A tourist board's film of a coastal town shows the harbour at golden hour and omits the boarded-up parade behind it. A television drama set on an estate makes crime the defining fact of a place where most people simply go to work. Neither is a straightforward lie — both are selections serving a purpose. The geographer's job is to ask: who produced this, for whom, and what does it want me to believe?

The insider/outsider distinction: residents' own representations of a place routinely conflict with external ones. Where those two diverge sharply, you have found something analytically valuable — and usually something the place's residents feel strongly about.

Who shapes place-meaning

Agencies, place-making and rebranding

Place-meaning is not left to chance. A range of agencies actively work to shape it — and they have very unequal power to do so:

  • Government (national and local) — planning policy, regeneration funding, transport investment, housing allocation, heritage designation. The state can literally rewrite the built environment, and decides which histories are commemorated.
  • Corporate bodies — developers, TNCs, retailers, estate agents and the tourist industry. They market place-images that support their investment, and their branding often precedes the change it describes: call it a "quarter", then build one.
  • Community groups — residents' associations, faith groups, campaigners, artists. They generally hold the least formal power, so they work through protest, occupation, local media, heritage claims and the everyday practice of making a place their own.

Rebranding, re-imaging and regeneration are the tools:

  • Rebranding — deliberately changing the image of a place (a new name, logo, slogan, flagship cultural event or building) to attract investment, visitors and residents.
  • Regeneration — physical and economic renewal: infrastructure, housing, public realm, employment.
  • Place-making — the more grounded practice of shaping public space so that people actually want to be in it and identify with it.

Always evaluate rebranding. Ask: (1) Is it authentic — does it grow out of the place's real history and community, or is it an imported template ("cultural quarter", "waterfront living") that could be pasted onto anywhere? (2) Is it inclusive — who is the new image for, and does the existing community recognise itself in it, or has it been written out? (3) Is it real — has the underlying economy actually changed, or only the marketing? A flagship building with a food hall in a town with no jobs has rebranded the surface and left the structure intact.

Required work · the two place studies

How to run your place studies

AQA requires two contrasting place studies: your local place (the area of your home or school) and a contrasting distant place. You must use both quantitative and qualitative sources, and explore how people experience and attach meaning to those places. This is your own work — so here is the method, not a fabricated case study.

  • 1. Define the place and its scale. A ward? A street? A whole town? Say which, and justify it — your conclusions will only be as valid as your boundary. Note immediately whether your chosen boundary matches the boundary your statistics use, because usually it does not.
  • 2. Build the endogenous profile. Site, topography, land use, built environment, infrastructure, demography, economy. Fieldwork (an environmental quality survey, land-use mapping, a photographic transect) plus census and deprivation data.
  • 3. Trace the exogenous relationships — past and present. Where did the money come from? Who migrated in, and why? What was this place connected to? What relict features prove it? This is where most weak studies fail: they describe the place in isolation.
  • 4. Gather representations, and interrogate them. Collect a deliberate spread: official (council plans, tourist board), commercial (estate agents, developers), media (news, film, literature, music), and vernacular (residents' interviews, graffiti, social media, oral history). For each, ask who made this, for whom, and what is it selecting.
  • 5. Triangulate. Set your sources against each other. Where quantitative and qualitative evidence agree, your conclusion is robust. Where they conflict — the statistics say the ward is improving, the residents say they are being pushed out — you have not found an error. You have found the most interesting thing in your study, and explaining that divergence is where the marks are.
  • 6. Be explicit about your own positionality. You are not a neutral instrument. Your age, background, and whether you are an insider or an outsider in this place shape what you notice, who talks to you, and how you read what you see. Saying so is not a weakness — it is methodological rigour, and examiners reward it.

Know the limits of your data. Census data is aggregated (it can hide extremes within a ward), it is a snapshot taken once a decade (so it may already be out of date), it undercounts transient and marginalised populations, and it measures what is countable rather than what is important. Deprivation indices rank areas, not people — a deprived person can live in an affluent ward, and a low-ranked area still contains non-deprived households. Say this in the exam and you sound like a geographer.

Exam technique · the 20-marker

Writing the evaluative essay

Paper 2's 20-mark essays are marked on AO1 (knowledge and understanding) and AO2 (application — analysis, evaluation and a substantiated conclusion). Changing Places essays fail in one predictable way: they tell the story of a place instead of arguing about the concept of place.

  • Lead with the concept, illustrate with the place. The place study is your evidence, not your structure. Build paragraphs around endogenous/exogenous, representation, contestation and agency — then bring your places in to prove each point.
  • Argue in blocks, not lists. Each paragraph: claim → mechanism → evidence from a place study → counter-argument → mini-judgement. A paragraph without a "but" is not evaluation.
  • Ask "for whom?" relentlessly. Better for whom, worse for whom, whose meaning, whose representation, whose power. This single question converts description into AO2.
  • Use both source types explicitly and comment on their limitations. Naming a limitation of your own evidence is a mark of maturity, not weakness.
  • Conclude with a judgement, not a summary. State to what extent, and on what condition your judgement depends.

Try it: "To what extent is the character of a place shaped more by exogenous factors than by endogenous ones?" Plan three blocks — (1) the case for exogenous dominance: shifting flows of investment, people and ideas can transform a place's demography, economy and identity within a decade, while its physical site is unchanged; (2) the case for endogenous: those flows do not land at random — they are attracted, filtered or repelled by the place's existing site, housing stock, infrastructure and skills, and a place with nothing to offer receives nothing; (3) the deeper point: the distinction is analytically useful but artificially clean, because today's endogenous characteristics are simply the fossilised residue of yesterday's exogenous flows — the palimpsest again. Judgement: exogenous factors drive change, endogenous factors determine what change is possible and where it lands — so the character of a place is best explained as the interaction, and the relative weight of each depends on the timescale you choose to look over.

Recap

The big ideas to know

Place: location (where) · locale (setting for social relations) · sense of place (subjective attachment); placelessness, clone towns, topophilia

Categories: near vs far (perceived, not just physical) · experienced vs media places · place as a social construct

Endogenous: location, topography, physical geography, land use, built environment, infrastructure, demographic and economic characteristics

Exogenous: relationships with other places — flows of people, money, ideas and resources. Always explain the interaction of the two

Past and present connections: relict features; the landscape as a palimpsest; past links shape present identity and present conflict

Shifting flows: gentrification and the spiral of decline; always ask "improved for whom?"

Representation: quantitative (census, IMD — comparable but blind to feeling, aggregated, quickly dated) and qualitative (film, art, literature, music, graffiti, interviews — rich but positioned)

Contested meaning: every representation is a selection; ask who produced it, for whom, and what it wants you to believe

Agencies: government · corporate bodies · community groups; rebranding, re-imaging, regeneration and place-making — evaluate for authenticity, inclusivity and substance

Place studies: a local and a contrasting distant place; quantitative + qualitative; triangulate, state your positionality, and treat conflicting sources as your best finding
