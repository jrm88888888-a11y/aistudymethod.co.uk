Mission 1

Mission 1: Is a place just a dot on a map, or something more?

Make a guess: Is a place just a dot on a map, or something more?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

  • 3.2.2.1 concept of place and its importance in human life and experience
  • 3.2.2.1 insider and outsider perspectives on place
  • 3.2.2.1 categories of place: near and far; experienced and media places
  • 3.2.2.1 endogenous and exogenous factors in place character
Mission 1

Space or place?

  • Space: a location or area, described by coordinates, size, distance.
  • Place: space that people have given meaning: memories, feelings, identity.
  • Yi-Fu Tuan (Space and Place, 1977) argued that space becomes place as we get to know it and attach value to it.
  • Places matter to people: belonging, identity, security, and where we choose to live, work and invest.
Mission 1

Why place is hard to define

  • A place has no single, fixed boundary: is "Blackpool" the promenade, the borough or the wider Fylde coast?
  • Different people give the same place different meanings.
  • Doreen Massey ("A global sense of place", 1991) argued that places are meeting points of flows and links to elsewhere, not sealed units with one identity.
  • So a place is better described by its connections and meanings as well as its location.
Mission 1

Insiders and outsiders

  • Edward Relph (Place and Placelessness, 1976) contrasted insider and outsider experiences of place.
  • Insider: feels they belong; knows the place's routines and history (a long-term resident).
  • Outsider: sees it from outside, often through images or brief visits (a tourist, a journalist, an investor).
  • The same street can feel like home to one person and alien to another; both views are real data about the place.
Mission 1

Categories of place

  • Near places: close to us, physically or emotionally (home town). Far places: distant and less familiar.
  • Experienced places: known by being there. Media places: known through films, news, social media, adverts.
  • Most people know far more places through media than through experience.
  • Media images can shape decisions to visit, move or invest, even if they are partial.

Mission 1

Two contrasting places

  • Blackpool (Lancashire coast) and Wokingham (Berkshire, west of London), Census 2021:
  • Population change 2011–21: Blackpool −0.7%; Wokingham +15.0%; England +6.6%.
  • Aged 65+: Blackpool 20.7%; Wokingham 17.2%; England 18.4%.
  • Born outside the UK: Blackpool 7.9%; Wokingham 18.9%; England 17.4%.
  • Jobs (residents in work): accommodation and food, Blackpool 10.0% vs Wokingham 2.9%; information and communication, 1.7% vs 15.0%.
  • Deprivation (IMD 2025, rank of average score, 296 districts): Blackpool 1st (most deprived); Wokingham 295th.
Mission 1

Endogenous and exogenous factors

  • Endogenous (inside the place): location, topography, land use, built environment and infrastructure, demographic and economic characteristics.
  • Blackpool: flat coastal site; promenade, piers and hotels of a seaside resort; older, less qualified workforce.
  • Wokingham: inland, commuter-belt location near Reading; newer housing estates; many residents in information and communication jobs.
  • Exogenous (relationships with other places): visitors and holiday choices for Blackpool; commuting, firms and investment linked to London and the Thames Valley for Wokingham.
Quick check

Mission check

?A new housing estate is built on an empty field. A year later, residents say it "has no sense of place yet". Following Tuan, what is most likely to turn this space into a place?

A place is a location plus meaning, shaped by features inside it and flows from outside, and seen differently by different people. Census and deprivation data measure how it changes; people's meanings explain why it matters.

Mission 2: When a steelworks owned from Thailand shut, why did a town in England feel it?

Make a guess: When a steelworks owned from Thailand shut, why did a town in England feel it?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

  • 3.2.2.2 how relationships, connections, meaning and representation affect continuity and change in places, local to global
  • 3.2.2.2.1 relationships and connections focus: economic change and social inequalities
  • 3.2.2.2.1 shifting flows of people, resources, money, investment and ideas shape places' demographic, socio-economic and cultural characteristics
  • 3.2.2.2.1 external forces at different scales: here, the decisions of a transnational corporation (with the government response)
  • 3.2.2.2.1 past and present connections shape places and embed them at regional to global scales
Mission 2

Places are made by connections

  • Every place sits in flows: of people (workers, commuters, migrants), resources (ore, coal, steel), money and investment, and ideas (technology, management).
  • When a flow changes, the place changes: jobs, wages, population and even identity.
  • Continuity and change: some characteristics last (an industrial landscape, skills), others change fast (who employs people).
  • Case: Redcar and Cleveland, on the Tees estuary, north-east England.
Mission 2

Redcar: a steel place in a global network

  • By 2015 the Redcar steelworks belonged to Sahaviriya Steel Industries (SSI), one of the Thai companies with investments in the UK.
  • Most of the rest of the wider site was owned by Tata Steel.
  • In 2011, 10.3% of Redcar and Cleveland residents in work were in manufacturing, against 8.8% in England.
  • So decisions by foreign owners and lenders, and prices in world steel markets, reached directly into local households.
Mission 2

An external force: a TNC decision

  • In 2015 SSI's UK steelworks went into liquidation.
  • The closure "gave rise to around 3,000 redundancies as well as wider supply chain impacts".
  • Between 2 October 2015 and 8 January 2016, 1,940 former SSI and supply-chain employees claimed Jobseeker's Allowance.
  • The decision was taken by a foreign-owned company, but the effects were local: lost wages, suppliers without orders, less spending in shops.
Quick check
?Which is the best example of an external force shaping Redcar in 2015?
Mission 2

The national response

  • Government promised "up to £80 million" of direct support.
  • In November 2015 it appointed Lord Heseltine to lead an inward investment initiative "to attract foreign investment" to the Tees Valley.
  • The South Tees Development Corporation was set up in 2017 to take over and redevelop the site.
  • By 2020, government spending on site works totalled £233 million since 2015.
  • A TNC decision triggered government policy: two external forces at different scales.
Mission 2

Economic change

  • Residents in manufacturing fell from 5,815 (10.3%) in 2011 to 4,609 (8.3%) in 2021.
  • Employment rate (16–64): 67.7% in 2015, below England's 73.8%.
  • By July 2025–June 2026 it was 73.5%, closer to England's 75.8%.
  • More people in work does not by itself mean the gap with the rest of England has closed: pay matters too.
Mission 2

Social inequalities

  • Median gross weekly pay for full-time workers living in Redcar and Cleveland: £506.20 in 2015, against £531.90 for England.
  • In 2025: £703.40, against £769.50 for England.
  • Deprivation (IMD 2025): 25% of the borough's small areas are in England's most deprived 10%; it ranks 37th most deprived of 296 districts on average score.
  • Its rank for local concentration of deprivation is 7th: the most deprived areas are very deprived.
Quick check
?Full-time median weekly pay in 2025 was £703.40 in Redcar and Cleveland and £769.50 in England. Redcar's pay was what percentage of England's?
Mission 2

New flows of investment

  • Teesside Freeport began operating on 19 November 2021; government estimated it would create more than 18,000 jobs and £3.2 billion over five years.
  • GE Renewables announced an offshore wind blade plant, estimated at up to 750 manufacturing jobs, to supply the Dogger Bank wind farm.
  • The development corporation planned more than 20,000 jobs on the 4,500-acre former steelworks site.
  • These are estimates and plans, not outcomes; check later data before judging them.
Mission 2

Past and present connections

  • Past: global ownership tied the site to Thailand (SSI, with three Thai banks holding a charge over its land) and to Tata Steel, whose land was bought for £12 million.
  • Present: the freeport describes Teesside as "a gateway to global markets via air, sea, rail and road" and as a base for the North Sea offshore wind industry.
  • Representation: in 2015 government called the closure "a huge blow to local people"; by 2021 Teesside was marketed as "a gateway to global markets". Changing how a place is represented aims to change who invests there.
  • The industrial past (heavy-industry land, port, skills) shapes which new connections are possible.
  • Redcar is embedded at regional (Tees Valley), national (government funding) and global (TNCs, energy markets) scales.
Mission 2

Continuity, change and who decides

  • Change: owner, main employer and economic function of the site.
  • Continuity: below-average pay and concentrated deprivation persisted after the closure (the pay and deprivation figures above).
  • Who decides: a foreign TNC closed the works; national government funded the response; a mayoral development corporation leads the redevelopment.
  • In 2024 an independent review examined the governance of the South Tees Development Corporation and the Teesworks joint venture: how a place's future is decided is itself contested.
Quick check
?Which evidence best shows continuity in Redcar and Cleveland despite major economic change?
Mission check

?A car factory owned by a TNC headquartered on another continent is the largest employer in a small town. The board decides to move production to a cheaper plant abroad. Why will the town feel it, and how could you measure the effect on inequality?

Places are shaped from outside by flows and external forces (here a TNC decision, then government policy). Measure the effects with local versus national data over time: Redcar's employment rate rose, but its pay gap with England widened.

Mission 3

Mission 3: 18.5 million people born in India live in other countries, more than from any other country. How do they change places at both ends?

Make a guess: 18.5 million people born in India live in other countries, more than from any other country. How do they change places at both ends?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

  • 3.2.2.2.1 relationships and connections: changing demographic and cultural characteristics
  • 3.2.2.2.1 shifting flows of people, resources, money, investment and ideas shape places' demographic, socio-economic and cultural characteristics
Mission 3

What is a diaspora?

  • A diaspora is a population living outside its country or region of origin that keeps links to it, including descendants born abroad.
  • Diasporas are networks: people, money, ideas and goods flow both ways between origin and destinations.
  • These flows reshape a place's demographic, socio-economic and cultural characteristics at both ends.
Mission 3

The world's largest diaspora

  • UN estimate, 2024: 18.5 million people born in India lived in other countries, the most of any country of origin. Next: China 11.7 million, Mexico 11.6 million.
  • India's emigrant stock was 6.6 million in 1990.
  • These are migrant stocks: people counted where they live, by country of birth.
  • India's Ministry of External Affairs counts 37.3 million "Overseas Indians" (January 2026), because it adds Persons of Indian Origin born abroad.
Mission 3

Where the India-born live

  • UN, 2024: United Arab Emirates 3.25 million · USA 3.17 million · Saudi Arabia 1.95 million · Kuwait 1.23 million · UK 1.04 million · Canada 1.02 million.
  • Western Asia (including the Gulf states): 8.33 million.
  • Two streams: contract work in the Gulf, and longer-term settlement in North America, Europe and Australia.
Quick check
?In 2024, 8,327,502 of the 18,533,845 India-born emigrants lived in Western Asia. What share is that?
Mission 3

Changing the origin: money home

  • Personal remittances to India were US$150.7 billion in 2025: 3.8% of GDP.
  • Kerala, in south-west India, had about 2.2 million emigrants in 2023 and received ₹2.16 lakh crore (₹2.16 trillion) in remittances; the UAE has the most emigrants from Kerala.
  • Remittances build houses and pay for schooling, but tie household incomes to jobs abroad.
Mission 3

Changing a destination: Leicester

  • Leicester has the highest share of India-born residents of any local authority in England and Wales: 16.2% (59,842 people) in 2021.
  • Gujarati was the main language of 12.6% of residents aged 3 and over.
  • Some arrived via East Africa: after Uganda ordered the expulsion of all Asians on 4 August 1972, just over 28,000 people came to the UK.
  • Leicester's Diwali is described by the University of Leicester as "one of the largest Diwali celebrations outside of India".
Mission 3

Flows from former colonies and the EU

  • Census 2021, England and Wales, people born in:
    • India 920,361 · Pakistan 623,557 · Bangladesh 273,042 · Jamaica 142,154 (former colonies);
    • Poland 743,083 · Romania 538,840 (EU members from 2004 and 2007).
  • Two waves with different drivers: Commonwealth migration after 1948, and EU free movement after enlargement.
  • The UK's Poland-born population rose from about 75,000 in 1990 to about 902,000 in 2024.
Mission 3

Southall: places of worship

  • In three Southall wards (Ealing), 31.7% of residents were Sikh in 2021; in England as a whole, 0.9% (our calculation).
  • Southall Green: Sikh 36.8%, Muslim 19.6%, Christian 24.2%, Hindu 11.6%. A mixed, not single-faith, place.
  • Sri Guru Singh Sabha Southall says it "welcomes over 50,000 people each week"; its langar (free vegetarian meal) is open to everyone "whatever their faith or background".
Mission 3

Identity and hybridity

  • Many cultures, not one: Gujarati in Leicester; Panjabi and Sikh institutions in Southall; Malayali migrants from Kerala in the Gulf.
  • Hybrid identities: children of migrants often hold several identities, such as British and Indian, and use two languages.
  • Exam move: name the scale (household, street, city, nation, world) and the direction of each influence (origin ↔ destination).
Quick check
?Which is an example of a diaspora changing culture at a local scale in a destination?
Mission check

?The UK's Poland-born population grew from about 75,000 in 1990 to about 902,000 in 2024. Which answer best describes how such a flow changes places at both ends?

A diaspora is a two-way network. Indian flows reshaped Leicester and Southall and sent US$150.7 billion home in 2025; EU flows did the same for Polish communities.

Mission 4: A poet begged bombs to fall on a town. Can a poem change how a real place is seen and treated?

Mission 4

Two ways to show a place

  • Representation: how a place is portrayed: what is shown, by whom, for whom.
  • Formal (statistical) representation: census tables, maps, geospatial data, deprivation indices. Made by official bodies with published methods.
  • Informal representation: media made with a purpose or a point of view.
  • AQA's examples: advertising, tourism material, art, film, song.
  • Neither is neutral: both select.
Mission 4

Slough, informally

  • A poem: John Betjeman's "Slough" asks for "friendly bombs" to fall on the town (as recalled in Parliament).
  • TV: the sitcom The Office and its manager David Brent are tied to Slough's image; in 2019 Slough's MP said the town has "a lot more than just David Brent and 'The Office' to offer".
  • 2002, an MP for another area: "I would not be as unkind as Mr. Betjeman in calling for friendly bombs to fall on Slough" — then used Slough as a unit of sprawl: "40 towns the size of Slough".
  • An outsider's image, still being repeated decades later.
Mission 4

Slough, formally

  • Population +13.0%, 2011–21 (England +6.6%).
  • Median age 34 (England 40).
  • 46.7% Asian, Asian British or Asian Welsh; 36.0% White (2021).
  • Index of Multiple Deprivation 2025: 105th of 296 districts (1 = most deprived); 0% of its neighbourhoods in England's most deprived 10%.
  • The formal image: young, growing, diverse; more deprived than about two-thirds of districts, yet with no neighbourhood in the worst 10%. Not the poem's image.
Quick check
?Which of these is a formal representation of Slough?
Mission 4

Insiders answer back

  • 2019, in a debate on a "Town of Culture" award, Slough's MP Tan Dhesi: "Slough is on the up: we have moved on from John Betjeman's poem."
  • "Slough is a fantastic, diverse cultural melting pot … a major business, creative and cultural powerhouse, with Pinewood Studios right on our doorstep."
  • Continuity: the outsider image persists. Change: insiders contest it, here to win a culture award.
Mission 4

Who feels they belong?

Community Life Survey, England, 2023/24: adults who feel they belong strongly to their immediate neighbourhood.

  • All adults: 61%.
  • Age: 16–24 54%; 25–34 51%; other age groups 59–73%.
  • Sex: female 63%; male 60%.
  • Sexual orientation: heterosexual 61%; gay or lesbian 49%; bisexual 45%.
  • Religion: Jewish 68%, Muslim 67%, Christian 66%; no religion 56%.
Mission 4

Same park, different place

  • ONS, Great Britain, Feb–Mar 2022: walking alone in a park or open space after dark.
  • Felt very or fairly unsafe: 82% of women, 42% of men.
  • Women aged 16–34 felt the most unsafe of any age and sex group using public transport alone after dark.
  • Perception depends on who you are, not only on the place.
Mission 4

Feelings change behaviour

  • Place attachment: an emotional bond between a person and a place.
  • Fear changes routines: adults who stopped walking in parks or open spaces after dark rose from 32% to 37% (women) and 18% to 24% (men), June 2021 → Feb–Mar 2022.
  • Attachment keeps people: 61% would still like to live in their area in five years; 84% of adults aged 75+.
  • Attachment mobilises people: at Elephant and Castle, London, the community group Latin Elephant objected at the January 2018 planning committee on the shopping centre.
Quick check
?Belonging is 54% among 16–24-year-olds and 59–73% for adults aged 35 and over. Which conclusion is best supported?
Mission 4

Representation and the "need" to regenerate

  • 2016, the Prime Minister: "so-called sink estates … brutal high-rise towers and dark alleyways that are a gift to criminals"; a £140 million fund for estate regeneration.
  • Heygate estate, Southwark (completed 1974, about 3,000 people): a study "concluded that the buildings were structurally sound, but in need of complete refurbishment".
  • The council cited "housing management challenges and regeneration opportunities"; "Some residents therefore say that releasing the very significant value of the land was the key factor".
  • A powerful image can make demolition look necessary; other evidence can question it.
Quick check
?A council says an estate "must be demolished". Which evidence most directly questions that need?
Mission 4

Past development shapes present meaning

  • Heygate: a 1970s council estate → labelled a problem → demolition completed July 2014.
  • Replaced by Elephant Park: 3,000 new homes; nearly half the site public space.
  • Slough: its MP ties its present meaning to film and business, "with Pinewood Studios right on our doorstep".
  • Each wave of development (council housing, regeneration, creative industries) leaves new meanings, and new arguments.
Mission 4

How to judge any representation

  • Who made it? For whom? When? Why?
  • What is left out?
  • Check informal images against formal data. Then check the data: a census is only every 10 years; categories are chosen by officials; averages hide neighbourhoods.
  • Exam move ("Evaluate"): weigh at least one informal and one formal representation, then judge which is more useful for what purpose.
Mission check

?A seaside town's tourism video shows sunny beaches and busy cafés. Census data show an old population and high deprivation. A local blog posts photos of closed shops. Which evaluation is strongest?

Representations are made for purposes. Informal and formal images of the same place differ because they are built by different people for different audiences, and they can change how a place is treated.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Why would a city spend millions on a year of art?

Make a guess: Why would a city spend millions on a year of art?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

  • 3.2.2.2.2 external agencies (government, corporate, community groups) try to create place meanings that shape behaviour
  • 3.2.2.2.2 past and present development processes shape places' social and economic character and present meanings
Mission 5

Who makes a place's meaning?

  • Places are not only lived in; they are presented.
  • Government (national and local), corporations (developers, landowners) and community groups all try to create meanings.
  • Aim: to change behaviour: where people invest, visit, live, work, shop.
  • Key terms: placemaking (shaping a place and its image); place marketing; rebranding = reimaging (new image) + regeneration (physical, economic and social renewal).
Mission 5

Why rebrand?

  • Decline: lost industries, empty sites, falling population.
  • Image: a negative representation that deters investors and visitors.
  • Competition: cities compete for firms, students, tourists and events.
  • London 2012: a key legacy aim was "the regeneration and transformation of East London, one of the city's most deprived areas at the time".
  • Past development leaves the character that a rebrand tries to change.
Mission 5

Culture-led: Hull, UK City of Culture 2017

  • Purpose of the UK City of Culture title: "to drive culture-led regeneration, enhance social cohesion and foster economic growth".
  • Hull 2017 "leveraged over £676 million in investments for venue refurbishments and urban enhancements".
  • New venues created and existing ones redeveloped; Hull's Humber Street Gallery "supports visual artists in the city who were historically underserved".
  • Arts Council National Portfolio funding rose 35.7% in Hull.
  • Art + architecture + heritage combined.
Mission 5

Did the rebrand work?

DCMS's 2026 legacy study tested outcomes against comparison cities:

  • Tourism jobs: Hull shows "a positive increase but is not statistically significant".
  • Wellbeing: "Short-term impact but not sustained after event."
  • Arts funding: the title raises public funding, "however the funding is not sustained".
  • Jobs: "many jobs are temporary for the delivery of the year."
  • COVID-19 disrupted Hull's later economic data.
Quick check
?Hull's tourism jobs rose after 2017, but the increase was "not statistically significant". What does this mean?
Mission 5

Sport-led: London 2012

  • The Games acted as "a catalyst for the regeneration of a previously underdeveloped area in East London".
  • Today: Queen Elizabeth Olympic Park, 560 acres, free to visit, "evolved into a thriving innovation district".
  • The park's own body claims £3.5 billion GVA and 38,300 jobs in the Legacy Area.
  • DCMS: tourism employment and turnover impacts "found a year after event"; no wellbeing impact found.
  • Caution: some jobs and visits would have happened anyway, or moved from elsewhere.
Mission 5

Community groups shape places too

  • Neighbourhood plans: communities write a plan; if approved at a referendum it has the same legal status as the local plan.
  • First in England: Upper Eden, Cumbria, approved by 90% of voters on 8 March 2013.
  • Residents' and traders' groups: Latin Elephant (Elephant and Castle, London) objected to the shopping-centre plan in 2018 and campaigned for traders' relocation.
  • Social media and digital archives: its MyElephantStory archive (2021) records the area's memory.
Quick check
?Which is a community group shaping place meaning?
Mission 5

Past development, present meaning

  • East London: "one of the city's most deprived areas at the time" → Games venues → "innovation district".
  • Hull: a city whose venues closed for refurbishment before 2017 → reopened as a cultural brand.
  • Each wave of development leaves buildings, jobs and a reputation that later agencies must work with or against.
Quick check
?Two years after a city's year of culture, visitor numbers are back to their earlier level. What is the best judgement?
Mission check

?A former steel town plans a new brand: a festival, a food market in old warehouses and a heritage trail. Residents' groups say local people were not asked. Which evaluation is strongest?

Agencies remake places to change behaviour. Judge a rebrand by its aims, lasting outcomes against comparison places, and whose meaning it creates.

Mission 6

What is a local place study?

  • A locality, neighbourhood or small community, urban or rural, where you live or study.
  • Goal: describe its character, explain how it developed, and show how people's lives changed.
  • AQA: a local place study plus a contrasting, distant place study.
  • This mission models the method with one exemplar: Elephant and Castle, London.
Mission 6

The place profile

Six lenses (a checklist for any place study):

  • Demographic: age, ethnicity, migration.
  • Socio-economic: jobs, income, deprivation, housing tenure.
  • Cultural: languages, faiths, food, festivals, shops.
  • Political: who governs; campaigns; votes.
  • Built: housing types, street pattern, landmarks.
  • Natural: relief, rivers, green space.

Sources: quantitative (census, IMD, employment) and qualitative (photos, oral histories, planning documents, newspapers, social media).

Mission 6

Exemplar: Elephant and Castle

  • A neighbourhood in the London Borough of Southwark.
  • Built, then: the Heygate estate, completed 1974, about 3,000 people; 1,212 homes.
  • Built, now: Elephant Park, approved January 2013: 3,000 new homes; nearly half public space.
  • Cultural: a cluster of Latin American–owned businesses, surveyed by the community group Latin Elephant from July 2012.
  • Political: the shopping centre's demolition application was rejected in January 2018, then approved in January 2019.
Mission 6

The numbers: Southwark, Census 2011 → 2021

  • Population 288,300 → 307,600 (+6.7%; London +7.7%).
  • Households renting socially 43.7% → 39.7%: England's second-largest fall.
  • Ethnic group: White 54.2% → 51.4%; Black 26.9% → 25.1%; Asian 9.4% → 9.9%.
  • Born in Nigeria: 13,600 → 11,200 residents.
  • In work (16+, excluding full-time students): 59.6% → 62.7%; England fell 56.5% → 55.7%.
Quick check
?Southwark's 2011–21 census figures are for the whole borough. What is their main limitation for studying Elephant and Castle?
Mission 6

Lived experience: the qualitative layer

  • Oral and community records: Latin Elephant began in 2012 as a blog by a researcher studying Latin Americans in Elephant and Castle.
  • In 2021 it launched MyElephantStory, a digital archive of the area, on the first anniversary of the shopping centre's closure.
  • Residents' views: some Heygate residents said "releasing the very significant value of the land was the key factor" in demolition.
  • Official accounts: the council reports that 99% of its rehoused Heygate tenants chose to stay in Southwark.
  • Different voices, different experiences of the same change.
Mission 6

Connections and flows

  • People: Latin American traders and residents; Nigeria-born residents fewer than in 2011.
  • Money: the council partnered with Lend Lease (now Lendlease) in 2007 to redevelop the Heygate site; Lendlease lists Elephant Park beside projects in Sydney, Singapore, Malaysia and New York.
  • Ideas: national talk of "so-called sink estates" and a £140 million regeneration fund (2016); local ideas, such as a "Latin Quarter" vision (2014–16).
  • These flows tie one London neighbourhood to the region, the nation and the world.

Mission 6

Continuity and change in people's lives

  • Change: 595 council tenants rehoused; 99% stayed in Southwark; all offered the right to return.
  • Change: shopping centre closed 24 Sep 2020; traders' relocation began the next day, "although not everyone could be relocated".
  • Continuity: Latin American businesses and community groups still organise in the area.
  • Ask: how did these changes affect your life, and the lives of others?
Mission 6

Choose your focus

AQA: each place study also focuses on one of:

  • Demographic and cultural change, e.g. Elephant and Castle's Latin American businesses and changing ethnic mix.
  • Economic change and social inequalities, e.g. falling social renting and contested affordable housing.
  • Pick the focus your evidence can support, and use the same focus for the distant place.
Mission 6

Your own study, step by step

  • 1. Define the boundary (a ward, an LSOA cluster, a village).
  • 2. Profile it with the six lenses.
  • 3. Collect at least two quantitative and two qualitative sources, past and present.
  • 4. Trace flows and influences at each scale.
  • 5. Record whose lives changed, how, and how you experience it.
  • 6. State your sources' limits (date, scale, author's purpose).

A strong place study mixes quantitative and qualitative sources, past and present, at the right scale, and traces the flows and influences that made the place.

Mission 7: A crowded ward on the edge of a global city: how do you study a place thousands of miles away?

Make a guess: A crowded ward on the edge of a global city: how do you study a place thousands of miles away?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

  • 3.2.2.2 how students' own and others' lives are affected by continuity and change in places
  • 3.2.2.4 contrasting place study: the developing character of a contrasting, distant place
  • 3.2.2.4 lived experience past and present, using qualitative and quantitative sources
  • 3.2.2.4 focus on either demographic and cultural change or economic change and social inequalities
Mission 7

Why a contrasting place?

  • Same method as your local study: profile, sources, flows, lived experience.
  • AQA: a distant place that contrasts in economic development, population density, culture and/or political-economic system.
  • Contrast sharpens explanation: why did two places develop so differently?
Mission 7

Exemplar: M/East ward, Mumbai

  • Mumbai: about 20.2 million people (2025, urban agglomeration); about 27,000 people per km².
  • An Alpha world city; home of India's central bank since 1937.
  • M/East: one of Mumbai's municipal wards; 807,720 people in 166,400 households (Census 2011).
  • Contrast with Elephant and Castle: India is lower-middle income, the UK high income (World Bank); far higher density; different culture and governance.

Mission 7

When sources disagree

  • Slum share in M/East: 84.9% from the municipal census tables; 77.5% in an NGO newsletter.
  • Mumbai as a whole: 52.5% (municipal tables) vs 41.84% (NGO newsletter).
  • Possible reasons: different definitions of "slum", different base years or areas.
  • Rule: quote the source and its definition; never mix figures from different sources in one comparison.
Mission 7

Lived experience: an insider NGO's account

Apnalaya, an NGO working in the ward, wrote in 2018:

  • "Since the 1960s, this ward has given shelter to migrants from different parts of India, as well as those who have been 'resettled' from slums in the inner recesses of the city."
  • It reports an infant mortality rate of 66 per 1,000 live births (national average 41) and an average age at death of 39, from its own 2017 study.
  • It describes residents winning "legal water connections, electricity connections, cooking gas and ration cards" through citizen campaigns.
  • Valuable voice; also an organisation with a mission. Check its figures against official data where possible.
Mission 7

Connections and flows

  • People: migrants from across India since the 1960s; residents resettled from inner-city slums.
  • Change over time: Mumbai's urban agglomeration grew from about 15.95 million (2000) to 20.2 million (2025): +27% (our calculation).
  • Resources: the ward has a large open landfill receiving "around 4500 metric tons of garbage every day" (Apnalaya's figure): a flow from the whole city into one ward.
  • Money: Mumbai is a global financial centre (Alpha city; central bank HQ), yet M/East "sits at the bottom of the 24 wards of Mumbai" on human development (Mumbai Human Development Report 2009, as quoted by Apnalaya).
  • Ideas: right-to-information requests and civic campaigns, such as "Mission 24".
Quick check
?Which is a flow of resources shaping M/East?
Mission 7

Economic change and social inequality

  • Within one city: slum share 84.9% (M/East) vs 10.0% (D ward); literacy 83.4% vs 91.0%.
  • Apnalaya: "Every second male and six out of seven females are unemployed" in the ward.
  • Global wealth and local deprivation sit side by side: the inequality is part of the place's character.
  • Change over time: compare with an earlier census and with recent NGO and municipal reports.
Mission 7

Lived experience, past and present

  • Past: rural migrants and resettled families building homes on the city's edge (Apnalaya).
  • Present: residents organising for water, power and toilets.
  • Compare with Elephant and Castle: there, long-standing residents and traders lived through demolition and rebuilding; here, newcomers live through missing services.
  • Ask how each change affected people's lives, and whose voices your sources include.

Mission 7

Researching at a distance

  • Official data: census tables, municipal reports.
  • Local voices: NGO reports, community websites, local news, published oral histories.
  • Visual: satellite and street-level imagery, dated photographs.
  • Triangulate at least two sources for each claim.
  • Name your outsider position: you see the place through others' representations.
Mission check

?A student in Leeds studies a village in rural Peru using district census tables, an NGO report and published interviews, all from 2023. Which addition would most improve it as a study of the place's developing character?

A distant place study works like a local one: profile, sources (counted and told) from past and present, flows, lived experience, plus extra care with scale, definitions and your outsider view.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Space vs place (Tuan); places as meeting points of flows (Massey); endogenous and exogenous factors. Insider/outsider (Relph); near/far, experienced/media places.
  • Blackpool vs Wokingham: −0.7% vs +15.0% population (2011–21); IMD 2025 rank 1 vs 295; tourism vs information and communication jobs.

Mission 2

  • Redcar and Cleveland: steelworks owned by Thai SSI went into liquidation in 2015 (about 3,000 redundancies; 1,940 JSA claims by January 2016); government support (up to £80 m; £233 m on site works) and new flows (Teesside Freeport, offshore wind).
  • Economic change: manufacturing 10.3% → 8.3% of residents in work (2011–21). Inequality: pay 95.2% → 91.4% of England's (2015–25); 25% of small areas in England's most deprived 10% (IMD 2025).

Mission 3

  • Diasporas change places at both ends: remittances and returning ideas at home; language, faith, food and festivals in destinations.

Mission 4

  • Slough: a poem and a sitcom made an outsider image; census and IMD data paint a young, diverse, mid-ranked town; insiders contest the image.
  • Perception and attachment vary with age, sex, sexuality and religion, and they change behaviour (avoiding parks after dark, staying, campaigning).
  • Representations such as "sink estates" can drive regeneration; contrasting evidence can question it.

Mission 5

  • Government, corporations and community groups create place meanings to shape investment, visits and residence.
  • Hull 2017 (culture-led): £676 million investment leveraged; tourism-jobs rise not significant; wellbeing gains short-lived.
  • London 2012 (sport-led): tourism effects a year on; park now an "innovation district".

Mission 6

  • Profile a place with six lenses; use quantitative and qualitative sources; check scale and date.
  • Elephant and Castle: Heygate to Elephant Park, Latin American traders, global developer, national policy, contested change.
  • Choose one focus: demographic and cultural, or economic and inequality.

Mission 7

  • M/East, Mumbai: 807,720 people; 84.9% in slums and 83.4% literacy (aged 7+) vs 52.5% and 89.7% for Mumbai (Census 2011, municipal tables).
  • Flows: migrants and resettled families, the city's waste, global wealth beside a ward at the bottom of Mumbai's human development ranking, civic ideas.
  • Sources disagree (84.9% vs 77.5%): name the source and definition; triangulate.
