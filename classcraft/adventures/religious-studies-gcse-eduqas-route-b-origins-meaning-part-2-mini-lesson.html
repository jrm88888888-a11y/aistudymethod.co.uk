Mission 1

Why paint the faith?

  • Christian painting expresses belief in pictures: for those who make it and those who look.
  • The artist may paint as an act of prayer and offer skill to God.
  • The observer may learn, pray, or be moved to awe.
  • Teaching: Pope Gregory the Great (c. 600) said pictures let those who cannot read "read" the faith on the walls (paraphrase).
  • Devotion: an image focuses prayer.
  • Beauty: Catholics believe beauty reflects God.
  • A fresco is painted on wet plaster, so the colour becomes part of the wall.
Mission 1

The Sistine Chapel ceiling

  • Michelangelo Buonarroti (1475–1564): Italian sculptor, painter and poet.
  • Pope Julius II asked him to paint the ceiling of the Sistine Chapel, the Pope's chapel in the Vatican (1508–1512).
  • Down the centre: nine scenes from Genesis, from God separating light from darkness to the story of Noah.
  • The Creation of Adam (c. 1511–12) is one of the central scenes.
  • The chapel is still used: cardinals elect a new pope there.
Mission 1

Reading the Creation of Adam

  • Left: Adam lies on bare earth, strong and beautiful, but limp: his hand hangs, waiting.
  • Right: God, shown as a powerful elderly man, sweeps through the sky, carried by angels in a billowing red cloak.
  • Centre: their fingers almost touch. The gap is the moment just before life passes from God to Adam.
  • Adam's pose roughly mirrors God's: humans are made in God's likeness.
  • Under God's arm, a young woman looks towards Adam: often read as Eve, not yet created; some read her as Wisdom or Mary.
  • Some modern viewers see the red cloak as shaped like a human brain: perhaps God gives a mind as well as life (one interpretation).

Mission 1Evaluate

"How far does the Creation of Adam express Catholic beliefs?"

  • Expresses them well: God as active, powerful creator (omnipotent); God comes from heaven to earth (transcendent, beyond the created world, yet personal); life as God's gift; Adam's likeness to God (imago Dei, "the image of God") and the beauty of the human body (dignity); Eve already in God's plan.
  • Falls short: Catholic teaching is that God is spirit (John 4:24), neither man nor woman; the old man is a symbol (cf. "Ancient of Days", Daniel 7:9), and some Christians think God should not be pictured at all. Genesis 2:7 has breath, not a touch. The painting shows the body, not reason, free will or the soul, which Catholics see as central to God's image.
  • Judge it by: Can a picture ever show an invisible God? Does a symbol have to be literal to be true? Which Catholic beliefs about humans matter most?
Mission check

?A modern Catholic artist paints a nurse holding a newborn baby's hand in a hospital, with the title In His Image. Which Catholic belief from the Creation of Adam is she most clearly expressing?

A painting is a sermon without words: Catholics read the Creation of Adam as life and dignity given by God, and the Last Judgement as hope and warning at the end.

Mission 2

Why symbols?

  • A symbol is a sign that stands for something more than itself.
  • Christian symbols pack a belief into a simple image: who Jesus is, what he did, what Christians hope for.
  • They teach (even those who cannot read), identify Christians and their buildings, and focus prayer.
  • They appear on church walls and windows, vestments, altar cloths, jewellery, gravestones and car stickers.
  • The most important Christian symbol is the cross.
  • The grid also shows the fish, the Greek letters Chi-Rho and Alpha–Omega, and the dove.
  • The fish: its Greek letters (ΙΧΘΥΣ) begin the words "Jesus Christ, God's Son, Saviour".
Mission 2

Cross and crucifix

  • Cross: reminds Christians that Jesus died on a cross to save people from sin. "The word of the cross is… to us who are being saved… the power of God." (1 Corinthians 1:18)
  • Plain (empty) cross: Jesus is no longer on it: he is risen. Common in Protestant churches; also used by Catholics.
  • Crucifix: a cross with the figure of Christ: his suffering and sacrifice out of love. Usual in Catholic churches, and in some Anglican and Lutheran ones; Orthodox use painted crosses.
  • Uses: on or above the altar; carried in processions; worn on a chain; held by the dying; the sign of the cross made over the body in prayer.
  • "If anyone desires to come after me, let him deny himself, take up his cross, and follow me." (Matthew 16:24)

Mission 2

Chi-Rho and Alpha–Omega

  • Chi-Rho (☧): the Greek letters Χ (ch) and Ρ (r), the first two letters of Christos (Christ, "anointed one").
  • Tradition links it with the emperor Constantine, who put Christ's sign on his soldiers' shields before winning the battle of the Milvian Bridge (AD 312).
  • Seen on vestments, altar cloths, tombs and church doors: Jesus is the Christ, the Messiah.
  • Alpha (Α) and Omega (Ω): the first and last letters of the Greek alphabet. "I am the Alpha and the Omega, the First and the Last, the Beginning and the End." (Revelation 22:13)
  • Meaning: Christ is eternal, there before creation and at the end of time.
  • At the Easter Vigil the priest marks Α, Ω and the year on the Paschal candle: all time belongs to the risen Christ.
Mission 2

Dove, eagle and lamb

  • Dove: the Holy Spirit. At Jesus' baptism "He saw the Spirit of God descending as a dove" (Matthew 3:16). Also peace: Noah's dove returned with an olive leaf (Genesis 8:11).
  • Eagle: many church lecterns are brass eagles with outstretched wings: carrying God's Word to the world. The eagle is also St John's symbol, and a sign of rising to God: "They will mount up with wings like eagles." (Isaiah 40:31)
  • Lamb: "Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!" (John 1:29). Christ the sacrifice, gentle and innocent; in Revelation the Lamb stands "as though it had been slain" (5:6): sacrificed yet alive.
Mission 2

The four evangelists

  • The four Gospel writers have symbols taken from the four living creatures (Ezekiel 1:10; Revelation 4:7).
  • Matthew: a man (or angel): his Gospel opens with Jesus' human family tree.
  • Mark: a lion: opens with John the Baptist, a voice "crying in the wilderness", like a lion's roar.
  • Luke: an ox (or calf), an animal of sacrifice: opens with the priest Zechariah in the Temple.
  • John: an eagle: his Gospel soars from the start: "In the beginning was the Word".
  • Found on pulpits, Bible covers, windows and around images of Christ: four Gospels, one Christ.

Mission 2

The San Clemente apse mosaic

  • Basilica of San Clemente, Rome; apse mosaic, early 12th century, gold background.
  • Centre: Christ on the cross, with Mary and St John. On the cross, twelve white doves: the apostles.
  • The cross grows like a plant: from its base spring green vine scrolls that fill the whole apse: the Church as a living vine ("I am the vine. You are the branches", John 15:5).
  • Four rivers flow from the foot of the cross, and deer drink: "As the deer pants for the water brooks, so my soul pants after you, God" (Psalm 42:1). Baptism and new life.
  • Above: God's hand holds out a victory wreath. Below: twelve lambs walk from Bethlehem and Jerusalem towards the Lamb of God.
  • Arch: Christ in a circle with the symbols of the four evangelists.
  • Top of the apse: the vine scrolls climb to a blue disc bearing the Chi-Rho, with Alpha and Omega hanging from it: Jesus is the Christ, eternal.
Mission 2

The cross as the tree of life; Christ the New Adam

  • Eden had a tree of life (Genesis 2:9). Adam's disobedience at a tree brought death.
  • Christians call the cross a tree (Acts 5:30; 1 Peter 2:24). By Christ's obedience on it, life is restored.
  • New Adam: "For as in Adam all die, so also in Christ all will be made alive." (1 Corinthians 15:22) · "through the obedience of the one, many will be made righteous" (Romans 5:19).
  • In heaven the tree of life returns: "The leaves of the tree were for the healing of the nations." (Revelation 22:2)
  • In San Clemente: the cross is shown as a living tree, its branches spreading over the world, with rivers like Eden's (Genesis 2:10). The mosaic's Latin inscription likens the Church to a vine that the cross makes flourish.
  • Belief: what was lost in Eden is restored at Calvary; the cross is not the end but the source of life.

Mission 3

Love your neighbour, in concrete ways

  • Jesus joins two commands: love God, and "you shall love your neighbour as yourself" (Mark 12:31, quoting Leviticus 19:18).
  • The Good Samaritan: the neighbour is "he who showed mercy", and "Go and do likewise" (Luke 10:37).
  • Love must be concrete: "if a brother or sister is naked and in lack of daily food" and you only wish them well, "what good is it?" (James 2:15–16).
  • Catholic Social Teaching (CST): the Church's teaching on how society should be organised so that every person can flourish.
  • Its modern form begins with Pope Leo XIII's letter on workers, Rerum Novarum (1891).
Mission 3

Every person in God's image

  • "God created man in his own image … male and female he created them." (Genesis 1:27)
  • Imago Dei (Latin, "image of God"): every human being has dignity: worth that does not depend on wealth, ability, age or usefulness.
  • This is the root of all CST: if every person bears God's image, harming or ignoring them wrongs God.
  • Gaudium et Spes 29: all people share a basic equality, because all are made in God's image and redeemed by Christ. Discrimination because of sex, race, colour, social condition, language or religion is against God's plan and must be overcome (paraphrase).
  • So imago Dei leads straight to justice: equal dignity demands equal rights.
Quick check
?Why does belief in imago Dei lead Catholics to work for social justice?
Mission 3

Key principles of CST

  • Dignity of the human person: every life matters, from conception to natural death.
  • Common good: society should give everyone what they need to flourish, not just the majority.
  • Solidarity: we are one human family; others' suffering is our concern.
  • Preferential option for the poor: the needs of the poorest come first.
  • Subsidiarity: decisions taken as close as possible to the people affected.
  • Care for creation: the earth is a gift for all, including future generations.
  • Peace: built on justice, not just the absence of war.
  • CST asks for charity (meeting today's need) and justice (changing what causes the need).
Mission 3

Justice, peace and reconciliation

  • Justice: giving each person what is due to them as a child of God: "let justice roll on like rivers" (Amos 5:24).
  • Peace: "Blessed are the peacemakers" (Matthew 5:9).
  • Gaudium et Spes 78: peace is not merely the absence of war or a balance of power; it is the work of justice (Isaiah 32:17) and the fruit of love, and must be built up constantly (paraphrase).
  • Reconciliation: God "reconciled us to himself through Jesus Christ, and gave to us the ministry of reconciliation" (2 Corinthians 5:18): healing broken relationships between people and with God.
  • Link: every person bears God's image, so injustice and violence wound that image; peace begins by respecting it.
Quick check
?A Catholic school runs a food bank collection and also writes to its MP asking for a law to raise low wages. Which principle of Catholic Social Teaching does the letter to the MP show most clearly?
Mission 3Evaluate

"Giving to charity is enough: changing society is not the Church's job."

  • Reasons for: Jesus fed and healed individuals; he did not run a political campaign. Charity is direct, personal and non-divisive. Some Christians argue the Church should stay out of politics and focus on salvation.
  • Reasons against: CST says faith cannot be kept private (EG 183) and that structural causes of poverty must be tackled (EG 202). The prophets denounced unjust rulers (Amos 5:24). If laws keep people poor, charity alone can never be enough.
  • Another view: both together: charity meets today's need; justice changes tomorrow. Catholics may disagree about which policies best serve the common good.
  • Judge it by: Does helping individuals change the causes of poverty? Where is the line between Gospel justice and party politics? What did Jesus' example ask of his followers?
Mission check

Catholic Social Teaching turns "love your neighbour" into justice for every person, because every person bears God's image.

Mission 4

Faith in action

  • "Faith, if it has no works, is dead in itself." (James 2:17)
  • Catholic Social Teaching (see Mission 3) is lived out by individuals, parishes and agencies.
  • Agencies work at different levels: local (next door), national, global.
  • Each one puts a CST principle to work: dignity, solidarity, option for the poor, care for creation, peace.
  • This mission starts with CAFOD, the agency every course studies.
  • Then SVP and inter-faith dialogue in Britain.
Mission 4

CAFOD: what it does

  • CAFOD: the Catholic Agency for Overseas Development, the official aid agency of the Catholic Church in England and Wales, founded 1962.
  • Part of Caritas Internationalis, the Church's worldwide network of charities.
  • Works through local partners (often local churches) rather than sending its own staff to run projects.
  • Emergency relief: food, water and shelter after disasters and wars.
  • Long-term development: farming, clean water, health, education, livelihoods.
  • Campaigning: asking UK governments and businesses to change unjust policies on debt, trade and climate.
  • Parishes and schools raise money on Family Fast Days (Lent and harvest).
Mission 4

CAFOD: why it does it

  • Imago Dei: every person has dignity, so no one should be left in poverty.
  • Matthew 25:40: "you did it to one of the least of these my brothers, you did it to me."
  • Solidarity: one human family; distance does not make someone less a neighbour (Luke 10:37).
  • Option for the poor and justice: charity for today, campaigns to change causes.
  • Partnership: local people decide and lead; this respects their dignity and subsidiarity.
  • Help is given to anyone in need, regardless of religion.
Mission 4

CAFOD and caring for creation

  • Catholics believe creation is good (Genesis 1:31) and a gift for all.
  • CAFOD sees environmental damage as an injustice to the poorest, who suffer first from drought, floods and failed harvests.
  • Sustainability work: helping farmers adapt to a changing climate, e.g. drought-resistant crops, water storage, clean energy.
  • Campaigns in the UK on climate and fair use of resources, inspired by Pope Francis' Laudato Si' (2015).
  • LiveSimply award: parishes and schools commit to live simply, sustainably and in solidarity with people in poverty.
  • So CAFOD reflects respect for creation as well as dignity and love of neighbour.
Quick check
?CAFOD funds a local church group in Ethiopia that helps farmers build water tanks, instead of sending British staff to build them. Which Catholic belief does working this way most clearly reflect?
Mission 4

SVP: love next door

  • Society of St Vincent de Paul (SVP), founded in Paris in 1833 by a student, Blessed Frédéric Ozanam (1813–1853), and friends.
  • Named after St Vincent de Paul (1581–1660), a French priest who served the poor.
  • Mostly lay volunteers in local groups called conferences, often in a parish.
  • Aims: person-to-person help: visiting the lonely, sick, prisoners and families in need at home; food, furniture and friendship; seeking to tackle the causes of poverty.
  • Help is given to anyone, regardless of faith; the volunteer sees Christ in the person (Matthew 25:36).
Quick check
?Which statement correctly compares the Society of St Vincent de Paul with CAFOD?
Mission 4

Inter-faith dialogue in Britain

  • Vatican II, Nostra Aetate (1965): the Church rejects nothing that is true and holy in other religions (NA 2, paraphrase).
  • Britain has a Christian heritage and is now diverse: Christians of many Churches, Buddhist, Hindu, Jewish, Muslim and Sikh communities, and many Humanists and atheists.
  • Four kinds of dialogue (Dialogue and Proclamation, 1991): of life (neighbours), of action (working together), of theological exchange, of religious experience (sharing prayer traditions).
  • Examples: the Council of Christians and Jews (1942, co-founded by Cardinal Hinsley); parishes visiting mosques, gurdwaras and mandirs; joint food banks; the bishops' guide Meeting God in Friend and Stranger (2010).
  • With Humanists and atheists: shared work for the common good and honest debate about meaning and ethics.
  • Aim: understanding, respect, tolerance and harmony, rooted in the dignity of every person. Dialogue does not mean saying all beliefs are the same.
Quick check
?A Catholic parish, a mosque and a local Humanist group together run a winter night shelter, and meet afterwards to talk about why each of them helps. According to Catholic teaching, what is the main value of this kind of inter-faith dialogue?
Mission 4Evaluate

"Supporting a charity is the best way for a Catholic to love their neighbour."

  • Reasons for: agencies such as CAFOD have expertise and reach that one person lacks; they tackle causes as well as symptoms; giving through a parish unites a whole community in solidarity.
  • Reasons against: Matthew 25 asks for personal acts (visiting the sick and prisoners), which money cannot replace; SVP's founders insisted on person-to-person contact; love of neighbour starts at home, with family and the people next door; prayer and peacemaking matter too.
  • Another view: different ways suit different people and stages of life; CST asks for both charity and justice.
  • Extent: how far do CAFOD and SVP reflect dignity, love of neighbour and care for creation? Weigh what each does well and what each cannot do.
  • Judge it by: Does the way of loving change the causes of need? Does it involve real relationship? Is it open to everyone, whatever their circumstances?

Mission 5

Mainly Christian, and diverse

  • Britain's religious traditions are in the main Christian: Anglican, Catholic, Methodist, Baptist, Orthodox, Pentecostal and many more.
  • Britain is also diverse: Muslims, Hindus, Sikhs, Jews and Buddhists, among others, have long-established communities.
  • Many people hold non-religious beliefs, such as atheism and humanism.
  • Diversity means living side by side: shared streets, schools, workplaces and friendships.

Mission 5

Rooted in Christianity

  • Festivals: Christmas Day, Good Friday and Easter Monday are bank holidays; school holidays follow Christmas and Easter.
  • Sunday: limits on large shops' opening hours (England and Wales) come from Sunday as the Christian day of rest.
  • Monarch: crowned in a Christian service; Supreme Governor of the Church of England.
  • Parliament: Church of England bishops sit in the House of Lords; each day begins with prayers.
  • Schools: in England, daily collective worship is meant to be mainly of a broadly Christian character; many schools are church schools.
  • Laws and values: many laws reflect values found in Christian teaching, such as the Ten Commandments' bans on murder and theft.
Mission 5

Other traditions recognised

  • Festivals in public life: Diwali lights in Leicester, Vaisakhi processions in Southall and Birmingham, Eid prayers in city parks, Hanukkah candles lit in Trafalgar Square.
  • Law: the Equality Act 2010 protects people from discrimination because of religion or belief, including non-belief.
  • Schools: religious education teaches several religions and non-religious views; many schools mark Eid, Diwali and other festivals; some are Jewish, Muslim, Sikh or Hindu faith schools.
  • Work and public services: many employers allow leave for religious festivals; hospitals and prisons have chaplains of many faiths.
  • At the 2023 coronation, leaders of other faiths took part in the service.
Mission 5

Atheism and humanism

  • Atheism: the belief that there is no God.
  • Agnosticism: the view that we cannot know whether God exists.
  • Humanism: a non-religious worldview. Humanists usually believe:
  • there is no God; this is the one life we have;
  • morality comes from human reason, empathy and care for others' wellbeing, not from scripture;
  • science and evidence are the best guides to understanding the world.
  • Most humanists are atheists or agnostics, but not every atheist is a humanist.
  • Humanists UK offers non-religious weddings, namings and funerals; humanist weddings are legally recognised in Scotland and Northern Ireland.

Mission 5

The Catholic Church and inter-faith dialogue

  • The Second Vatican Council's Nostra Aetate (1965) says the Church "rejects nothing that is true and holy in these religions" (2).
  • Dialogue aims at understanding, respect, tolerance and harmony, not giving up one's own faith.
  • Catholic teaching names four forms of dialogue: of life (being good neighbours), of action (working together for justice), of theological exchange (experts discussing beliefs) and of religious experience (sharing ways of prayer).
  • In Britain: the Bishops' Conference of England and Wales has a department for dialogue and unity; Catholics helped found the Council of Christians and Jews (1942); parishes take part in Inter Faith Week each November.
  • Dialogue also includes people of no religion: Pope Benedict XVI invited dialogue with non-believers (the "Courtyard of the Gentiles", 2011).
Mission 5

Evangelising in a diverse Britain

  • Catholics believe the Church is missionary: called to share the Gospel with everyone.
  • Pope Francis taught that the Church grows by attraction, through joy and service, not by pressure.
  • Benefits in Britain: freedom of religion protects sharing faith; Catholic schools, charities and parishes reach many people; migrants (e.g. from Poland, the Philippines and Nigeria) have renewed many parishes.
  • Challenges: the census shows fewer Christians and many more people of no religion; many are indifferent rather than hostile; respect for other faiths must be kept; secular culture can see faith as private.
  • Dialogue and evangelising go together: Catholics share their faith while respecting the freedom and beliefs of others.
Quick check
?A Catholic parish joins a local mosque and synagogue to run a food bank together. Which form of dialogue is this?
Mission 5Evaluate

"Britain is no longer a Christian country."

  • Reasons for: in 2021 fewer than half of people in England and Wales said "Christian"; "no religion" is growing fast; church attendance is much lower than the census figure; Britain is now diverse in religion and belief.
  • Reasons against: Christian is still the largest answer; the monarch, Parliament, laws, festivals and many schools remain rooted in Christianity; many who rarely attend still identify as Christian; Christian charities and churches are active in every community.
  • Another view: some say Britain has Christian roots and a diverse present, so the answer depends on what "Christian country" means.
  • Judge it by: Is a "Christian country" about what people believe, how laws and customs began, or what the state does? Do census answers measure belief or identity?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Michelangelo's Creation of Adam (Sistine Chapel, c. 1511–12): God as creator; life as God's gift; humans in God's image (Genesis 1:27); how far it fits Catholic belief is debated.

Mission 2

  • The cross is the central Christian symbol: plain (risen Christ) or crucifix (sacrifice); altar, table and different crosses emphasise different aspects of belief.
  • Chi-Rho = Christ; Alpha–Omega = Christ eternal.
  • Dove = Spirit; man, lion, ox and eagle = the four evangelists.
  • San Clemente's mosaic shows the cross as the tree of life: Christ the New Adam restores what Adam lost.

Mission 3

  • CST applies love of neighbour to society: dignity (imago Dei), common good, solidarity, option for the poor, care for creation, peace.
  • GS 29: equal dignity in God's image; GS 78: peace is the work of justice and love.

Mission 4

  • CAFOD (1962) puts CST into action globally through partners: emergencies, development, campaigning; help regardless of religion.
  • Inter-faith dialogue in Britain seeks understanding, respect and harmony with other faiths and with Humanists and atheists.

Mission 5

  • Britain's religious traditions are mainly Christian, but religions and beliefs are diverse, including atheism and humanism.
  • Census (England and Wales): Christian 71.7% (2001), 59.3% (2011), 46.2% (2021); no religion rose from 14.8% to 37.2%.
  • The Catholic Church promotes inter-faith dialogue (life, action, theological exchange, religious experience) while sharing its faith.
