Mission 1

Mission 1: Your nerve cells and muscle cells carry the same genes. So why are they so different?

Make a guess: Your nerve cells and muscle cells carry the same genes. So why are they so different?

Mission 1

One cell becomes hundreds of cell types

  • You began as one cell: a zygote.
  • Mitosis made many genetically identical, unspecialised cells.
  • Differentiation: each cell becomes specialised, with a structure suited to one function.
  • A nerve cell and a muscle cell keep the same genes.
  • They differ in which genes are switched on.
  • Visible differentiation comes from expressing genes for tissue-specific proteins.
  • Say: "different genes are expressed" — not: "different genes are kept".
Quick check
?In a human, a neuron and a liver cell are both descended by mitosis from the same zygote. What is the difference between them once they have differentiated?
Mission 1

Cells → tissues → organs → systems

  • Tissue: a group of specialised cells working together to perform the same function.
  • Organ: several tissues grouped together to perform an overall function.
  • Organ system: organs working together, e.g. the digestive system.
  • Stomach (organ): epithelium + muscle + connective tissue.
  • Leaf (organ): epidermis + mesophyll + xylem + phloem.
  • A tissue can hold more than one cell type, e.g. ciliated cells and goblet cells in airway epithelium.
Quick check
?The wall of the stomach contains epithelium, smooth muscle and connective tissue. Together they digest and churn food. Which statement is correct?
Mission check

?In a hypothetical species of plant, leaf cells make large amounts of a protein that binds chlorophyll, but the root cells of the same plant make none of it. Which statement best explains this difference?

Same genes in every cell; different genes switched on. That is what makes a leaf cell, a root cell, a nerve cell or a muscle cell.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Some of your cells have no nucleus and some have hundreds. How does a cell's build match its job?

Make a guess: Some of your cells have no nucleus and some have hundreds. How does a cell's build match its job?

Mission 2

Read a cell by its organelles

  • Many mitochondria → lots of ATP: for active transport or movement.
  • Lots of RER and Golgi → making and secreting proteins.
  • Microvilli or folded membranes → large surface area.
  • Many lysosomes → digesting engulfed material.
  • Many chloroplasts → photosynthesis.
  • Say: "mitochondria release energy in respiration, making ATP" — not: "mitochondria make energy".
Mission 2

Specialised plant cells

  • Palisade cell: column-shaped, packed with chloroplasts, near the upper surface → absorbs most light.
  • Root hair cell: long hair → large surface area for water and mineral ion uptake. Many mitochondria → ATP for active transport of mineral ions.
  • Guard cells: a pair around each stoma. Take in water → swell → bend apart → stoma opens.
  • Water enters a root hair by osmosis, not active transport.
Quick check
?Root hair cells have no chloroplasts but contain many mitochondria. What is the best explanation for the many mitochondria?
Mission 2

The ileum: an organ in layers

  • From the lumen outwards: mucosa → muscularis mucosa → submucosa → muscularis externa → serosa.
  • Mucosa: villi raise surface area. Columnar epithelium with microvilli (brush border) and many mitochondria.
  • Mucosa: goblet cells secrete mucus. Crypts of Lieberkühn: Paneth cells protect dividing stem cells at the base.
  • Each villus: blood capillaries take amino acids and monosaccharides; a lacteal takes fats.
  • Absorption by diffusion, active transport and pinocytosis.
  • Muscularis mucosa moves the villi → better contact with digested food.
  • Submucosa: blood and lymph vessels. Muscularis externa: peristalsis, pendular movements, local constrictions that churn food. Serosa: protects and supports.
Mission 2

The mesophytic leaf: light in, water kept

  • Large, thin leaf → large surface area for light.
  • Upper epidermis with waxy cuticle → conserves water.
  • Palisade mesophyll: packed cells, many chloroplasts → maximum light absorption.
  • Spongy mesophyll: loose cells, air spaces → gases diffuse.
  • Xylem brings water and ions; phloem carries sugars away.
  • Lower epidermis with cuticle; most stomata here, between guard cells. Stomata close at night → less water loss.
  • The leaf balances maximum photosynthesis against minimum transpiration.
Quick check
?In a mesophytic leaf, which statement correctly links a tissue to its role?
Mission check

?Cells lining the first part of a kidney tubule take glucose back from the filtrate into the blood. Under the electron microscope they show a dense border of microvilli and very many mitochondria. What do these features suggest about how the cells work?

A cell's shape, size and parts are clues to its job.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Food in your gut is still outside your body. So when does a meal actually get inside you?

Make a guess: Food in your gut is still outside your body. So when does a meal actually get inside you?

Mission 3

A tube through the body

  • Holozoic nutrition: food is taken in and digested inside the body.
  • Four stages: ingestion → digestion → absorption → assimilation. Undigested waste is egested.
  • The gut is a tube open at both ends. Its lumen is still outside the body's cells.
  • Digestion: enzymes hydrolyse large, insoluble molecules into small, soluble ones.
  • Absorption: those small molecules cross the gut lining into the blood or lymph. Only now are they inside.
  • Assimilation: cells build the absorbed molecules into the animal's own molecules and tissues.
Quick check
?A dog eats meat. At which stage are the amino acids from the meat first inside the dog's body?

Mission 3

The mucosa

  • Villi: finger-like folds of the mucosa. They greatly increase the surface area for absorption.
  • Columnar epithelium covers each villus. Each cell has microvilli (a brush border).
  • Goblet cells in the epithelium secrete mucus.
  • Inside each villus: blood capillaries and a lacteal.
  • Between villi: crypts of Lieberkühn. Stem cells at their base divide to replace the epithelium.
  • Paneth cells at the crypt base secrete antimicrobial substances. They protect the dividing stem cells.
  • Say: "villi are folds of the whole mucosa; microvilli are folds of one cell's membrane" — not: "villi are on the epithelial cells".
Mission 3

How the epithelium absorbs

  • Brush border of microvilli: a huge membrane area.
  • Many mitochondria: ATP for active transport.
  • Three mechanisms:
    • diffusion: down a concentration gradient;
    • active transport: against a gradient, using ATP;
    • pinocytosis: fluid taken in by small vesicles.
  • Amino acids and monosaccharides → blood capillaries.
  • Fats → lacteals (lymph).
Mission 3

Moving food along

  • Peristalsis: a wave of contraction of the circular muscle behind the food, relaxation in front. It pushes food one way.
  • It works without gravity: you can swallow upside down.
  • Muscularis externa: peristalsis, pendular movements and local constrictions that churn food.
  • Muscularis mucosa: moves the villi, improving contact with the products of digestion.
  • Serosa: protects and supports the gut.
Mission 3Practical

Drawing the ileum

  • Practical: examine stained ileum sections; draw a block diagram of its tissue layers.
  • Use the low-power objective to find the whole wall; high power to check a layer.
  • A block diagram shows where tissues are, not individual cells.
  • Draw each layer's outline in proportion. Clear, continuous lines; no shading.
  • Label with ruled lines that do not cross. Add a title and the magnification.
Quick check
?Which structures are found on the surface of individual epithelial cells lining the ileum?
Mission check

?A person eats celery. Humans make no enzyme that digests cellulose. One cellulose fibre from the celery passes along the whole gut and leaves in the faeces. Which statement about this fibre is correct?

A meal gets inside you only when its molecules cross the gut lining. Before that, it is still in a tube that runs through you.

Mission 4

  • The leaf is broad and thin: a large area for light, and a short path for gases.
Mission 4

How carbon dioxide gets in

  • The cuticle is almost impermeable. Gases enter and leave through stomata.
  • CO2 diffuses through the air spaces of the spongy mesophyll to every mesophyll cell.
  • It dissolves in the moist cell surfaces, then diffuses into the chloroplasts.
  • The mesophyll gives a huge, moist internal surface. No cell is far from an air space.
  • Photosynthesis keeps using CO2, so the gradient into the leaf stays steep. No ventilation is needed.
  • Say: "CO2 diffuses in through the stomata and air spaces" — not: "the veins carry CO2 into the leaf".
Quick check
?Which adaptation of a leaf allows carbon dioxide to reach the photosynthesising cells inside it?
Mission 4

Opening and closing a stoma

  • (1) Guard cells take up water by osmosis and become turgid.
  • (1) Their inner wall, next to the pore, is thicker. Swelling bends each cell outward: the stoma opens.
  • (2) Guard cells lose water and become flaccid: the stoma closes.
  • Stomata usually open in the light, letting CO2 in for photosynthesis.
  • They close in the dark and when water is short, reducing water loss.
  • Say: "the guard cells become turgid" — not: "the guard cells contract".
Mission 4

Net gas exchange by day and night

  • Respiration runs all the time: O2 in, CO2 out.
  • Photosynthesis runs only in light: CO2 in, O2 out.
  • Bright light (midday): photosynthesis faster than respiration → net CO2 uptake and O2 release.
  • Dark (night): respiration only → net CO2 release and O2 uptake.
  • Compensation point: the light intensity at which photosynthesis and respiration are equal. No net gas exchange.
Mission 4

The trade-off: gas in, water out

  • Open stomata let CO2 in, but water vapour diffuses out down its gradient: transpiration.
  • A mesophytic leaf limits this: waxy cuticle on both epidermal layers; most stomata in the lower epidermis; stomata close at night.
  • Say: "water vapour diffuses out while the stomata are open" — not: "the plant opens its stomata in order to lose water".
Quick check
?Marram grass grows on sand dunes. In dry wind its leaves roll up. Its stomata lie on the inner surface, in pits among hairs. How does rolling reduce water loss?
Mission 4Practical

Drawing a low-power plan

  • A plan diagram shows tissue layers as outlined regions. No individual cells.
  • This is a block diagram of the leaf section.
  • Sharp pencil; clear, continuous lines; no shading; layers in correct proportion; ruled label lines.
  • On sections, identify epidermal layers, waxy cuticles, palisade and spongy mesophyll, chloroplasts, xylem, phloem, guard cells and stomata.
Mission check

?A water lily's leaves float flat on the surface of a pond. Where would you expect most of its stomata to be?

A leaf takes in CO2 only where its stomata meet air, and every open stoma also lets water vapour out. Where the stomata sit is part of that trade-off.

Mission 5

Mission 5: A plant takes in carbon dioxide and water and gives out oxygen. Which of the two did the oxygen come from?

Make a guess: A plant takes in carbon dioxide and water and gives out oxygen. Which of the two did the oxygen come from?

Mission 5

Photosynthesis in one line

  • carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen
  • 6CO2 + 6H2O → C6H12O6 + 6O2 (light energy, absorbed by chlorophyll)
  • Light energy becomes chemical energy in the carbon compounds made.
  • Energy is transformed, not created.
  • Say: "photosynthesis transforms light energy" — not: "plants make energy".
Mission 5

Water is split for its hydrogen

  • CO2 has no hydrogen. Glucose has 12 H atoms. They come from water.
  • Light energy is used to split water.
  • Its hydrogen (as protons and electrons) reduces CO2 to glucose.
  • The oxygen left over is a by-product: it is released.
  • CO2's carbon and oxygen end up in glucose and in some new water, not in the O2.
  • Plants, algae and cyanobacteria (prokaryotes) all split water and release O2.
  • Say: "the oxygen released comes from water" — not: "the plant keeps the carbon of CO2 and releases its oxygen".

Mission 5

Inside a chloroplast

  • Envelope: outer and inner membrane.
  • Thylakoids: flattened membrane sacs. A stack is a granum (plural grana).
  • Lamellae (intergranal thylakoids) link the grana.
  • Stroma: the fluid around the thylakoids, inside the inner membrane.
  • Thylakoid membranes hold chlorophyll and electron carriers.
  • The chlorophyll is organised into two photosystems.
  • Light-dependent stage: thylakoid membranes. Light-independent stage: stroma.
  • Stroma also holds circular DNA, 70S ribosomes, starch grains and lipid droplets.
Mission 5

Built for the job

  • Huge thylakoid membrane area: room for many photosystems, carriers and ATP synthase.
  • Small volume inside each thylakoid: protons pumped in build a steep gradient fast.
  • Stroma: Calvin cycle enzymes and substrates concentrated together, right next to the thylakoids that supply ATP and reduced NADP.
  • DNA and ribosomes: the chloroplast makes some of its own proteins.
  • Starch grains: sugar stored where it is made.
Quick check
?Which statement correctly matches an adaptation of the chloroplast to its function in photosynthesis?
Mission 5

The leaf: built to trap light

  • Upper epidermis with waxy cuticle: transparent; cuts water loss.
  • Palisade mesophyll: tall, tightly packed cells near the top surface. Most chloroplasts are here, for maximum light absorption.
  • Spongy mesophyll: loosely packed cells, air spaces for gas diffusion.
  • Stomata (mostly in the lower epidermis): CO2 in, O2 and water vapour out.
  • Xylem (in veins) brings water and inorganic ions; phloem carries sugars away.
  • Broad, thin leaf: large surface area, short diffusion path.
Mission check

?Chloroplasts from a hypothetical alga are broken open and separated into two fractions: the thylakoid membranes, and the stroma with no thylakoids. Each fraction is placed in the light with water, ¹⁴C-labelled CO2, ADP, inorganic phosphate, and a dye that accepts electrons in place of NADP. No ATP or reduced NADP (NADPH) is added. Which results are predicted?

The O2 comes from water split in the thylakoid membranes; the carbon from CO2 is built into sugar in the stroma.

Mission 6

Mission 6: A green leaf also hides orange and yellow pigments. How can you reveal them, and what are they for?

Make a guess: A green leaf also hides orange and yellow pigments. How can you reveal them, and what are they for?

Mission 6

One leaf, several pigments

  • Chloroplasts hold several photosynthetic pigments:
  • Chlorophyll a: blue-green.
  • Chlorophyll b: yellow-green.
  • Carotenoids: carotene (orange) and xanthophylls (yellow).
  • Chlorophyll a is the primary pigment. The others are accessory pigments.
  • Each pigment absorbs a different set of wavelengths, so together they use more of the spectrum.
Mission 6

Absorbing light

  • A pigment absorbs a photon only if its energy matches the gap to an excited state.
  • Then an electron is raised to a higher energy level. This energy is later turned into chemical energy.
  • Chlorophyll absorbs blue (about 430–450 nm) and red (about 640–680 nm) strongly.
  • It absorbs little green (about 500–600 nm): green is reflected or transmitted, so leaves look green.
  • Absorption spectrum: absorbance against wavelength (nm), for one pigment.
  • Say: "leaves look green because green light is reflected" — not: "chlorophyll absorbs green light".
Quick check
?The absorption spectrum of chlorophyll a has peaks at about 430 nm (blue) and 660 nm (red) but a trough at about 550 nm (green). Which statement best explains this pattern?
Mission 6

Action spectrum

  • Action spectrum: rate of photosynthesis against wavelength.
  • Rate = O2 produced, or CO2 taken in, per unit time.
  • Similar to the absorption spectra: peaks in blue and red, trough in green.
  • Different: the action spectrum is not zero in green. Accessory pigments absorb some green and pass the energy on.
  • Absorption spectrum: one pigment, light absorbed. Action spectrum: whole leaf, photosynthesis done.
  • Engelmann (1882) lit a filament of alga with a spectrum: oxygen-seeking bacteria crowded in the blue and red zones.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A leaf sealed in a chamber was illuminated with light of one wavelength at a time and the fall in carbon dioxide concentration in the chamber was recorded: at 450 nm the concentration fell by 80 ppm in 4 minutes; at 550 nm by 30 ppm in 5 minutes; at 650 nm by 90 ppm in 6 minutes; at 700 nm by 20 ppm in 2 minutes. Which statement about these results is correct?
Mission 6

Practical: separating the pigments

  • Practical 5.2.6: paper chromatography of plant pigments; calculate Rf.
  • Grind leaf with sand and solvent (e.g. propanone). Spot the extract on a pencil origin line; dry; repeat to concentrate.
  • Stand in solvent below the origin, in a sealed container. Remove before the front reaches the top; mark the front at once.
  • Pigments separate by solubility in the solvent versus attraction to the stationary phase.
  • Rf = distance from origin to spot centre ÷ distance from origin to solvent front.
  • Identify by Rf and colour against reference values for the same solvent and stationary phase.
Mission 6

Worked example: Rf

Illustrative data. Distances from the origin line: solvent front 82 mm.

1. Orange spot: 77 mm. Rf = 77 ÷ 82 = 0.94.

2. Blue-green spot: 49 mm. Rf = 49 ÷ 82 = 0.60.

3. Yellow-green spot: 33 mm. Rf = 33 ÷ 82 = 0.40.

  • Rf has no units and is always less than 1.
  • Measure from the origin, not the paper edge; to the centre of the spot.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. On a paper chromatogram of leaf pigments, the origin line was 20 mm from the bottom edge of the paper, the centre of the carotene spot was 65 mm from the bottom edge and the solvent front was 70 mm from the bottom edge. What is the Rf value of carotene?
Mission check

?Illustrative data. Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Differentiation: cells with the same genes become specialised by expressing different genes.
  • Specialised cells form tissues; tissues form organs; organs form systems.

Mission 2

  • A cell's shape, size and organelles match its function.
  • Many mitochondria → active transport or movement; microvilli → large surface area; chloroplasts → photosynthesis.
  • The ileum and the leaf are organs built of tissue layers, each with its own role.

Mission 3

  • Holozoic nutrition: ingest, digest (extracellular hydrolysis in the lumen), absorb across the gut lining, assimilate.
  • Ileum: mucosa, muscularis mucosa, submucosa, muscularis externa, serosa; villi, microvilli, crypts, lacteals.

Mission 4

  • CO2 enters through stomata and diffuses through air spaces to a large, moist mesophyll surface; guard cells open (turgid) and close (flaccid) the stomata.
  • Open stomata also lose water vapour; leaves, and xerophytes even more, balance gas exchange against water loss.
  • At the compensation point, photosynthesis equals respiration: no net gas exchange.

Mission 5

  • Photosynthesis transforms light energy into chemical energy: water is split, its hydrogen reduces CO2 to sugar, and its oxygen is released.
  • Light-dependent stage (light reactions): thylakoid membranes. Light-independent stage (Calvin cycle): stroma.

Mission 6

  • Chromatography separates leaf pigments; identify each by Rf (origin to spot ÷ origin to front) and colour, against references for the same solvent.
  • Pigments absorb only wavelengths whose energy matches an electron jump: mainly blue and red. Accessory pigments widen the range used.
  • Absorption spectrum: light absorbed by a pigment. Action spectrum: rate of photosynthesis at each wavelength.
