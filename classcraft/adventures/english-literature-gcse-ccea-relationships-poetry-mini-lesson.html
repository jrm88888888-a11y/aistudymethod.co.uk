Mission 1

A father calls his hope a 'sin'. Why blame himself for loving?

Make a guess: A father calls his hope a 'sin'. Why blame himself for loving?

Relationships poetry · On My First SonScreen 1

The poem in context

  • Ben Jonson (1572–1637) was a poet and playwright. His first son, Benjamin, died of the plague in 1603, aged seven.
  • The poem is one of Jonson's Epigrams, printed in his Workes (1616) under the title "On My First Sonne".
  • The Hebrew name Benjamin is usually explained as son of the right hand, so line 1 translates the boy's name.
  • Christians of the time were taught to accept a death as God's will, so heavy grief could feel like a fault.

What the father says

Relationships poetry · On My First SonScreen 5

Form: an epitaph in couplets

  • Twelve lines of rhyming couplets in iambic pentameter: ten syllables, five stresses.
  • An epigram (short, polished poem) and an elegy (poem of mourning) at once; lines 9–10 work like words carved on a gravestone.
  • Caesuras (mid-line pauses) break the regular lines: "O, could I lose all father now. For why". The neat form holds the grief, but only just.
  • Lines 5–8 ask rhetorical questions to argue himself out of mourning.

Mission checkScreen 9

?A father calls his hope a 'sin'. Why blame himself for loving?

Jonson calls his hope a "sin" because he loved his son as if he could keep him, forgetting that the boy was only "lent". The loan metaphor helps him try to accept the death, but the broken lines and the final vow show grief breaking through.

Mission 2

Her eyes are nothing like the sun. Love poem or insult?

Make a guess: Her eyes are nothing like the sun. Love poem or insult?

Relationships poetry · Sonnet 130Screen 1

The poem in context

  • Printed in 1609 with Shakespeare's other sonnets; most of Sonnets 127–152 are about a woman readers call the Dark Lady.
  • The Italian poet Petrarch (1304–1374) made the love sonnet famous. Elizabethan poets copied his conventions: the beloved's eyes like the sun, lips like coral, skin like snow, cheeks like roses.
  • A blazon praises a woman part by part. Sonnet 130 runs through a blazon, then refuses every comparison.
  • Mistress here means the woman he loves.

Relationships poetry · Sonnet 130Screen 4

Form: the twist at the end

  • A Shakespearean sonnet: three quatrains and a couplet, abab cdcd efef gg, in iambic pentameter.
  • Each quatrain works through conventions: eyes, lips, skin, hair; cheeks and breath; voice and walk.
  • The volta is held back to the couplet, "And yet", so the reader waits twelve lines for the love.
  • Plain statements ("I grant", "well I know") make the speaker sound honest, not flowery.

Mission checkScreen 8

?Her eyes are nothing like the sun. Love poem or insult?

Sonnet 130 is a love poem that mocks other love poems. It denies every flattering comparison (sun, coral, snow, roses, music, a goddess), then turns in the couplet: his love is as rare as any woman "belied with false compare". Readers still debate how teasing the tone is, so weigh both sides.

Mission 3

She promises to 'count the ways'. Can love really be measured?

Make a guess: She promises to 'count the ways'. Can love really be measured?

Barrett Browning · Sonnet 43Screen 1

The poem in brief

  • Elizabeth Barrett wrote Sonnets from the Portuguese in 1845–46, while Robert Browning was courting her.
  • They married secretly on 12 September 1846; her father never forgave her.
  • She first showed him the sonnets in 1849, and they were published in her Poems (1850).
  • The title made them look like translations: Robert's pet name for her was "my little Portuguese".

Barrett Browning · Sonnet 43Screen 6

Form: a Petrarchan sonnet

  • 14 lines of iambic pentameter: an octave rhyming ABBAABBA (ways / height / sight / Grace…) and a sestet rhyming CDCDCD.
  • The octave covers the soul, daily life and goodness. From line 9 the volta turns to her own life: "old griefs", "lost saints".
  • Enjambment, "height / My soul can reach", lets love spill over the line end.
  • The sonnet was traditionally a man's love poem; here a woman speaks her love. "If God choose" adds humility to the final promise.

?She promises to 'count the ways'. Can love really be measured?

The sonnet sets up a count, then breaks it: each "I love thee" adds a new dimension, from space and daily life to goodness, grief, faith and finally life after death. Measuring her love shows that it has no limit.

Mission 4

He'd court her for centuries. So why won't he wait at all?

Make a guess: He'd court her for centuries. So why won't he wait at all?

Relationships poetry · To His Coy MistressScreen 1

The poem in context

  • Andrew Marvell (1621–1678) probably wrote the poem in the early 1650s; it was published after his death, in Miscellaneous Poems (1681).
  • He is grouped with the metaphysical poets, known for clever arguments and startling comparisons (conceits).
  • It is a carpe diem poem: Latin for "seize the day", a phrase from the Roman poet Horace.
  • Coy means shy or holding back. The speaker tries to persuade a woman to return his love now.

  • It works like a logical argument: a premise, a problem, a conclusion.
  • Written in rhyming couplets of iambic tetrameter (eight syllables, four stresses): quick and witty.

Mission checkScreen 9

?He'd court her for centuries. So why won't he wait at all?

The centuries of courtship belong to the "If" part of his argument: a time they will never have. Time's chariot and the silent grave show life is short, so he concludes with carpe diem: since they can't stop time, they should make the most of it. Readers can debate how persuasive, or how self-serving, the argument is.

Mission 5

She's buying poison. Why does she want to watch it being made?

Make a guess: She's buying poison. Why does she want to watch it being made?

Relationships · "The Laboratory"Screen 1

A poisoner's monologue

  • Robert Browning (1812–1889) published "The Laboratory" in Hood's Magazine in June 1844.
  • The subtitle, Ancien Régime, means the "old order": France before the 1789 Revolution, a world of royal courts, dances and intrigue.
  • It is a dramatic monologue: one character speaks, and we never hear the reply.
  • The speaker is a woman at court. Her silent listener is an old apothecary (a maker of medicines) mixing a poison for her rival.

What happens in the rest of the poem

StanzasWhat happens
4–5She admires the apothecary's poisons and imagines hiding poison in jewellery.
6She imagines poisoning two other women at court, Pauline and Elise.
7–8She asks for the poison to look pleasant. Her rival is not small like her, which she thinks is how "she ensnared him".
9–10Last night she tried to make her rival fall just by staring. Now she wants the death to be felt, so her lover will remember it.

  • Nobody is harmed during the poem: we hear only her plans. Browning lets her words judge her.
Relationships · "The Laboratory"Screen 5

A dance tune for a murder

  • The poem has 12 stanzas of rhyming couplets (AABB) with four strong beats a line.
  • The rhythm skips quickly, like a dance: "Grind away, moisten and mash up thy paste". This light sound clashes with the deadly subject.
  • Exclamations, questions and dashes show her excitement and impatience.
  • She links beauty and death: "the exquisite blue" phial is "Sure to taste sweetly".
  • Her real aim is her lover: "He is sure to remember her dying face!"

Mission checkScreen 9

?She's buying poison. Why does she want to watch it being made?

She has been mocked by her lover and her rival, so the laboratory gives her the power she lacks at court. She lingers over each step because the revenge must be hers, and she wants her lover to "remember her dying face". Browning lets her own words reveal how jealousy has twisted love.

Mission 6

She begs to be remembered, then says forget her. Why?

Make a guess: She begs to be remembered, then says forget her. Why?

Relationships · "Remember"Screen 1

A sonnet about being remembered

  • Christina Rossetti (1830–1894) wrote "Remember" in 1849, aged 18.
  • It was published in 1862 in her first collection, Goblin Market and Other Poems.
  • Her Christian faith was central to her life. Belief in an afterlife helps explain why death is pictured as going away, not as an ending.
  • The speaker addresses a loved one, perhaps a fiancé ("our future that you planned"), about how to remember her after she dies.
Extract Relationships · "Remember"Screen 2

"Remember" (the whole sonnet)

Relationships · "Remember"Screen 3

Two halves, two requests

  • "Remember" is a Petrarchan sonnet: an octave (8 lines) and a sestet (6 lines).
  • Rhyme scheme: ABBAABBA CDDECE.
  • The octave repeats one request: remember me.
  • The volta (turn) comes with "Yet" in line 9. The sestet changes the request: if you forget for a while, "do not grieve".
  • The last two lines weigh being forgotten against being remembered with sadness.
Relationships · "Remember"Screen 4

Gentle words for death

  • Euphemism: death is "gone away" into "the silent land", a quiet journey rather than an ending.
  • Physical closeness is lost: "When you can no more hold me by the hand".
  • "Nor I half turn to go yet turning stay" catches the hesitation of a parting.
  • Repetition: "remember" appears five times, a plea that grows softer.
  • "the darkness and corruption" is the one harsh image: the body's decay, which some readers also link to sin.

?She begs to be remembered, then says forget her. Why?

The octave asks to be remembered again and again. At the volta, love turns selfless: "Better by far you should forget and smile / Than that you should remember and be sad." Readers argue about how calm she really is, but the change itself is deliberate.

Mission 7

He pictures her old and alone. Love poem or warning?

Make a guess: He pictures her old and alone. Love poem or warning?

Relationships · "When You Are Old"Screen 1

A young poet imagines old age

  • W. B. Yeats (1865–1939), the Irish poet, wrote the poem in 1891, in his mid-twenties.
  • It was published in 1892 in The Countess Kathleen and Various Legends and Lyrics.
  • It is thought to be addressed to Maud Gonne, whom Yeats loved and who refused to marry him.
  • It loosely adapts a 16th-century French sonnet by Pierre de Ronsard, Quand vous serez bien vieille (When you are very old).
Extract Relationships · "When You Are Old"Screen 2

"When You Are Old" (the whole poem)

Relationships · "When You Are Old"Screen 3

Speaking to the future

  • The speaker addresses "you" in the second person, and gives a series of imperatives: "take down this book", "read", "dream", "Murmur".
  • "this book" is the poem's own book: he imagines her reading his words when she is old.
  • Stanza 2 sets "many" lovers against "one man". They loved her "beauty"; he loved something deeper than her looks.
  • Stanza 3 ends with Love leaving and hiding among the stars.

Relationships · "When You Are Old"Screen 5

A slow, controlled form

  • Three quatrains, each rhymed ABBA; the lines are mostly iambic pentameter, slow and even.
  • The whole poem is one long sentence, joined by "And": it moves gently, like her slow reading.
  • Contrast: youth ("the soft look / Your eyes had once") against age ("full of sleep").
  • Personification: Love "fled", "paced upon the mountains" and "hid his face". Love becomes distant and out of reach.

Mission checkScreen 9

?He pictures her old and alone. Love poem or warning?

It is a love poem: he loved "the pilgrim soul in you" when others loved only her beauty. It is also a gentle warning: in old age she will read this book and regret that Love "fled". The strongest answers argue that both are true and show how the tender tone carries the reproach.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Jonson's son Benjamin died of plague in 1603, aged seven; the poem appeared in his Epigrams (Workes, 1616).
  • The loan metaphor ("lent", "pay", "Exacted") tries to make the death fair; the caesuras and the final vow show grief breaking through.
  • The epitaph calls the boy Jonson's "best piece of poetry": fatherhood above fame.
  • Sonnet 130 (1609) works through a blazon of Petrarchan comparisons and denies each one.
  • The volta is held back to the couplet ("And yet by heaven"), which declares a love that needs no flattery.
  • The poem is about honest love versus false poetry, not about the woman's looks.
  • Written during Elizabeth Barrett's courtship with Robert Browning (1845–46) and published in 1850; a woman speaks her own love in a form once used by men.
  • Anaphora ("I love thee") and a list of "ways" (space, daily life, goodness, past grief, death) show a love too large to count.
  • A Petrarchan sonnet: octave and sestet, a volta to her own life at line 9, and a final promise beyond death.
  • Your anthology is open on the desk: mark the "ways" in your head, and compare methods, not just topics.
  • "To His Coy Mistress" (probably early 1650s, published 1681) is a persuasive carpe diem poem in rhyming couplets.
  • Its argument has three steps: If (endless time), But (time's chariot, the grave), Therefore (love now).
  • Key methods: hyperbole, personified Time, the contrast between "vast eternity" and a short life, and the final sun image.
  • "The Laboratory" (1844) is a dramatic monologue: a jealous woman at a French court watches an apothecary mix poison for her rival.
  • A quick, dance-like rhythm and images that join beauty and death show how easily she mixes murder with court life.
  • Pair it in Section B with poems about love, loss or jealousy, comparing methods as well as ideas.
  • "Remember" (1849, published 1862) is a Petrarchan sonnet in which a speaker imagines her death and asks her lover to remember her.
  • The volta at "Yet" turns the request around: his happiness matters more than her memory.
  • Pair it with poems about love, loss or remembering.
  • "When You Are Old" (1892) imagines a woman in old age reading the poem and remembering the one man who truly loved her.
  • Three slow ABBA quatrains, imperatives and the personification of Love make it both tender and quietly reproachful.
  • Pair it in Section B with poems about love, memory, ageing or rejection.

Key quotations to learn

  • Farewell, thou child of my right hand, and joy, (line 1) Translates "Benjamin"; tender farewell.
  • My sin was too much hope of thee, loved boy; (line 2) Self-blame: hope as a "sin".
  • Seven years th' wert lent to me, and I thee pay, (line 3) The loan metaphor.
  • O, could I lose all father now. (line 5) Grief breaking the form.
  • Ben Jonson his best piece of poetry; (line 10) The son as his finest creation.
  • As what he loves may never like too much. (line 12) The vow: fear of loving again.
  • My mistress’ eyes are nothing like the sun; (line 1) The reversed simile that sets up the poem.
  • Coral is far more red, than her lips red: (line 2) A Petrarchan convention undercut.
  • I love to hear her speak, yet well I know (line 9) Love admitted, even in a "negative" line.
  • My mistress, when she walks, treads on the ground: (line 12) A real woman, not a goddess.
  • And yet by heaven, I think my love as rare, (line 13) The volta: love declared.
  • As any she belied with false compare. (line 14) The target is false comparison.
  • How do I love thee? Let me count the ways. (Line 1) The question and the promise to count.
  • I love thee to the depth and breadth and height (Line 2) Love measured as space, in every direction.
  • Most quiet need, by sun and candlelight. (Line 6) Love in everyday life, day and night.
  • I love thee freely, as men strive for Right; (Line 7) Simile: love as chosen and morally good.
  • In my old griefs, and with my childhood’s faith. (Line 10) After the volta: her own past.
  • I shall but love thee better after death. (Line 14) The ending goes beyond life.
  • Had we but world enough, and time, (line 1) The "If" that starts the argument.
  • My vegetable love should grow / Vaster than empires, and more slow; (lines 11–12) Hyperbole: impossibly slow courtship.
  • Time’s winged chariot hurrying near; (line 22) Personified Time in pursuit.
  • Deserts of vast eternity. (line 24) Death as empty endlessness.
  • The grave’s a fine and private place, (line 31) Dark irony: death ends love.
  • Stand still, yet we will make him run. (line 46) Carpe diem conclusion.
  • Which is the poison to poison her, prithee? (line 4) Her single purpose, asked politely.
  • they believe my tears flow / While they laugh, laugh at me (lines 6–7) Humiliation drives her revenge.
  • I am not in haste! (line 10) She wants to watch every step.
  • To carry pure death in an earring, a casket, (line 19) Death joined to court fashion.
  • He is sure to remember her dying face! (line 40) The revenge is really aimed at her lover.
  • next moment I dance at the King's! (line 48) She returns to court life at once.
  • Remember me when I am gone away, (line 1) The first request; death as going away.
  • Gone far away into the silent land; (line 2) Euphemism: death as a quiet place.
  • Nor I half turn to go yet turning stay. (line 4) Hesitation at parting.
  • You tell me of our future that you planned: (line 6) The future the couple has lost.
  • the darkness and corruption (line 11) The one harsh image of death.
  • Better by far you should forget and smile (line 13) The selfless reversal.
  • When you are old and grey and full of sleep, (line 1) The imagined future, slow and quiet.
  • take down this book, (line 2) She will read the poem itself.
  • How many loved your moments of glad grace, (line 5) The many who loved her beauty.
  • But one man loved the pilgrim soul in you, (line 7) The one true love, for her inner self.
  • Murmur, a little sadly, how Love fled (line 10) Regret, gently stated.
  • And hid his face amid a crowd of stars. (line 12) Love personified, now out of reach.
