Mission 1: Air bubbles trapped in Antarctic ice are up to 800,000 years old. What can they tell us about past climate?

Mission 1

Why we need proxies

  • Thermometer records cover only the last century or two. Older climates are read from proxy data: natural records that respond to climate.
  • Historical documents reach back centuries: harvest dates for grapes were used to reconstruct summer temperatures in Paris "from 1370 to 1879".
  • Long-term proxies (hundreds of thousands to millions of years): ice cores, ocean-floor deposits.
  • Medium-term proxies (thousands of years): pollen, lake sediments, tree rings.
  • Pollen in lake sediments records the vegetation that grew nearby, and so the conditions that suited it.
Mission 1

Ice cores

  • Snow builds up in layers that "contain dust, air bubbles, or isotopes of oxygen".
  • The ice "encloses small bubbles of air that contain a sample of the atmosphere": a direct record of past greenhouse gases.
  • Temperature comes from "the ratios of different water isotopes": less heavy oxygen in the ice "means that temperatures were cooler".
  • Oldest continuous record: EPICA Dome C, Antarctica, back 800,000 years.
Mission 1

What the ice shows

  • CO₂ was "low … during cold times, and high … during warm periods".
  • Glacials were "interspersed roughly every 100,000 years" by warmer interglacials.
  • In the ice-age cycles of the last million years or so, CO₂ "didn't get any higher than 300 ppm". In 2025 it reached 425.7 ppm.
  • Methane and nitrous oxide are higher than at any time in at least 800,000 years.
Quick check
?A section of an Antarctic ice core has less heavy oxygen than the layers above and below it, and its air bubbles hold less CO₂. What does it most likely record?
Mission 1

Ocean-floor deposits

  • Deep-sea sediments hold the shells of tiny plankton, including foraminifera; their chemistry, and which species are present, record past oceans.
  • In glacials, light oxygen is locked up in ice sheets, so seawater, and shells grown in it, gain heavy oxygen; colder water adds more heavy oxygen too.
  • So a shell's oxygen-isotope ratio (δ¹⁸O) tracks ice volume and temperature together.
  • The LR04 stack combines δ¹⁸O records from 57 sites and covers 5.3 million years.
Mission 1

Pollen analysis

  • Pollen grains are "well preserved in the sediment layers in the bottom of a pond, lake, or ocean", and in peat bogs.
  • Counting each type in each layer shows what plants grew nearby, and so what climate suited them.
  • Layers are dated, often by radiocarbon: one review used 150 radiocarbon dates from 139 sites in Britain and Ireland.
  • Caution: plants respond to people and disease too. The elm decline in British and Irish pollen records began between 6,343 and 6,307 calibrated years BP; disease, climate and human activity were probably all involved.
Quick check
?Which proxy gives the longest continuous record?
Mission 1

The Pleistocene: glacials and interglacials

  • The Pleistocene (and Quaternary) begins 2.58 million years ago; the Holocene begins 0.0117 million years (11,700 years) ago.
  • Over the last 800,000 years, cold glacials were broken by warmer interglacials roughly every 100,000 years.
  • The last interglacial, about 125,000 years ago, had sea level likely 5–10 m higher than today.
  • Last Glacial Maximum: nearly all ice sheets were at their largest from 26,500 to about 19,000–20,000 years ago.
  • In Britain the last cold stage is the Devensian; repeated ice in its late part (26,000–15,000 years ago) shaped upland Wales.
Quick check
?How long ago did the Pleistocene begin, and roughly how often did glacials recur in the last 800,000 years?
Mission 1

End of the last glaciation and the Holocene

  • Northern deglaciation began 19,000–20,000 years ago, driven by rising northern summer sunlight.
  • Warming was not smooth: a last cold phase, the Younger Dryas (in Scotland, the Loch Lomond Stadial), ended abruptly.
  • The base of the Holocene is fixed at 11,700 years before AD 2000 in the NGRIP Greenland ice core, at the first sign of warming after the Younger Dryas.
  • The Holocene varied too: its most recent multi-century warm period was about 6,500 years ago.
Mission check

(Tundra plants grow in cold, open land; birch, pine and oak need progressively warmer conditions.) The top of the tundra band is dated to about 11,700 years ago. Ice cores, ocean-floor deposits and pollen record past climate at different timescales. Together they show glacial–interglacial cycles through the Pleistocene (from 2.58 million years ago), an abrupt last cold phase, and the start of the Holocene 11,700 years ago.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Ice ages came and went for millions of years before humans. What switched them on and off?

Make a guess: Ice ages came and went for millions of years before humans. What switched them on and off?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

  • A2-1.D1.i natural causes of climate change: astronomic (Milankovitch), solar, continental drift and volcanic
Mission 2

Four natural drivers

  • Climate changes when the energy reaching Earth, or the share kept, changes.
  • Four natural causes:
    • Astronomic: Earth's orbit and tilt (tens of thousands of years).
    • Solar: the Sun's output (years to centuries).
    • Continental drift: positions of land and oceans (millions of years).
    • Volcanic: eruptions (a few years).
  • Match the cause to the timescale of the change.
Mission 2

Milankovitch cycles

  • Eccentricity: the orbit's shape, from more circular to more elliptical, in a cycle of about 100,000 years.
  • Obliquity: the axis tilt, between 22.1° and 24.5° over the last million years; now 23.4°, in a cycle of about 41,000 years.
  • Precession: the wobble of the axis and orbit, about 23,000 years on average.
  • They change where and when sunlight falls, more than the yearly total.
Mission 2

How orbits trigger ice ages

  • Key factor: summer sunlight at high northern latitudes. Cool summers let snow survive and ice sheets grow.
  • Interglacials "began with a small increase in incoming sunlight in the Northern Hemisphere summer"; extra CO₂ "greatly amplified the initial, solar-driven warming".
  • Ice ages came every 41,000 years from 3 to 1 million years ago; about 800,000 years ago the cycle "lengthened to 100,000 years".
  • 1976: deep-sea cores showed the cycles "correspond with periods of major climate change over the past 450,000 years".
Quick check
?Why can a small change in orbit trigger a large change in climate?
Mission 2

Solar output

  • The Sun "brightens and dims slightly, taking 11 years to complete one solar cycle".
  • Satellites show output has varied "by less than 0.1 percent" over more than 40 years.
  • Maunder Minimum (1645–1715): few sunspots, during the Little Ice Age (13th to mid-19th century).
  • But NASA notes that cooling "was more likely influenced by increased volcanic activity and ocean circulation shifts".
Quick check
?What does the Maunder Minimum show about solar causes of climate change?
Mission 2

Continental drift

  • Over millions of years, plates move land and open or close ocean gateways.
  • This changes where land lies near the poles (where ice can build) and how ocean currents carry heat.
  • Example: Antarctica glaciated rapidly at the Eocene/Oligocene boundary, "approximately 34 million years ago".
  • Long thought due to Southern Ocean gateways opening, which "enabled the formation of the Antarctic Circumpolar Current" and isolated the continent.
  • A climate model found the gateways played "a secondary role … relative to CO2 concentration": drift can set the stage, while other factors may trigger change.
Mission 2

Volcanic activity

  • Large explosive eruptions inject sulfur dioxide into the stratosphere, forming aerosols that "scatter and absorb incoming sunlight".
  • Mount Pinatubo (Philippines, 1991): NASA reports about 15 million tons of SO₂.
  • Global temperature fell by about 0.6 °C over the next 15 months, with measurable cooling "for a period of almost two years".
  • Effect: short-term cooling, unless many eruptions cluster together.
Quick check
?A climate record shows a global cooling of about half a degree that lasts two years, then disappears. Which natural cause fits best?
Mission 2

Comparing the drivers

  • Timescale: volcanic (years) < solar (decades to centuries) < orbital (10,000s of years) < drift (millions of years).
  • Size: orbital changes, amplified by feedbacks, drove glacials and interglacials; solar and volcanic effects are smaller and shorter.
  • Today: "Earth's current orbital positions within the Milankovitch cycles predict our planet should be cooling, not warming".
  • Since 1750, warming from greenhouse gases from fossil fuels is "over 270 times greater" than the extra warming from the Sun.
Mission check

?One long climate record shows three features: (1) a gradual shift over tens of millions of years as a continent drifts towards a pole; (2) warm–cold swings about every 41,000 years; (3) a global dip of about half a degree that lasts two years. Which matching of causes is correct?

Each natural cause works on its own timescale. Orbital cycles, amplified by CO₂ and ice, paced the ice ages; solar and volcanic changes cause smaller, shorter shifts; drifting continents set the long-term stage.

Mission 3: Between 1941 and 2004, Alaska's Muir Glacier retreated 12 km. All that time, its ice kept flowing towards the sea. How can both be true?

Make a guess: Between 1941 and 2004, Alaska's Muir Glacier retreated 12 km. All that time, its ice kept flowing towards the sea. How can both be true?

Specification: what this mission covers

CCEA A-level Geography

Mission 3

A glacier is a system

  • Inputs: snow, mainly in the accumulation zone, high on the glacier.
  • Store: ice, which flows downhill under its own weight.
  • Outputs: meltwater, vapour and icebergs, mainly from the ablation zone lower down.
  • Equilibrium line: the boundary between the two zones, where a year's gain equals a year's loss.
Mission 3

From snow to ice sheet

  • Buried snow compacts and recrystallises. After about a year it becomes firn, about two-thirds as dense as water; over time, firn becomes glacier ice.
  • An ice sheet is glacial ice covering more than 50,000 km². Only two exist today: Greenland and Antarctica.
  • In the last ice age, ice sheets also covered much of North America and Scandinavia.
  • In Ireland, Scottish and local ice merged to form an Irish ice sheet early in the last glacial cycle.
Quick check
?Which condition must hold, year after year, for an ice sheet to build up over a lowland?
Mission 3

Mass balance

  • Mass balance = accumulation − ablation, over a year.
  • Positive: the glacier thickens and its snout may advance. Negative: it thins and its snout may retreat. Glaciers grow "when winter snowfall exceeds summer melting" (USGS).
  • Measured in water equivalent (w.e.): the depth of water if the ice gained or lost were spread evenly over the glacier.
  • Ice still flows downhill whatever the balance.
Mission 3

Glacial erosion

  • Plucking: meltwater freezes onto jointed rock, and moving ice tears blocks away. "As the ice moves, it plucks rock from the sides and bottom of the valleys" (NPS).
  • Abrasion: debris frozen into the base scrapes the bed like sandpaper, smoothing it and leaving scratches (striations).
  • Warm-based ice erodes most, because it slides.
  • Meltwater erodes too: it cuts channels under, beside and in front of the ice.
Mission 3

Transport and deposition

  • Debris travels on the ice (supraglacial), within it (englacial) and beneath it (subglacial).
  • Ice carries debris "like a conveyor belt" and drops it where the ice melts (NPS).
  • Till is dropped directly by ice: unsorted and angular.
Mission 3

Meltwater: fluvioglacial processes

  • Meltwater carries debris away from the ice, as rivers do.
  • As water slows, it drops its load by size: gravel first, then sand, then silt. So fluvioglacial deposits are sorted and layered, with rounded stones.
  • Landforms built this way (eskers, outwash plains) come in a later mission.
Quick check
?A cutting shows a deposit of angular stones of all sizes, mixed with clay and with no layers. What laid it down?
Mission check

?Stakes show a glacier's ice moving 80 m down-valley a year near its snout. Yet the snout ends 20 m further up-valley than a year ago. What explains this?

Retreat describes only where the snout is. NSIDC: a retreating glacier "simply melts faster than it flows". That is how Muir Glacier retreated 12 km while its ice kept moving towards the sea: its mass balance was negative.

Mission 4

Till: what ice leaves behind

  • Ice carries debris "like a conveyor belt" and drops it where the ice melts (NPS).
  • Material dropped directly by ice is till (boulder clay).
  • Till is unsorted (clay to boulders, mixed) and angular, because ice does not sort or round what it carries.
Mission 4

Moraines along the glacier

  • Lateral moraine: a ridge of debris along the valley side, much of it from frost-shattered walls above.
  • Medial moraine: where two glaciers join, their lateral moraines merge into a stripe down the middle, as on Alaska's Gilkey Glacier.
  • All moraines are till, so they are unsorted and angular.
Mission 4

Moraines across the valley

  • Terminal moraine: a ridge at the ice's furthest point. "The longer a glacier stays in one spot... the larger this moraine will be" (NPS).
  • Recessional moraines: smaller ridges behind it, where retreat paused. A glacier may stay "in a steady state for decades, or even longer" (NPS).
  • Moraines mark old ice limits: Glacier National Park's Little Ice Age maximum was reconstructed from them (USGS).
Quick check
?Three ridges of unsorted debris cross a valley floor, one behind another, below a glacier. The ridge furthest down-valley is the largest. What are they?
Mission 4

Drumlins

  • Drumlins: smooth, oval hills of till, shaped beneath moving ice.
  • The steeper, blunt end faces the way the ice came from; the long, tapering end points down-ice (RGS).
  • They often occur in swarms, a "basket of eggs". In Strangford Lough, County Down, the sea has flooded a drumlin swarm, leaving "drowned drumlins" as islands.
  • How they form is still debated. RGS: the most widely accepted idea is that ice overloaded with till moulded it into streamlined hills.
Mission 4

Ribbed moraine: north-central Ireland

  • Ribbed (Rogen) moraine: ridges of till formed beneath ice, lying across the direction of flow (drumlins lie along it).
  • Most of the landforms in the "Drumlin Belt" of north-central Ireland are ribbed moraine, some up to 16 km long.
  • They formed late in the last glaciation, in "one of the world's largest and most contiguous ribbed moraine fields" (Clark and Meehan).
  • Drumlins and crag and tails are mixed in, recording ice flow that changed direction over time.
Quick check
?A field of drumlins has steep, blunt ends facing north-west and long, tapering tails pointing south-east. Which way did the ice flow?
Mission 4

Erratics: Big Rock, Alberta

  • An erratic is a rock carried by ice and dropped far from its source, often on different bedrock.
  • Okotoks "Big Rock": a 16,500-tonne quartzite boulder, about 41 m long, 18 m wide and 9 m tall.
  • It was part of a mountain formation in what is now Jasper National Park. A rockslide dropped debris onto a glacier, which carried it out of the mountains; it arrived between 10,000 and 30,000 years ago.
Mission 4

Meltwater landforms: eskers and outwash plains

  • Meltwater sorts and layers what it carries; ice does not.
  • Esker: a long, winding ridge of sorted sand and gravel, laid down by meltwater flowing in a tunnel under the ice.
  • Outwash plain (sandur): a flat spread of sorted, layered sand and gravel beyond the snout. Coarse gravel is dropped near the ice; fine sand further away.
Quick check
?A long, winding ridge, a few kilometres long, is made of rounded sand and gravel in layers. What is it most likely to be?
Mission check

?On limestone moorland lies a large granite boulder. The nearest granite outcrop is 30 km to the north-west, and drumlins nearby point north-west to south-east. What best explains the boulder?

Big Rock is an erratic. A rockslide in what is now Jasper National Park dropped it onto a glacier, which carried it out onto the plains and left it as the ice melted, 10,000–30,000 years ago.

Mission 5

Where meltwater flows

  • Meltwater flows supraglacially (on top of the ice), englacially (within the ice) and subglacially (below the ice).
  • Surface water reaches the bed "through the bases of crevasses and moulins".
  • Discharge peaks in summer and in the afternoon of each melt day, and is lowest in winter, so deposits record pulses of high and low flow.
Mission 5

Meltwater erodes and carries

  • Meltwater "is usually very rich in sediment, which increases its erosive power": it erodes by abrasion with its load, as rivers do.
  • Under the ice, water is under pressure. Its path is "controlled mostly by the slope of the ice surface, rather than the slope of the bed".
  • So subglacial water can flow uphill over obstacles for short distances, cutting channels a normal river could not.
  • It carries huge loads of sand and gravel when discharge is high, then drops them when it slows.
Mission 5

Fluvioglacial deposits are different

  • Till (ice-laid): unsorted, unlayered, angular to sub-angular clasts in a fine-grained matrix.
  • Fluvioglacial (water-laid): sorted by size, stratified (in layers), with rounded clasts.
  • Why: water drops the largest particles first as it slows, and each flood pulse lays a new bed.
Quick check
?A pit face shows rounded pebbles in distinct layers, coarse gravel beds alternating with sand beds. Which agent laid it down, and why?
Mission 5

Eskers

  • An esker is a long ridge of sand and gravel, "deposited by glacial meltwater flowing through tunnels within and underneath glaciers".
  • The tunnel fills with sediment; "As the ice retreats, the sediments are left behind as a ridge".
  • Size: "usually metres to tens of metres high"; length "from hundreds of metres to hundreds of kilometres".
  • Eskers "tend to be oriented parallel to ice flow", and can cross hills because the water was under pressure.
  • UK: near Carlisle, "Several parallel eskers were created by meltwaters that flowed subglacially towards an ice margin" (BGS). Ireland: an esker exposed at Kinnitty, Co. Offaly.
Mission 5

Outwash plains (sandur)

  • Outwash plains, also called "'Sandur' (after the Icelandic term for sand)", are built by "braided, sediment-rich streams" flowing away from the ice.
  • Beyond the ice, meltwater velocity falls: "The largest sediment (usually gravel) is deposited first", with finer sand "carried further downstream".
  • So grain size fines with distance from the snout, and channels braid around gravel bars.
  • Iceland's Skeiðarársandur is a classic sandur; in 1996 an eruption beneath the ice "collected in a subglacial lake at a tremendous rate", then burst out as a jökulhlaup (glacial outburst flood) across it.
Quick check
?Samples are taken along an outwash plain, from the ice front outwards. Which pattern is most likely?
Mission check

?A dry channel is cut across a col between two valleys. Its floor rises, then falls, along its length, and no stream flows in it today. How did it most likely form?

Beneath ice, meltwater flows under pressure, guided by the ice surface, not the land. That is why subglacial channels and eskers can run uphill.

Mission 6

The region: a drumlin lowland

  • County Down, around Strangford Lough: lowland shaped by ice sheets in the last glacial period.
  • In the last glacial period, "glaciers and ice sheets enveloped the land".
  • The AONB calls it "one of the world's finest examples of drumlin hills and island landscapes".
  • Strangford Lough: about 30 km long and up to 8 km wide.
Mission 6

Till: what the ice left

  • Till: unsorted clay, sand and boulders dropped by ice.
  • Moving ice smears till into hills, or leaves it as a sheet (a till plain).
  • The RGS: the most accepted idea is that drumlins formed "when the ice became overloaded with sediment, known as 'till'". There is "still some debate".
Mission 6

Drumlins

  • Drumlins: oval hills, long axis parallel to ice flow, typically 250–1,000 m long and 120–300 m wide.
  • The up-ice (stoss) face is usually steeper than the down-ice (lee) face.
  • They occur in swarms: a "basket of eggs".
Mission 6

Drowned drumlins and erratics

  • As the ice melted, "sea levels rose, inundating huge areas": the hollows between drumlins flooded.
  • Strangford Lough: about 70 islands, many of them drowned drumlins.
  • Pladdies: rocky islands of "massive boulders (glacial erratics or the cores of eroded drumlins)".
Mission 6

Ribbed moraine

  • Ribbed (Rogen) moraine: fields of many till ridges, formed beneath the ice, lying across the ice flow (drumlins lie along it).
  • North-central Ireland holds "one of the world's largest and most contiguous ribbed moraine fields"; ridges reach 16 km long.
  • Ribbed moraine, drumlins and crag-and-tails overlap: "a palimpsest record of changing flow geometries".
Mission 6

Meltwater landforms

  • Eskers: winding ridges of sand and gravel, laid by meltwater in tunnels under the ice.
  • Outwash plains: flat spreads of sorted sand and gravel, laid by meltwater in front of the ice.
  • Moraines: ridges of till at the ice margin (terminal, recessional).
  • Exam move: unlike till, meltwater deposits are sorted and layered.
Quick check
?A field of many closely spaced, parallel till ridges lies at right angles to the ice flow shown by nearby drumlins. What is it most likely to be?
Mission 6

Benefits of development

  • Farming: grazing by sheep and cattle; "the crisscross pattern of hedges and field boundaries overlaid on the drumlins".
  • Farmland "follows the contours of the land".
  • Settlement: Newtownards lies at the head of the lough, with Belfast visible to the west.
  • Tourism: "an abundance of major, high quality visitor attractions".
  • Wildlife: the sheltered drowned-drumlin lough holds horse mussel reefs.
Mission 6

Problems of development

  • Farming pressure: "There are pressures for more intensive food production"; the AONB aims at "maintaining high water quality in streams".
  • Recreation: "Access must be managed in a way to mitigate against wildlife disturbance and user conflict".
  • Protection limits change: the lough is a Special Area of Conservation of 15,392 ha and was Northern Ireland's first marine nature reserve.
  • Landscape value: the AONB values the field-and-hedge pattern over the drumlins; large new buildings could break it (inference).
Quick check
?Which statement is a problem of socio-economic development in this environment, rather than a benefit?
Mission 6

Exam move: weighing benefits and problems

  • Same landforms, both effects: drumlins give farmland and scenery; drowned drumlins give a sheltered lough for wildlife and recreation.
  • Conflicts: intensive farming v water quality; recreation v wildlife; new building v landscape protection.
  • Management: AONB plan, SAC and marine nature reserve status set limits.
  • Judgement: the landscape is an asset when use fits its limits; it becomes a problem when pressures exceed them.
Mission check

?A developer proposes a large intensive pig unit on a drumlin whose streams drain into a sea lough protected as a Special Area of Conservation. Which benefit-and-problem pair is best supported for this landscape?

The "basket of eggs" is both: a gift for farming, tourism and wildlife, and a source of conflict when development pushes too hard. In County Down, protection and management decide which way it goes.

Mission 7

The enhanced greenhouse effect

  • Fossil fuels, deforestation, farming and industry add greenhouse gases faster than natural processes remove them.
  • Higher concentrations absorb more outgoing longwave radiation: the enhanced greenhouse effect.
  • IPCC: in 2019, CO₂ was higher than at any time in at least 2 million years; methane and nitrous oxide higher than in at least 800,000 years.
Mission 7

The additional gases

  • 2024 global averages: CO₂ 423.9 ppm, methane 1,942 ppb, nitrous oxide 338.0 ppb.
  • That is 52%, 166% and 25% above pre-industrial (before 1750) levels.
  • CO₂ rose 3.5 ppm in 2024, the largest rise since measurements began in 1957.
  • Fluorinated gases (F-gases) showed the highest relative growth since 1990, from low levels.
Mission 7

Why emissions have risen since pre-industrial times

  • Cumulative net CO₂ 1850–2019: about 2,400 billion tonnes; 42% of it emitted in just 1990–2019.
  • 2019 total: 59 billion tonnes CO₂-eq, 54% higher than 1990.
  • In 2010–2019, cleaner energy and more efficient GDP cut emissions less than rising activity in industry, energy supply, transport, farming and buildings has added.
  • 2019 shares by sector: energy supply 34%, industry 24%, agriculture/forestry/land use 22%, transport 15%, buildings 6%.
Mission 7

Observed evidence of recent warming

  • 2025: global surface temperature 1.44 °C (± 0.13) above 1850–1900; 2015–2025 were the eleven warmest years on record.
  • Warming to 2010–2019 was 1.06 °C; the best estimate of the human-caused part is 1.07 °C.
  • Almost all the world's glaciers are retreating together: unprecedented in at least 2,000 years.
  • Sea level has risen faster since 1900 than in any century in at least 3,000 years.
  • Exam move ("Evaluate"): pair each line of evidence with how confident the IPCC is.

Mission 7

History: cumulative emissions

  • Shares of all fossil and industrial CO₂ emitted 1750–2024: USA 23.5%, EU27 16.3%, China 15.5%, Russia 6.6%, UK 4.3%, India 3.6%.
  • The least developed countries: less than 0.4% of fossil CO₂ in 1850–2019.
  • CO₂ stays in the air for centuries, so cumulative emissions drive today's warming.
Mission 7

Case study: the UK over time

  • Fossil CO₂: 663 Mt in 1970 (4.5% of the world) → 318 Mt in 2024 (0.8%).
  • All greenhouse gases 2025: 367 Mt CO₂-eq (provisional), 54% below 1990.
  • Real change at home: the last coal power station closed in September 2024.
  • Defra also points to "a shift from manufacturing towards a service-based economy, alongside an increased reliance on imported goods" (see "Whose emissions?" below).
  • Still hard: domestic transport is now the largest source, 31% of net emissions.
  • The UK is an MEDC (more economically developed country).
Mission 7

Case study: China over time

  • Fossil CO₂: 802 Mt in 1970 (5.5% of the world) → 12,401 Mt in 2024 (32.1%).
  • Per person: 0.97 t → 8.74 t.
  • Cumulative share 1750–2024: 15.5%, below the USA and EU27.
  • Industrialisation and coal-fired power drove the rise; much of the output is exported (see "Whose emissions?" below).
  • Note: these are fossil CO₂ (Global Carbon Project); "The global balance" screen uses all greenhouse gases (EDGAR), so the numbers differ.

Whose emissions? Territorial v consumption

  • UK carbon footprint 2023: about 699 Mt CO₂-eq; imports' share rose from 31% (1996) to 53% (2023); 25% of import emissions come from China.
  • Since 1996: UK territorial emissions −50%, footprint only −15%.

Mission check

A territorial fall can be real yet incomplete. For the UK, territorial emissions fell 50% since 1996 but the footprint only 15%, and its cumulative share is still 4.3%: measures of "part" depend on territory, consumption and history.

Mission 8

Water stored in ice

  • Ice sheets hold over 68% of Earth's fresh water.
  • Fully melted: Antarctica's ice sheet would raise sea level about 58 m, Greenland's about 7.4 m.
  • Both are losing ice. 2006–2015 averages: Greenland 278 billion tonnes a year, Antarctica 155.
Mission 8

Sea level is rising faster

  • IPCC: global mean sea level rose 0.20 m between 1901 and 2018.
  • The rate rose from 1.3 mm a year (1901–1971) to 1.9 mm (1971–2006) and 3.7 mm (2006–2018).
  • Satellites: about 10 cm since 1993; the yearly rate has more than doubled.
  • Faster since 1900 than in any century for at least 3,000 years.
Quick check
?Sea level rose 0.20 m between 1901 and 2018. What was the average rate in mm per year?
Mission 8

Where the water comes from

  • Thermal expansion: seawater expands as it warms.
  • Land ice: glaciers and ice sheets melt or flow into the sea.
  • IPCC, 1971–2018: thermal expansion 50%, glaciers 22%, ice sheets 20%, land-water storage 8%.
  • In 2024 the rise was 0.59 cm; unusually, two-thirds came from thermal expansion (usually about two-thirds comes from land water).
  • Floating sea ice adds almost nothing: it already displaces its own weight of water.
Quick check
?Which of these adds least to global sea-level rise?
Mission 8

Projections and uncertainty

  • IPCC projections for 2100 (vs 1995–2014): likely 0.28–0.55 m (very low emissions) to 0.63–1.01 m (very high).
  • Approaching 2 m by 2100 "cannot be ruled out" because of deep uncertainty in ice sheets.
  • Local sea levels that were once-a-century events are projected to occur at least yearly at most places by 2100.
  • These are scenarios, not forecasts: they depend on emissions.
Mission 8

Impacts in an MEDC: the Netherlands

  • 26% of the land is below sea level; with river flooding, 55% is at risk of flooding.
  • By 2050 everyone behind a primary defence should have a yearly flood-death risk of no more than 1 in 100,000.
  • Delta Programme budget about €29 billion (2015–2050) against costs of about €38 billion: a funding gap.
  • High exposure, but high capacity to adapt.
Mission 8

Impacts in an LEDC: Bangladesh

  • Coastal zone home to nearly 42 million people (2010), projected 61 million by 2050.
  • Embanked islands in the south-west have lost 1.0–1.5 m of elevation since the 1960s, as embankments blocked sediment.
  • After Cyclone Aila (2009) breached embankments, land stayed tidally flooded for up to two years.
  • IPCC: without more adaptation, erosion, land loss, flooding and salinisation risks rise this century under all scenarios.
Quick check
?Both the Netherlands and coastal Bangladesh face rising seas. Which statement is best supported?
Mission check

?A news report says: "Melting glaciers and ice sheets explain most of the sea-level rise since 1971." How accurate is it?

The extra water comes from melting land ice (glaciers 22%, ice sheets 20%) and the ocean taking up heat and expanding (50%), with land-water storage 8% (1971–2018). In recent years water added from land (such as melting ice sheets and glaciers) has usually made up about two-thirds of each year's rise; in 2024 expansion did.

Mission 9

Extremes are changing

  • IPCC: hot extremes, including heatwaves, have become more frequent and more intense across most land regions since the 1950s: "virtually certain".
  • Heavy rainfall has become more frequent and intense over most land areas since the 1950s.
  • The share of tropical cyclones that are major (Category 3–5) has likely increased over four decades.
Mission 9

Every increment of warming matters

  • A hot extreme that came once in 10 years without human influence:
    • now (about 1 °C warmer): 2.8 times a decade, 1.2 °C hotter
    • at 1.5 °C: 4.1 times, 1.9 °C hotter
    • at 2 °C: 5.6 times, 2.6 °C hotter
    • at 4 °C: 9.4 times, 5.1 °C hotter.
Quick check
?In a 2 °C warmer world, a once-a-decade heat extreme would happen 5.6 times per decade. About how many would you expect in 30 years?
Mission 9

Attribution: the 2021 heatwave

  • Attribution science compares how likely an event is now with a modelled world without human-caused warming.
  • In late June 2021, Lytton, British Columbia, set Canada's all-time record: 49.6 °C.
  • World Weather Attribution: the regional heatwave was "virtually impossible without human-caused climate change".
  • An event this rare would have been at least 150 times rarer without it, and was about 2 °C hotter than at the start of the industrial era.
Quick check
?What does "at least 150 times rarer without human-induced climate change" mean?
Mission 9

Impacts in a wealthy country: British Columbia, Canada

  • 619 deaths were identified as heat-related in British Columbia (25 June–1 July 2021).
  • 67% of those who died were 70 or older; 56% lived alone; 98% died indoors.
  • Response: the coroners' panel recommended a provincial heat alert and response system.
  • Even a wealthy country had people with high vulnerability: older, isolated, without cooling.
Mission 9

Drought: three types

  • Meteorological: much less rain than normal.
  • Agricultural and ecological: soils too dry for crops and plants; higher temperatures raise evaporative demand.
  • Hydrological: low rivers, lakes, reservoirs and groundwater.
  • IPCC: a 10-year agricultural drought in drying regions now happens 1.7 times a decade (range 0.7–4.1).
Mission 9

Impacts in lower-income countries: the Horn of Africa, 2020–2023

  • From October 2020, southern Ethiopia, southern Somalia and eastern Kenya had five failed rainy seasons in a row.
  • WWA: climate change made such agricultural droughts "about 100 times more likely", mainly through higher evaporative demand.
  • Somalia: a study commissioned by UNICEF and WHO estimated 43,000 excess deaths may have occurred in 2022; half may have been children under five.
  • Most vulnerable: households relying on rain-fed farming, agropastoralism and pastoralism.
Quick check
?How did climate change mainly worsen the Horn of Africa drought, according to the attribution study?
Mission 9

Health

  • Heat-related deaths averaged about 546,000 a year; the rate has risen 23% since the 1990s (Lancet Countdown 2025, reported by WHO).
  • WHO projects about 250,000 additional deaths a year in 2030–2050 from undernutrition, malaria, diarrhoea and heat stress alone.
  • Vector-borne diseases have increased as vectors expand their range.
  • Low-income countries and small island developing states "endure the harshest health impacts".
Mission 9

MEDC v LEDC: evaluating impacts

  • Canada (MEDC): a short, intense heatwave; deaths concentrated among older people living alone; a heat-alert system as response.
  • Ethiopia, Kenya, Somalia (LEDCs): a multi-year drought; livelihoods, food and child survival hit; at least 180,000 refugees moved into drought-hit areas of Kenya and Ethiopia.
  • IPCC: deaths from floods, droughts and storms were 15 times higher in highly vulnerable regions than in very-low-vulnerability regions (2010–2020).
  • Exam move ("Evaluate"): the same warming produces different impacts because vulnerability differs between and within countries.
Mission check

?A new study finds a record heatwave in another country was "made 10 times more likely by human-caused climate change". Which conclusion is justified?

For Lytton, World Weather Attribution went further: the regional heatwave was "virtually impossible" without human-caused warming, about 2 °C hotter and at least 150 times more likely. Attribution answers "could it have happened?" with probabilities, and impacts then depend on vulnerability.

Mission 10

Two ways to intervene in the carbon cycle

  • Reduce emissions: slow the transfer of carbon from fossil stores to the atmosphere.
  • Remove or capture carbon: move carbon from the air (or a chimney) back into rock, soil, plants or the ocean.
  • IPCC: carbon dioxide removal "to counterbalance hard-to-abate residual emissions is unavoidable if net zero CO2 or GHG emissions are to be achieved".
Mission 10

Reducing emissions: efficiency and fuel shifts

  • Energy efficiency and conservation: insulation, efficient motors and lighting, less energy wasted.
  • Fuel shifts and low-carbon energy: coal to gas, then to renewables and nuclear. 2010–2019 unit costs fell by 85% for solar, 55% for wind and 85% for lithium-ion batteries.
  • Example: the UK closed its last coal power station in September 2024; its emissions were 54% below 1990 in 2025 (provisional).
Mission 10

Carbon capture and storage (CCS)

  • CCS: capture CO₂ from power stations or factories, compress it, and inject it into deep rock (old oil and gas fields, saline aquifers).
  • CCUS facilities "currently capture more than 50 Mt CO2 annually".
  • IPCC: geological storage capacity is "on the order of 1000 GtCO2", but CCS deployment is "far below" modelled pathways for 1.5 °C or 2 °C.
  • CCS deployment "has trailed behind expectations in the past".
Quick check
?Fossil CO₂ emissions in 2025 were projected at 38.1 billion tonnes. CCUS captures about 50 million tonnes a year. What share of fossil emissions is that?
Mission 10

Putting a price on carbon

  • Carbon tax: a fixed price per tonne emitted.
  • Cap and trade (emissions trading): a government caps total emissions and issues allowances; firms that cut cheaply sell spare allowances; the cap falls over time.
  • By 2020 these covered over 20% of global emissions, "although coverage and prices have been insufficient to achieve deep reductions".
Mission 10

Direct air capture

  • Direct air capture (DAC) machines pull CO₂ from ordinary air.
  • Climeworks' Mammoth plant in Iceland is designed for up to 36,000 tonnes a year; the CO₂ is injected underground, where it reacts with basalt and turns to stone.
  • Reported capture: 675 tonnes in the first half of 2026, far below capacity.
Quick check
?How many Mammoth-sized plants, at full design capacity (36,000 tonnes a year), would capture one year's fossil CO₂ (38.1 billion tonnes)?
Mission check

?A country plans to reach net zero mainly by building CCS and buying forest offsets, while keeping its emissions roughly unchanged. Which evaluation is best?

Machines like Mammoth help only in part. At full design capacity, about 1.06 million would be needed to capture one year's fossil CO₂, and Mammoth has captured far less. Cutting emissions (efficiency, fuel shifts, carbon pricing) does most of the work; removal (forests, CCS, DAC) deals with what is left.

Mission 11

Mitigation and adaptation

  • Mitigation: reducing the causes: cutting emissions or removing greenhouse gases (renewables, carbon pricing, forests, carbon capture).
  • Adaptation: adjusting to actual or expected climate to moderate harm or use opportunities (flood defences, heat alerts, drought-tolerant crops).
  • Mitigation needs global action; adaptation is mostly local, but poorer places need help to pay for it.
Mission 11

The IPCC: role

  • Role: to assess "on a comprehensive, objective, open and transparent basis" the science of human-induced climate change, its impacts and options for adaptation and mitigation.
  • Reports "should be neutral with respect to policy": policy-relevant, not policy-prescriptive.
  • Works in support of the UN climate convention process; reports are reviewed by experts and governments, and approved by consensus where possible.
  • Its findings frame negotiations: e.g. policies in place at the end of 2020 implied about 3.2 °C by 2100.
Mission 11

Global agreements

  • UNFCCC (Convention): adopted 9 May 1992, in force 1994.
  • Kyoto Protocol: adopted 1997, in force 2005; binding targets for 37 industrialised countries and economies in transition only.
  • Limits: the USA signed but never ratified; Canada withdrew with effect from 15 December 2012.
  • Paris Agreement: adopted 12 December 2015, in force 4 November 2016; US withdrawal took effect 27 January 2026.
Mission 11

Kyoto: did it work?

  • Target: an average 5% cut below 1990 over 2008–2012, with emissions trading, the Clean Development Mechanism and joint implementation.
  • Of the 36 countries that fully participated (not the USA or Canada), only 9 emitted more than committed; after trading, all complied; together they beat their commitment by 2.4 Gt CO₂-eq a year.
  • But the authors put 2.2 Gt of that surplus down to "hot air": targets that needed little or no policy to meet.
  • IPCC: Kyoto cut emissions in some countries and built capacity for reporting and carbon markets. Yet global emissions in 2019 were 54% above 1990.
Quick check
?Which judgement on the Kyoto Protocol's success is best supported?
Mission 11

The Paris Agreement

  • Goal: "well below 2°C" and pursue efforts to limit warming to 1.5 °C.
  • Every country sets its own target, a nationally determined contribution (NDC), on a five-year cycle, with a global stocktake.
  • Principle: "common but differentiated responsibilities and respective capabilities"; developed countries "should take the lead".
  • Strength: near-universal. Weakness: targets are self-chosen.
Mission 11

The gap

  • 2024 was likely the first calendar year above 1.5 °C (1.55 ± 0.13 °C); 2025 was 1.44 °C. Paris is judged over decades, not single years.
  • UNEP (2025): full NDCs → 2.3–2.5 °C this century; current policies → up to 2.8 °C.
  • Carbon budget left for 1.5 °C: 170 billion tonnes CO₂. 2025 emissions: 38.1 Gt fossil + 4.1 Gt land-use change.
Quick check
?At 2025 levels of total CO₂ emissions, how many years would the remaining 1.5 °C budget last?
Mission 11

Who pays? Sources far from impacts

  • Least developed countries: under 0.4% of fossil CO₂ (1850–2019).
  • Deaths from floods, droughts and storms were 15 times higher in highly vulnerable regions (2010–2020).
  • Finance: the US$100 billion goal was first met in 2022 (US$115.9 billion); new goal US$300 billion a year by 2035.
  • A Loss and Damage Fund assists developing countries particularly vulnerable to climate change. These are goals and funds, not delivered outcomes.
Quick check
?What is the main argument for high-emitting countries funding loss and damage in poorer ones?
Mission 11

Beyond international directives: national and sub-national

  • National (UK): the Climate Change Act 2008 set a legal 80% cut by 2050, raised to 100% (net zero) in 2019; five-year carbon budgets; an independent Committee on Climate Change. 2025: 54% below 1990.
  • Sub-national (Scotland): law requires net-zero emissions by 2045, five years ahead of the UK.
  • IPCC: by 2020, 56 countries had direct climate laws covering 53% of emissions; Paris has spurred national and sub-national targets.
  • Exam move ("Evaluate"): compare a target with delivery, e.g. UK transport is now its largest source.
Mission 11

Adaptation strategies: costs and risks

  • Water management: on-farm water management, storage and irrigation reduce vulnerability, but irrigation can deplete groundwater and salinise soil.
  • Flood management: early warnings plus levees have cut deaths; land-use planning (no-build zones) and wetland restoration reduce risk further.
  • Resilient agriculture: cultivar improvements, agroforestry, diversification.
  • Costs: the Dutch Delta Programme has about €29 billion for 2015–2050 against costs of about €38 billion.
  • Risk: seawalls protect now but can lock in exposure: maladaptation.
Mission 11

Mitigation: the toolkit

  • Carbon tax / trading: covered over 20% of global emissions by 2020, but prices too low for deep cuts; the EU Emissions Trading System (EU ETS) cut emissions from covered sectors about 47% from 2005.
  • Renewables and efficiency: 2010–2019 unit costs fell 85% (solar) and 55% (wind).
  • CCS captures CO₂ at power stations or factories and stores it underground; direct air capture removes CO₂ already in the air.
  • Carbon capture: Iceland's Mammoth direct-air-capture plant is designed for up to 36,000 t CO₂ a year; it captured 675 t in the first half of 2026.
  • Afforestation and forest offsets: most of 26 projects studied did not significantly reduce deforestation.
  • Solar radiation management: does not reduce greenhouse gases; a sudden stop would cause rapid climate change.
Mission check

?Negotiators design a new global deal on shipping emissions. Which design best applies the lessons of Kyoto and Paris to "who cuts, who pays"?

The UN agreements' answer is common but differentiated responsibilities: every Party cuts through its NDC, developed countries lead, and finance (US$300 billion a year by 2035; the Loss and Damage Fund) flows to the most vulnerable. Pledges still point to 2.3–2.8 °C.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Proxies: ice cores (gases, isotopes, dust; 800,000 years), ocean-floor deposits (foraminifera δ¹⁸O; millions of years), pollen and lake sediments (thousands of years), tree rings (annual; centuries to millennia).

Mission 2

  • Astronomic (Milankovitch: ~100,000, ~41,000, ~23,000-year cycles), solar (11-year cycle, <0.1% change), continental drift (millions of years; Antarctica ~34 million years ago) and volcanic (Pinatubo: ~0.6 °C for about 15 months) causes act on very different timescales.
  • None of them explains today's warming: orbital positions point to cooling.

Mission 3

  • Mass balance = accumulation − ablation. A negative balance makes the snout retreat, even though the ice keeps flowing forward.
  • Ice sheets grow where snow survives each summer; meltwater sorts what ice leaves unsorted.

Mission 4

  • Meltwater sorts and layers: eskers and outwash plains.

Mission 5

  • Meltwater flows on, in and under ice; it erodes, carries and sorts sediment, so fluvioglacial deposits are sorted, stratified and rounded, unlike till.

Mission 6

  • County Down's lowland post-glacial landscape: till, drumlin swarms, drowned drumlins and erratic "pladdies" in Strangford Lough; ribbed moraine across north-central Ireland.
  • Benefits (farming, settlement, tourism, wildlife) and problems (intensive farming pressure, recreation disturbance, limits from protection) depend on how development is managed.

Mission 7

  • Extra CO₂, methane, N₂O and F-gases enhance the greenhouse effect; emissions rose because activity grew faster than efficiency improved.
  • Observed warming (1.44 °C in 2025), glacier retreat and sea-level rise are evidence; the human-caused estimate matches the observed warming.

Mission 8

  • Sea level rose 0.20 m in 1901–2018 and is accelerating; thermal expansion and land ice are the main sources, sea ice almost none.
  • Same hazard, different impact: the Netherlands (MEDC) can pay to adapt; coastal Bangladesh (LEDC) has sinking polders and less capacity.

Mission 9

  • Heat extremes are more frequent and intense with every increment of warming; attribution studies measure how much more likely an event became.
  • MEDC (Canada) v LEDC (Horn of Africa): different hazards, different vulnerabilities, different scale of impact.

Mission 10

  • Interventions either cut emissions (efficiency, fuel shifts, carbon pricing) or store carbon (forests, CCS, DAC). Capture is tiny today: about 50 Mt a year against 38.1 Gt emitted.

Mission 11

  • Mitigation cuts causes; adaptation reduces harm. Kyoto bound few countries; Paris includes almost everyone but relies on self-set NDCs.
  • The IPCC assesses evidence neutrally and frames negotiations; national (UK) and sub-national (Scotland) laws go beyond international directives.
