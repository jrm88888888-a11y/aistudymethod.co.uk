Mission 1

Mission 1: Who counts as unemployed, and who slips through the count?

Make a guess: Who counts as unemployed, and who slips through the count?

Mission 1

Three groups, not two

  • Start with the working-age population.
  • UK: everyone aged 16 and over.
  • Split it three ways:
    • Employed: did any paid work in the survey week (even 1 hour), or has a job but is temporarily away.
    • Unemployed: no job, available to start, and actively looking for work.
    • Economically inactive: everyone else.
  • ILO definition of unemployed: without a job, looked for work in the last 4 weeks, can start in the next 2 weeks (or has found a job and is waiting to start it).
  • Labour force = employed + unemployed.
  • The labour force is also called the economically active population.
Mission 1

Worked example: the unemployment rate

  • Illustrative data. Working-age population 50 m: employed 28.5 m, unemployed 1.5 m, inactive 20 m.
  • Labour force = 28.5 + 1.5 = 30 m.
  • Unemployment rate = unemployed ÷ labour force × 100.
  • = 1.5 ÷ 30 × 100 = 5%.
  • Not 1.5 ÷ 50 (= 3%): the inactive are not in the labour force.
  • Economic inactivity rate = inactive ÷ working-age population × 100 = 20 ÷ 50 × 100 = 40%.
  • UK practice: ONS quotes employment and inactivity rates for ages 16–64; the unemployment rate uses everyone 16 and over.
  • Say: "5% of the labour force" — not: "5% of the population".
Mission 1

Inactive is not unemployed

  • Economically inactive: not working and not both looking and available.
  • Who? Full-time students, retired people, people looking after family, the long-term sick or disabled.
  • And discouraged workers: they want a job but have stopped looking, because they think none is available.
  • Discouraged workers are not counted as unemployed. Joblessness is understated.
  • Inactivity matters too: inactive people produce no market output and many rely on benefits.
  • Say: "economically inactive" — not: "unemployed", for someone who is not looking for work.

Mission 1

Two ways to count

  • Labour force survey (ask people):
    • interviewers question a large sample of households about the week before
    • anyone who meets the ILO definition is counted as unemployed
    • UK: the Labour Force Survey (LFS), run by the ONS, gives the ILO unemployment figure
  • Claimant count (count benefit claims):
    • counts people claiming benefits because they are unemployed
    • comes straight from benefit records, so no survey is needed
    • UK: people claiming Jobseeker's Allowance, plus Universal Credit claimants who are required to look for work

Mission 1

The UK's two measures in practice

  • ONS publishes both every month.
  • Universal Credit (introduced from 2013) changed who must look for work to keep receiving benefit.
    • Some people in low-paid work must now look for more work and are included in the claimant count.
    • From spring 2020 until mid-2025 the claimant count was higher than LFS unemployment (ONS). Before 2020 it was lower.
  • The LFS has its own problem: fewer households answer.
    • Response fell sharply by 2023. ONS paused its usual LFS estimates in October 2023 and published adjusted figures instead.
    • Reweighted LFS estimates returned in February 2024, labelled "official statistics in development".
  • Lesson: every measure depends on its method. Check how the data were collected before comparing years.
Mission 1

What the rate misses

  • Hidden unemployment: discouraged workers count as inactive, not unemployed.
  • Underemployment:
    • part-time workers who want full-time work count as employed
    • people in jobs well below their skills count as employed too
  • Real case: in 2013 Spain's unemployment rate for under-25s was above 50% (Eurostat: 55.5%). Many under-25s are full-time students, so the youth labour force is small: this is a share of the young people in the labour force, not of all young people.
  • So the unemployment rate understates how many people want work, or more work.
Mission 1Evaluate

How well does the unemployment rate measure joblessness?

It depends on:

  • Hidden unemployment: in a long slump, more people give up looking. The rate can fall with no new jobs.
  • Underemployment: when firms cut hours instead of jobs, the rate rises less than lost work.
  • The method: survey response, benefit rules and the size of the informal economy all change what is counted.
  • So use it alongside other data: the employment rate, inactivity, hours worked and vacancies.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why are some people out of work even when the economy is booming?

Make a guess: Why are some people out of work even when the economy is booming?

Mission 2

Between jobs

  • Frictional unemployment: people between jobs, searching for a suitable one.
  • Examples: a school leaver's first search, someone who quit to find better work, a worker who moved town.
  • Cause: imperfect information. Matching workers to vacancies takes time.
  • Usually short-term. Some is unavoidable, even useful: a good match raises productivity.
  • Seasonal unemployment: jobs that disappear at certain times of year (farm harvests, beach resorts, ski resorts, winter construction).
  • Seasonal unemployment is predictable and recurs every year.
Mission 2

The wrong skills, the wrong place

  • Structural unemployment: a mismatch between the skills or locations of the jobless and the jobs on offer.
  • Cause: the pattern of demand and production changes: industries decline, new ones grow.
  • Occupational immobility: workers lack the skills for new jobs (a laid-off steelworker cannot become a nurse overnight).
  • Geographical immobility: jobs are elsewhere, but moving is costly (house prices, family ties).
  • Technological unemployment: machines or software replace a skill, a type of structural unemployment.
  • Often long-term, and concentrated in particular regions.
  • Migration and skills: migrants can fill skill shortages. UK evidence finds little overall effect of migration on UK-born workers' unemployment (Migration Advisory Committee, 2018); effects can differ for some groups and areas.
Mission 2

Not enough demand

  • Cyclical unemployment: caused by too little aggregate demand, in a downturn or recession.
  • Also called demand-deficient unemployment.
  • AD falls → firms sell less → they need fewer workers (labour is a derived demand).
  • Real national output falls below full-employment output: a negative output gap.
  • It affects many industries and regions at once.
  • Real case: UK unemployment rose from about 5% in early 2008 to 8.4% in late 2011 (ONS, Labour Force Survey).
Quick check
?A car factory installs robots to do its welding. The welders it lays off have no skills that other employers in the area want. Which type of unemployment is this?
Mission 2

Wages stuck above equilibrium

  • If wages are held above the market-clearing level, more people want to work than firms want to hire.
  • The excess supply of labour is unemployment.
  • This is unemployment caused by real-wage inflexibility.
  • Causes: a minimum wage set above equilibrium; trade union bargaining; wages that do not fall when demand for labour falls.
  • Debate: whether minimum wages cost jobs in practice depends on the level set and the market. Studies disagree; the evidence is in a later lesson.
  • Say: "an excess supply of labour at the minimum wage" — not: "a shortage of workers".

Mission 2

The natural rate

  • Even at full-employment output, some people are unemployed. Full employment does not mean zero unemployment.
  • Natural rate of unemployment: the unemployment rate when output is at its full-employment level.
  • It is the unemployment that remains when the labour market is in equilibrium: frictional, structural and seasonal.
  • So: cyclical unemployment = actual rate − natural rate.
  • The natural rate changes slowly: with skills, mobility, benefit rules, demographics and technology.
Mission 2

Worked example: splitting the rate

  • Illustrative data. Actual unemployment rate 7%. Frictional 1.5%, structural 3.5%.
  • Seasonal 0.5%.
  • Natural rate = 1.5 + 3.5 + 0.5 = 5.5%.
  • Cyclical = 7 − 5.5 = 1.5 percentage points.
  • Cyclical unemployment above zero means output is below full employment.
  • Say: "1.5 percentage points above the natural rate" — not: "1.5% above".
Quick check
?Illustrative data: a country's unemployment rate is 6.5%. Its natural rate is estimated at 4.5%. Which statement is correct?
Mission 2

The costs of unemployment

  • Personal: lost income; skills fade; stress and worse mental and physical health.
  • Social: links with crime and family strain; whole communities decline when a big employer closes; inequality rises.
  • Economic:
    • lost output that can never be recovered: the economy is inside its PPC
    • lower tax revenue and higher benefit spending, so a larger budget deficit
    • long spells reduce skills, so potential output may fall too
  • Say: "benefits are a transfer" — not: "benefits are lost output". The lost output is what the unemployed could have produced.
Quick check
?A deep recession keeps unemployment high for three years. Which effect could reduce the economy's potential output, not just its actual output?
Mission 2Evaluate

How serious is a rise in unemployment?

It depends on:

  • The type and length: short frictional spells can improve job matches; long spells cut skills and can raise the natural rate.
  • Who and where: losses concentrated in one region or group cause deeper social costs than the same rise spread thinly.
  • Support and policy response: benefits, retraining and quick action on demand reduce the lasting damage.
  • What else is happening: rising unemployment with falling inflation leaves room for demand-side policy; with high inflation, the choice is harder.
Mission check

?Four people are unemployed in the same country. Aggregate demand then rises, and output returns to its full-employment level. Whose unemployment is most likely to end because of the rise in demand?

Extra demand cures cyclical unemployment. Frictional and structural unemployment remain even at full employment: they are part of the natural rate.

Mission 3

Mission 3: When a country buys more from the world than it sells, how does it pay the difference?

Make a guess: When a country buys more from the world than it sells, how does it pay the difference?

Mission 3

The nation's accounts

  • The balance of payments records all transactions between a country's residents and the rest of the world over a period (a quarter or a year).
  • Credit (+): money flows in (a payment received from abroad).
  • Debit (−): money flows out (a payment made abroad).
  • Three accounts:
    • current account: trade and income flows
    • capital account: capital transfers (small)
    • financial account: buying and selling assets, lending and borrowing
  • Purpose: it shows how a country pays its way in the world, and whether it is borrowing from abroad or lending.
  • The current account balance, often as a % of GDP, is a key macroeconomic indicator.
Mission 3

  • Balance of trade = exports − imports of goods and services.
  • Primary income: wages, interest, profits and dividends earned abroad.
  • Secondary income: transfers with nothing in return.
  • Credits > debits on an account: a surplus. Debits > credits: a deficit.
  • Say: "a current account deficit" when debits exceed credits — not: "the country made a loss".

Mission 3

Capital and financial accounts

  • Capital account (small): capital transfers, e.g. debt forgiveness; buying or selling non-produced assets, e.g. land for an embassy or a brand name.
  • Financial account: cross-border buying and selling of assets.
    • Foreign direct investment (FDI): buying at least 10% of a firm, or building a factory abroad. A lasting interest and some control.
    • Portfolio investment: buying shares (under 10%) or bonds for a financial return. No control, and easy to sell quickly.
    • Other investment: bank loans and deposits.
    • Reserve assets: the central bank's foreign currency and gold.
  • Credit (+): foreign residents buy our assets or lend to us (money in).
  • Debit (−): our residents buy foreign assets; or the central bank adds to its reserves (money out).
  • Say: "FDI is a financial account flow" — not: "an export", even though money comes in.
Mission 3

Why it always sums to zero

  • Every transaction is recorded twice: once as a credit and once as a debit (double-entry).
  • Example: a firm imports machines worth 10 bn.
    • Current account: import of goods, −10.
    • The foreign seller is paid in our currency and holds it in our banks: a foreign-owned asset, so financial account +10.
  • So a current account deficit is matched by a net inflow on the financial account: borrowing from abroad, selling assets to foreigners, or running down reserves.
  • Current + capital + financial account + net errors and omissions = 0.
  • Errors and omissions: a balancing item for transactions that are missed or measured wrongly.
  • Say: "a current account deficit" — not: "a balance of payments deficit". The balance of payments as a whole always balances.

Mission 4: Is a big gap between what a country sells abroad and what it buys a problem, and can it be fixed?

Make a guess: Is a big gap between what a country sells abroad and what it buys a problem, and can it be fixed?

Mission 4

Why imbalances arise

  • Cyclical deficit: in a boom, incomes rise and spending on imports rises. Firms also sell more at home and less abroad. It shrinks in a downturn.
  • Structural deficit: it persists even at normal levels of output, because of weak competitiveness:
    • productivity growing more slowly than in other countries
    • inflation (costs and prices) higher than in trading partners
    • a strong, overvalued exchange rate
    • non-price weakness: quality, design, a small export sector
  • Saving and investment: a country that invests more than it saves must borrow the gap from abroad, so it runs a current account deficit. A country that saves more than it invests runs a surplus.
  • External shocks: a rise in oil prices widens deficits of oil importers and surpluses of oil exporters; a world recession cuts everyone's exports.
  • Say: "a current account deficit" — not: "a balance of payments deficit".
Quick check
?Illustrative data. Country Q's current account deficit widened from 1% to 4% of GDP during a two-year consumer boom. When growth slowed, it shrank back to 1%. Q's costs and prices rose no faster than its trading partners'. What best describes the extra deficit?
Mission 4

A persistent deficit: why it can matter

  • Exchange rate: pressure to depreciate; dearer imports add to inflation.
  • Interest rates: higher rates may be needed to attract the inflows that finance the deficit. This hurts investment.
  • Foreign ownership: assets are sold to foreigners, so future profits and dividends flow out (primary income debits).
  • Debt: borrowing builds up debt to the rest of the world, with interest to pay.
  • Credit rating: a large, persistent deficit can lower it, raising the cost of borrowing.
  • Demand management: the government may have to use contractionary fiscal or monetary policy to cut spending on imports, at a cost to output and jobs.
  • Economic growth: negative net exports are a drag on AD now, and jobs are lost in export and import-competing industries. But if the deficit pays for investment, it can raise growth later.
  • Links to other objectives: fast growth often worsens the current account as imports rise. Consumers gain cheap imports; firms competing with imports lose.
Mission 4

A persistent surplus: problems too

  • Domestic consumption and investment: lower than the economy's output. Living standards could be higher now.
  • Exchange rate: pressure to appreciate, which erodes export competitiveness over time.
  • Inflation: strong export demand adds to AD and can pull prices up; an appreciating currency makes imports cheaper, which pulls the other way.
  • Employment: good for jobs in export industries, while it lasts.
  • The surplus is lent or invested abroad: it may earn low returns or be lost.
  • One country's surplus is others' deficit: trading partners may complain or turn to protectionism.
Quick check

Mission 4

Two kinds of policy

  • Expenditure switching: changes relative prices, so spending switches from imports to home-produced goods.
    • a depreciation or devaluation of the currency
    • protectionism: tariffs, quotas, export subsidies
  • Expenditure reducing: cuts total spending, so imports fall too.
    • contractionary fiscal policy: higher taxes, lower government spending
    • contractionary monetary policy: higher interest rates
  • Supply-side policies: long-term competitiveness: training, infrastructure, research, flexible markets.
  • Say: "expenditure reducing cuts all spending, so imports fall with it" — not: "it cuts spending on imports only".
Mission 4

Worked example: the cost of expenditure reducing

  • Illustrative data. Current account deficit: 20 bn. GDP: 1,000 bn.
  • For every 1 bn fall in income, spending on imports falls by 0.25 bn.
  • This is the marginal propensity to import (MPM) = 0.25.
  • Fall in income needed = 20 ÷ 0.25 = 80 bn.
  • That is 80 ÷ 1,000 × 100 = 8% of GDP: a deep recession, with higher unemployment.
  • Not 20 bn: most of the lost spending falls on domestic goods, not imports.

Mission 4

Switching, supply side and surpluses

  • Depreciation: works only if demand for exports and imports is elastic enough (Marshall–Lerner), and only after a lag (J-curve); dearer imports raise inflation.
  • Protectionism: risks retaliation, can break trade rules (WTO), and raises prices for consumers and firms.
  • Supply-side policies: tackle the cause of a structural deficit, but are slow and uncertain.
  • To reduce a surplus:
    • expansionary fiscal or monetary policy: more spending at home, so more imports
    • allow the currency to appreciate
    • remove barriers to imports

Mission 4Evaluate

How much does a deficit matter, and which policy works?

It depends on:

  • The cause: a cyclical deficit corrects itself; a structural one needs supply-side policy.
  • Size, persistence and financing: a small deficit funded by long-term FDI is safer than a large one funded by short-term portfolio flows ("hot money"), which can leave suddenly.
  • What it pays for: investment can raise future output; consumption cannot repay the debt.
  • Conflicts with other objectives: expenditure reducing costs growth and jobs; depreciation adds to inflation.
  • Elasticities and time lags: switching works slowly, and only if demand responds enough.
  • External shocks (a world recession, an oil price jump) can swamp domestic policy: policy has limits.
Mission check

?Country W has a large, persistent current account surplus that its trading partners complain about. Which policy would reduce the surplus by expenditure switching?

Switching policies work through relative prices. To shrink a surplus, they must make imports relatively cheaper, not dearer.

  • Say: "low and stable inflation" — not: "falling prices". Deflation is not the goal.
Mission 5

More objectives

  • Balanced budget: government borrowing and debt at levels that can be sustained.
  • Environment / sustainability: growth that does not run down natural resources or harm future generations.
  • Greater equality: a fairer distribution of income and wealth (redistribution).
  • Objectives can support each other: growth creates jobs and raises tax revenue.
  • But they can also conflict: a trade-off means getting more of one costs some of another.

Mission 5

Conflict: low unemployment vs low inflation

  • (1) AD1 → AD2 in a slump: real output rises a lot, the price level a little. Unemployment falls.
  • (2) AD2 → AD3 near full capacity: real output rises a little, the price level a lot.
  • Near capacity, firms compete for scarce workers: wages and prices rise.
  • So pushing unemployment ever lower tends to raise inflation. Cooling inflation tends to raise unemployment.
  • This is the short-run trade-off shown by the Phillips curve.
Mission 5

Conflict: growth vs inflation and the current account

  • Demand-led growth faster than potential output opens a positive output gap: inflation rises.
  • Supply-led growth (LRAS shifts right) raises output without adding to inflation.
  • Faster growth raises incomes, and part of that income is spent on imports: the current account can worsen.
  • Higher inflation than in trading partners makes exports less competitive: another drag on the current account.
  • Raising interest rates to curb inflation may strengthen the currency, making exports dearer.
  • Budget vs growth: cutting a deficit by cutting spending or raising taxes reduces AD in the short run. Supporters say it lowers future borrowing costs and builds confidence; critics say it slows growth. How much depends on the size of the multiplier.

Mission 5

Conflict: growth vs the environment

  • More output usually means more energy and raw materials, more emissions and more waste.
  • These are negative externalities: the costs fall on third parties and future generations.
  • But the link can weaken: UK territorial greenhouse gas emissions fell by about 50% between 1990 and 2022, while real GDP grew.
  • Caution: part of that fall came from making less at home and importing more. Emissions measured by what the UK consumes fell by less.
  • Green technology, carbon taxes and regulation aim to decouple growth from emissions. How far that can go is debated.
Mission 5

Conflict: growth vs equity

  • Growth may reward some groups more: owners of capital, highly skilled workers, booming regions.
  • So inequality can widen even as average incomes rise.
  • Redistribution (progressive taxes, benefits) can narrow the gap.
  • One view: high taxes and generous benefits reduce incentives to work and invest, so equity costs some efficiency (Okun, 1975).
  • Another view: IMF research (Ostry, Berg and Tsangarides, 2014) found lower inequality was linked to faster and more durable growth, and redistribution was generally not harmful to growth except in extreme cases.
  • Which effect dominates depends on the policy design and the country.
Mission 5

Can conflicts be reconciled?

  • Supply-side policies (education, training, infrastructure, competition) shift LRAS right.
  • Then growth and falling unemployment come with less inflation.
  • But supply-side policies take years to work.
  • Policy mix: use different tools for different goals, e.g. interest rates for inflation, supply-side policies for growth.
  • Prioritise: tackle the most pressing problem first, accepting a cost elsewhere for a time.
Quick check
?A government wants faster growth without higher inflation. Which policy is most likely to achieve both over time?
Mission 5Evaluate

Which objective should come first?

It depends on:

  • Where the economy is: a deep slump makes jobs the priority; a positive output gap makes inflation the priority.
  • The cause: a demand shock and a supply shock call for different responses.
  • Time horizon: short-run trade-offs may ease in the long run if capacity grows.
  • Value judgements: how much inflation is worth a lower jobless rate, or how much growth is worth more emissions, is normative. Economists can show the trade-off; voters and governments choose.
Mission check

?An economy is already close to full capacity. The government cuts income tax to give growth a boost. Which outcome is most likely in the short run?

Pushing demand when capacity is nearly used up trades one objective (growth) against another (low inflation).

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Working-age population = employed + unemployed + inactive. Labour force = employed + unemployed.
  • Unemployment rate = unemployed ÷ labour force × 100.
  • Survey (ILO) and claimant count measure different things and often differ.
  • Discouraged and underemployed workers are not counted as unemployed, so the rate understates joblessness.

Mission 2

  • Frictional: between jobs. Structural: skills or location mismatch. Cyclical: too little aggregate demand.
  • Seasonal: jobs that vanish at certain times of year.
  • Real-wage: wages held above equilibrium.
  • Costs are personal, social and economic; lost output is never recovered.

Mission 3

  • Current account: goods, services, primary income, secondary income.
  • Capital and financial accounts record capital transfers and the buying and selling of assets (FDI, portfolio, other investment, reserves).
  • Current + capital + financial + errors and omissions = 0.

Mission 4

  • Imbalances can be cyclical or structural, and reflect saving and investment.
  • Persistent deficits and persistent surpluses both carry costs.
  • Expenditure switching changes relative prices; expenditure reducing cuts total spending (and output); supply-side policy works on competitiveness over time.

Mission 5

  • Main objectives: growth, low unemployment, low and stable inflation, a sustainable current account; also budget, environment and equality.
  • Near full capacity, pushing unemployment down tends to push inflation up.
  • Demand-led growth risks inflation; growth may harm the environment and widen inequality.
  • Supply-side policies can ease conflicts, but slowly. Choosing priorities involves value judgements.
