Sounds & exam technique

The final piece: how French sounds, and how to turn your grammar into marks. This lesson covers silent final consonants and verb endings, liaison, nasal vowels, the key graphemes (ou, u, oi, au/eau), accents, and exam skills — the dictée, reading aloud, gap-fill, translation both ways, and those tricky faux amis.

Phonics · silent letters

Silent final consonants

French usually does not pronounce a final consonant. The word looks longer than it sounds:

  • petit — the final t is silent
  • grand — the final d is silent
  • vous — the final s is silent

A memory aid: the consonants that are often sounded at the end are in the word CaReFuL — c, r, f, l — as in avec, bonjour, chef, parc. Most others go silent.

Phonics · verb endings

Silent -e / -es / -ent

The regular -er verb endings -e, -es and -ent are all silent — so several forms sound identical:

parle = parles = parlentall sound the same — only the spelling differs

This is why listening tasks give you context to work out the subject, and why writing tasks reward you for spelling the ending correctly even though it isn't heard.

Exam trap: in a dictée or gap-fill, you must still write the right ending. Ils parlent and il parle sound the same but only one fits the subject.

Phonics · liaison

Liaison: the linking sound

When a normally-silent final consonant meets a following vowel, it wakes up and links across — this is liaison:

  • les⁠_amis — the s of les is pronounced /z/ → "lay-zamis"
  • vous⁠_avez — the s links → "voo-zavez"

Why it matters: in reading aloud, liaison makes you sound natural and earns pronunciation marks. The linking consonant is only heard before a vowel.

Phonics · nasal vowels

The nasal vowels

French has vowels pronounced partly through the nose. The spelling usually ends in n or m:

spellingexample
an / engrand, en
onbon, bonbon
in / ain / einvin, pain, plein
unun, brun

Key point: the n is not a separate sound — the vowel itself is nasalised. on in bonbon is one nasal vowel, not "o" + "n".

Phonics · key graphemes

Letter combinations & their sounds

Learn these fixed sound-spellings — they unlock reading aloud and the dictée:

spellingsound (example)
ou/u/ — nous
u/y/ — tu
oi/wa/ — moi
au / eau/o/ — beau
ai / ei/ɛ/ — maison
gn/ɲ/ — montagne
ch · qu · -tion/ʃ/ chat · /k/ qui · /sjɔ̃/ nation

Careful with u vs ou: tu (/y/, rounded lips) is a very different sound from nous (/u/, "oo"). Mixing them changes the word.

Phonics · accents & h

Accents change the sound

Accents are not decoration — they change pronunciation and, in the dictée, they count:

  • é = /e/ (closed) — été
  • è / ê = /ɛ/ (open) — père, tête
  • ç = /s/ — ça, français

The two h's: h is always silent (muet), and usually allows elision (j'habite). But an aspirate h blocks liaison and elision: le héros (not l'héros).

Exam technique · speaking & listening

The dictée & reading aloud

Two tasks reward accurate sound-spelling knowledge:

  • Dictée: spell every word — and every accent — accurately. A missing é or ç loses marks.
  • Reading aloud: apply liaison, keep silent endings silent, and pronounce nasal vowels through the nose.

Quick wins: in the dictée, listen for a nasal vowel to decide between an / en / on; and for reading aloud, mark where liaison happens before you speak.

Exam technique · faux amis

Watch the false friends

These look like English words but mean something else — a classic translation trap:

Frenchreally means (not…)
librairiebookshop (library = bibliothèque)
actuellementcurrently (actually = en fait)
sensiblesensitive (sensible = raisonnable)

In translation: never assume a look-alike matches its English. Je reste à la maison = "I stay at home", not "I rest at home".

Exam technique · reading & translation

Gap-fill & translation both ways

Two tasks reward careful grammar, not guessing:

  • Gap-fill: use the surrounding words as clues — agreement and tense markers tell you the ending.
  • Translation: respect French word order — translate the meaning, never word-for-word.

Word-for-word fails: "I am 15" is j'ai 15 ans (I have 15 years), not je suis 15. Translate the idea, not the individual words.

Recap

Your sounds & technique toolkit

Silent letters: most final consonants are silent (petit, grand, vous); CaReFuL (c, r, f, l) are usually heard.

Silent endings: parle = parles = parlent — but you must still spell the right ending.

Liaison: a silent final consonant links before a vowel — les⁠_amis, vous⁠_avez.

Graphemes: ou=/u/, u=/y/, oi=/wa/, au/eau=/o/, gn=/ɲ/; é=/e/ vs è/ê=/ɛ/; silent h allows elision (j'habite).

Exam: dictée = accurate spelling & accents; reading aloud = liaison & silent endings; translate the idea, respect word order.

Faux amis: librairie=bookshop, actuellement=currently, sensible=sensitive, assister à=to attend, rester=to stay, journée=day.
