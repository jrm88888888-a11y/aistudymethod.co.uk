Mission 1

Mission 1: How does warm sodium hydroxide turn a bromoalkane into an alcohol?

Make a guess: How does warm sodium hydroxide turn a bromoalkane into an alcohol?

Mission 1

Halogenoalkanes

  • Halogen X is more electronegative than carbon: the C–X bond is polar, Cδ+–Xδ−.
  • The δ+ carbon can be attacked by electron-rich species.
  • Classify by the carbon bonded to X: bonded to 1, 2 or 3 other carbons.
  • Primary: CH3CH2CH2Br · secondary: CH3CHBrCH3 · tertiary: (CH3)3CBr.
Mission 1

Nucleophiles

  • A nucleophile is an electron-pair donor.
  • It donates both electrons of a new covalent bond, usually from a lone pair.
  • Examples: OH⁻, CN⁻, NH3, H2O. No charge needed: NH3 and H2O are neutral.
  • "Nucleus-loving": it is attracted to a δ+ atom.
Quick check
?Which set contains only species that can act as nucleophiles?
Mission 1

Heterolytic fission and curly arrows

  • Heterolytic fission: both bonding electrons go to one atom, so ions form.
  • The more electronegative atom takes the pair and becomes negative: C–Br → C⁺ + Br⁻.
  • A curly arrow (two barbs) shows an electron pair moving.
  • Forming a bond: arrow from a lone pair (or a bond) to the atom it bonds to.
  • Breaking a bond: arrow from the bond to the atom that takes the pair.
Mission 1

The mechanism, step by step

  • Bromoethane + warm aqueous NaOH (or KOH): CH3CH2Br + OH⁻ → CH3CH2OH + Br⁻
  • 1 Draw the dipole: Cδ+–Brδ−.
  • 2 Arrow from a lone pair on O of OH⁻ to the δ+ C: the new C–O bond.
  • 3 Arrow from the C–Br bond to Br: Br leaves as Br⁻, taking the pair.
  • Products: ethanol and bromide ion.
  • Name: nucleophilic substitution. With OH⁻ it is also called hydrolysis.
Quick check
?Hydroxide ions react with 1-chloropropane: CH3CH2CH2Cl + OH⁻ → CH3CH2CH2OH + Cl⁻. Which describes the curly arrows correctly?
Mission 1

Which reacts fastest? Bond enthalpy

  • The C–X bond must break, so its strength controls the rate.
  • Bond enthalpies: C–F 467 · C–Cl 346 · C–Br 290 · C–I 228 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Rate: iodo > bromo > chloro (fluoro hardly reacts).
  • Polarity predicts the opposite order (C–F most polar), so bond enthalpy wins.
Mission 1

Comparing rates with silver nitrate

  • Add the halogenoalkane to aqueous silver nitrate in ethanol (ethanol dissolves both). Warm all tubes equally, e.g. 50 °C water bath.
  • Water is the nucleophile: R–X + H2O → R–OH + H⁺ + X⁻
  • X⁻ meets Ag⁺: Ag⁺(aq) + X⁻(aq) → AgX(s). Time how long until the precipitate appears.
  • Precipitates: AgI yellow (first) · AgBr cream · AgCl white (last).
  • Same halogen, different class: tertiary fastest, then secondary, then primary (Core practical 4).
Quick check
?1-chlorobutane, 1-bromobutane and 1-iodobutane are each added to aqueous silver nitrate in ethanol at 50 °C. Which tube shows a precipitate first, and what colour is it?
Mission 1

Cyanide: a longer chain

  • Reagent: KCN in ethanol (with a little water), heat under reflux.
  • CH3CH2Br + CN⁻ → CH3CH2CN + Br⁻. Product: propanenitrile.
  • Arrow from the lone pair on C of CN⁻ to the δ+ carbon; arrow from C–Br to Br.
  • A nitrile forms, and the carbon chain grows by one.
  • KCN is very toxic.
Mission 1

Ammonia: making amines

  • Reagent: excess ammonia in ethanol, heated in a sealed tube (under pressure).
  • Step 1: lone pair on N of NH3 → δ+ C; C–Br → Br. Forms CH3CH2NH3⁺ + Br⁻.
  • Step 2: a second NH3 removes H⁺ from N: lone pair on N → H; N–H bond → N.
  • Overall: CH3CH2Br + 2NH3 → CH3CH2NH2 + NH4Br. Product: ethylamine, a primary amine.
Quick check
?Bromoethane is heated with excess ammonia in ethanol: CH3CH2Br + 2NH3 → CH3CH2NH2 + NH4Br. What does the second NH3 molecule do?
Mission 1

Substitution or elimination?

  • OH⁻ can act as a nucleophile (attacks C) or as a base (removes H⁺).
  • Aqueous, warm → mainly substitution: an alcohol.
  • Ethanolic, hot → mainly elimination: an alkene.
  • 2-bromopropane + KOH gives both propan-2-ol and propene: the two reactions happen at the same time.
  • Elimination mechanism: Mission 4.
Mission check

?Iodoethane, CH3CH2I, is warmed with aqueous sodium hydroxide, and ethanol forms. Which statement about the electron pairs is correct?

OH⁻ donates a lone pair to the δ+ carbon; the C–X pair leaves with the halogen as X⁻. That is how the warm alkali turns a bromoalkane into an alcohol.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Hydrogen bromide can add to propene two ways round, so why does one product win almost every time?

Make a guess: Hydrogen bromide can add to propene two ways round, so why does one product win almost every time?

Mission 2

Two ways round

  • Propene, CH3CH=CH2, is unsymmetrical: its two C=C carbons carry different groups.
  • H–Br can add two ways:
  • H to C1, Br to C2 → CH3CHBrCH3, 2-bromopropane.
  • H to C2, Br to C1 → CH3CH2CH2Br, 1-bromopropane.
  • The H⁺ adds first. Where it goes decides where the C⁺ ends up, and so where Br goes.
Mission 2

Carbocations: primary, secondary, tertiary

  • Count the carbon (alkyl) groups bonded to the C⁺.
  • Primary: one, e.g. CH3CH2CH2⁺. Secondary: two, e.g. CH3CH⁺CH3. Tertiary: three, e.g. (CH3)3C⁺.
  • Stability: tertiary > secondary > primary.
  • Why: alkyl groups are electron-releasing. They push electron density towards C⁺.
  • More alkyl groups spread the positive charge more, so the ion is more stable.
Quick check
?Which carbocation is the most stable?
Mission 2

The more stable carbocation wins

  • Route 1: H⁺ to C1 (the CH2 end) → secondary CH3CH⁺CH3.
  • Route 2: H⁺ to C2 → primary CH3CH2CH2⁺.
  • The route via the more stable carbocation has the lower activation energy, so it is much faster.
  • So 2-bromopropane is the major product; 1-bromopropane is the minor product.
Mission 2

Drawing it

  • Draw the mechanism through the more stable carbocation for the major product.
  • Arrow 1: from the C=C bond to the Hδ+ of HBr. Arrow 2: from the H–Br bond to Br.
  • H bonds to the CH2 end; C⁺ is on the middle carbon (secondary).
  • Arrow 3: from a lone pair on Br⁻ to that C⁺ → 2-bromopropane.
  • The minor product forms by the same arrows, through the primary carbocation.
Quick check
?2-methylbut-1-ene, CH2=C(CH3)CH2CH3, reacts with hydrogen bromide. What is the major product, and why?
Mission 2

Same rule, other reagents

  • Water with an acid catalyst: propene → mainly propan-2-ol; minor propan-1-ol.
  • The electrophile (H⁺ or Hδ+) adds first. The nucleophile goes to the more substituted carbon.
  • Symmetrical alkenes (e.g. but-2-ene) give one product: no major or minor.
Quick check
?2-methylpropene, (CH3)2C=CH2, reacts with water in the presence of an acid catalyst. What is the major organic product?
Mission check

?1-methylcyclohexene is a six-membered ring with one C=C. One C=C carbon carries a CH3 group and no H; the other carries one H. It reacts with hydrogen bromide. What is the major product, and why?

The two directions give different carbocations. The route via the more stable one is faster, so its product is the major one. For propene + HBr, that is 2-bromopropane.

Mission 3

Mission 3: When a plastic softens in heat, what is breaking?

Make a guess: When a plastic softens in heat, what is breaking?

Mission 3

Polymers

  • A polymer is a very long molecule made of many small monomers joined by covalent bonds.
  • Neighbouring chains are held together only by intermolecular forces.
  • Heat a plastic such as poly(ethene): the chains gain energy, overcome the forces between them and slide past each other. It softens.
  • The covalent bonds along each chain stay intact.
Mission 3

Addition polymerisation

  • One bond of each monomer's C=C breaks. The carbons join neighbouring monomers by single bonds.
  • Nothing else is made: the polymer contains every atom of the monomers.
  • Ethene → poly(ethene): [–CH2–CH2–]n.
  • Propene → poly(propene): [–CH2–CH(CH3)–]n. The CH3 is a side group.
  • The repeating unit has the two former C=C carbons in the chain, with extension bonds at each end.
  • Substituted alkenes too: chloroethene, CH2=CHCl → poly(chloroethene), PVC.
Quick check
?Propene, CH2=CHCH3, forms the addition polymer poly(propene), written as [repeating unit]n. Which option gives the repeating unit and describes it correctly?
Mission 3

Back to the monomer

  • In a section of chain, find the repeating unit: two main-chain carbons for an addition polymer.
  • Keep each side group on the carbon it is attached to.
  • Remove the extension bonds and put a C=C between the two carbons.
  • –CH2–CHCl–CH2–CHCl– → repeating unit –CH2–CHCl– → monomer CH2=CHCl.
  • Check: the monomer has the same atoms as the repeating unit.
Quick check
?A section of an addition polymer is –CH2–C(CH3)2–CH2–C(CH3)2–CH2–C(CH3)2–. Which is the monomer?
Mission 3

Why polyalkenes last

  • A polyalkene is saturated: its chains have only strong C–C and C–H bonds.
  • These bonds are non-polar, so nucleophiles and electrophiles are not attracted.
  • So polyalkenes are unreactive: ideal for containers and pipes.
  • They are also non-biodegradable: microorganisms cannot break them down, so they persist for many years.
Mission 3

Landfill or incineration?

  • Landfill: cheap and simple, but polyalkenes persist, use land, and the material is lost.
  • Incineration (combustion): releases energy to generate heat or electricity, and cuts waste volume.
  • But it releases CO2, and can form toxic gases.
  • Burning PVC releases HCl, a toxic, acidic gas.
  • Remove it by passing the waste gases through a base (e.g. calcium oxide or carbonate): a scrubber.
Mission 3

Recycling

  • First, sort waste polymers into specific types.
  • Mechanical recycling: wash, shred, melt and remould into new products.
  • Saves crude oil and much of the energy used to make new polymer.
  • But: sorting and cleaning cost money; quality falls each time the polymer is reprocessed.
  • Feedstock recycling: heat or crack the polymer into small molecules, used to make new plastics and other chemicals.
Mission 3

Polymers that break down

  • Biodegradable polymers are broken down by microorganisms, e.g. poly(lactic acid), made from plant starch.
  • Often they need the right conditions, such as an industrial composter.
  • Benefits: less persistent plastic waste and, for plant-based polymers, less use of finite crude oil.
Mission 3

Making plastics more sustainable

  • Life cycle: count energy and resources at every stage: raw materials, manufacture, transport, use, disposal.
  • Chemists cut these: lighter products, lower-energy processes, designs that recycle easily, renewable feedstocks.
Quick check
?A council burns mixed plastic waste, including PVC, to generate electricity. Which extra step deals with the main problem caused by burning the PVC?
Mission check

?Poly(propene) can be heated until soft, moulded into a new shape, and cooled. The same plastic can be remoulded many times, and its chemical properties do not change. Which statement explains this?

When a plastic softens, nothing covalent breaks. The forces between the chains are overcome, and the chains slide past each other.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Why does hydroxide turn a bromoalkane into an alcohol in water, but into a gas in ethanol?

Make a guess: Why does hydroxide turn a bromoalkane into an alcohol in water, but into a gas in ethanol?

Mission 4

One ion, two jobs

  • OH⁻ has lone pairs on its O. It can donate one to two different places.
  • To a δ+ carbon: OH⁻ acts as a nucleophile → substitution (see Mission 1).
  • To an H on the carbon next to the C–Br carbon: OH⁻ acts as a base (it accepts H⁺) → elimination.
  • Same reagent, same compound. Conditions decide which job wins.
Quick check
?2-bromopropane is heated under reflux with potassium hydroxide dissolved in ethanol, and propene forms. What is the role of the hydroxide ion?
Mission 4

Elimination

  • Elimination: a small molecule is removed from two neighbouring carbons, leaving a C=C.
  • Here HBr is removed: H from one carbon, Br from the next.
  • CH3CHBrCH3 + KOH → CH2=CHCH3 + KBr + H2O
  • 2-bromopropane → propene, a gas.
  • Edexcel reagent: ethanolic potassium hydroxide; the hydroxide ion acts as a base.
Mission 4

Conditions decide

  • Both reactions happen at the same time (concurrently). Conditions change the proportions.
  • Aqueous KOH, warm → mainly substitution → alcohol.
  • Ethanolic KOH, hot (reflux) → mainly elimination → alkene.
  • Elimination is also favoured by tertiary halogenoalkanes; substitution by primary.
  • So 2-bromopropane with KOH gives both propan-2-ol and propene.
Quick check
?A chemist wants as much propene, and as little propan-2-ol, as possible from 2-bromopropane. Which conditions should be used?
Mission 4

Symmetrical or not?

  • H can be removed from any carbon next to the C–X carbon.
  • Symmetrical: 2-bromopropane. Both neighbours are CH3, so one alkene: propene.
  • Unsymmetrical: 2-bromobutane, CH3CHBrCH2CH3.
  • H from C1 → but-1-ene. H from C3 → but-2-ene, as E and Z isomers.
  • So unsymmetrical halogenoalkanes give a mixture of alkenes.
Quick check
?2-bromo-2-methylbutane, (CH3)2CBrCH2CH3, is heated with ethanolic potassium hydroxide. How many different alkenes can form?
Mission check

?Chlorocyclohexane is warmed with aqueous sodium hydroxide in one tube, and heated under reflux with sodium hydroxide in ethanol in another. The main product in the second tube is cyclohexene. Which statement explains the second result?

Same reagent, two jobs. In water, warm, OH⁻ mostly attacks carbon (alcohol). In ethanol, hot, it mostly removes H⁺ (alkene, often a gas).

Mission 5

Mission 5: How does a breath tester detect alcohol?

Make a guess: How does a breath tester detect alcohol?

Mission 5

Primary, secondary, tertiary alcohols

  • Find the carbon bonded to the –OH. Count the carbons attached to it.
  • Primary: one (methanol: none). Ethanol, CH3CH2OH.
  • Secondary: two. Propan-2-ol, CH3CH(OH)CH3.
  • Tertiary: three. 2-methylpropan-2-ol, (CH3)3COH.
  • Primary and secondary alcohols have an H on the C–OH carbon; tertiary alcohols have none.
Quick check
?Which of these is a secondary alcohol?
Mission 5

The oxidising agent

  • Acidified potassium dichromate(VI): K2Cr2O7 in dilute H2SO4.
  • Cr2O7²⁻ is orange. As it oxidises the alcohol it is reduced to Cr³⁺, which is green.
  • In equations, write the oxidising agent as [O].
  • The alcohol loses two H atoms: one from the O–H, one from the C–OH carbon. C=O forms.
  • No H on the C–OH carbon (tertiary) → no oxidation.
Mission 5

What oxidation gives

  • Primary → aldehyde: CH3CH2OH + [O] → CH3CHO + H2O (ethanal).
  • The aldehyde → carboxylic acid: CH3CHO + [O] → CH3COOH (ethanoic acid).
  • Overall: CH3CH2OH + 2[O] → CH3COOH + H2O.
  • Secondary → ketone: CH3CH(OH)CH3 + [O] → CH3COCH3 + H2O (propanone). It stops there.
  • Tertiary: not oxidised. The dichromate stays orange.
Mission 5

Distil or reflux?

  • Boiling points: ethanal 20 °C, ethanol 78 °C, ethanoic acid 118 °C. The aldehyde has no O–H, so no hydrogen bonds between its molecules.
  • Aldehyde: alcohol in excess; warm, and distil the aldehyde off as it forms, so it is not oxidised further. Cool the receiver in ice.
  • Acid: excess oxidising agent; heat under reflux. The condenser is vertical and open at the top, so the aldehyde returns and is oxidised. Then distil off the acid.
  • Ketone: heat under reflux, then distil. It cannot be oxidised further.
  • Use anti-bumping granules; heat with a water bath or electric heater, not a flame.
  • Core practical 5 does both with ethanol.
Quick check
?Propan-1-ol is heated with an acidified oxidising agent in apparatus set up for distillation, and the distillate is collected. What is the main organic product collected, and why?
Quick check
?Pentan-3-ol is heated under reflux with excess acidified potassium dichromate(VI). Which equation is correct?
Mission 5

The dichromate test

  • Warm a few drops with acidified potassium dichromate(VI) in a water bath.
  • Orange → green: a primary or secondary alcohol (or an aldehyde).
  • Stays orange: a tertiary alcohol (or a ketone).
  • To tell an aldehyde from a ketone, use Tollens' reagent or Fehling's solution.
  • Benedict's or Fehling's solution with an aldehyde: blue → red precipitate.
  • Early roadside breath tubes used this test: orange crystals turned green with ethanol on the breath.
Quick check
?Three colourless liquids, X, Y and Z, are butan-1-ol, butan-2-ol and 2-methylpropan-2-ol, in some order. Each is warmed with acidified potassium dichromate(VI). X and Y turn the mixture green; Z leaves it orange. Which conclusion is correct?
Mission 5

Background: adding hydrogen

  • Reduction is the reverse: hydrogen is added. [H] stands for the reducing agent.
  • Aldehyde + 2[H] → primary alcohol: CH3CHO + 2[H] → CH3CH2OH.
  • Ketone + 2[H] → secondary alcohol: CH3COCH3 + 2[H] → CH3CH(OH)CH3.
  • The reducing agent LiAlH4 does this. It also reduces acids: CH3COOH + 4[H] → CH3CH2OH + H2O.
  • Hydrogenation: H2 adds across C=C over a nickel catalyst. A C≡C triple bond takes two H2.
Mission check

?A few drops of cyclohexanol, a six-carbon ring with an –OH on one ring carbon, are warmed with acidified potassium dichromate(VI). What do you see, and why?

A breath tester works the same way. Ethanol is oxidised. Old tubes showed it as dichromate turning green; most modern devices oxidise ethanol in a fuel cell and measure the current.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Nucleophiles donate an electron pair to the δ+ carbon; the halogen leaves as X⁻ (heterolytic fission).
  • Curly arrows: from lone pair to C; from C–X bond to X.
  • Rate: C–I fastest, C–Cl slowest: bond enthalpy, not polarity.
  • CN⁻ gives nitriles (chain + 1 C); excess NH3 gives primary amines.

Mission 2

  • Unsymmetrical alkene + HX: two carbocations possible. Stability: tertiary > secondary > primary.
  • The more stable carbocation forms faster, giving the major product; the other route gives the minor product.
  • Alkyl groups release electron density towards C⁺, spreading its charge.

Mission 3

  • Addition polymer: C=C opens; repeating unit = two main-chain carbons with their side groups; monomer has the same atoms.
  • Softening overcomes intermolecular forces between chains; covalent bonds stay intact.
  • Polyalkenes are unreactive and persist: landfill, incineration (remove HCl), recycling, or degradable alternatives.

Mission 4

  • OH⁻ is a nucleophile (substitution → alcohol) or a base (elimination → alkene).
  • Ethanolic KOH, hot → elimination of HX; unsymmetrical halogenoalkanes give a mixture of alkenes.

Mission 5

  • Primary → aldehyde (distil) → carboxylic acid (reflux); secondary → ketone; tertiary: no oxidation.
  • Acidified dichromate(VI) turns orange → green with primary and secondary alcohols (and aldehydes).
