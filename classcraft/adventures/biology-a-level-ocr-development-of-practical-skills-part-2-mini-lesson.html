Mission 1

Mission 1: Many foods hide sugar, starch, protein or fat. How can a few drops of reagent tell you which, and how much?

Make a guess: Many foods hide sugar, starch, protein or fat. How can a few drops of reagent tell you which, and how much?

Mission 1

Starch: the iodine test

  • Add a few drops of iodine in potassium iodide solution.
  • Starch present: orange-brown → blue-black.
  • Works on solids or solutions.
  • Iodine slots inside the coiled amylose chain: that is what gives the colour.
Mission 1

Reducing sugars: Benedict's test

  • Reducing sugars: all monosaccharides, and the disaccharides maltose and lactose.
  • (1) Add an equal volume of Benedict's reagent (blue: Cu²⁺ ions).
  • (2) Heat in a boiling water bath for about 5 minutes.
  • (3) Reducing sugar reduces Cu²⁺ to Cu⁺: a brick-red precipitate of copper(I) oxide forms.
  • The more reducing sugar, the further along: blue → green → yellow → orange → brick red.
  • So the colour is semi-quantitative. For a number, use a colorimeter.
Mission 1

Non-reducing sugars

  • Sucrose is a non-reducing sugar: Benedict's stays blue.
  • (1) Take a fresh sample. Boil with dilute hydrochloric acid: this hydrolyses the glycosidic bond.
  • (2) Neutralise with sodium hydrogencarbonate (Benedict's works only in alkaline conditions). Check with pH paper.
  • (3) Benedict's test again. Brick red now: a non-reducing sugar was present.
  • This only works if the first Benedict's test was negative, or gave less precipitate.
Quick check
?A solution gives a negative result with iodine. With Benedict's reagent, heated, it stays blue. A fresh sample is boiled with dilute hydrochloric acid, neutralised, and tested with Benedict's again: a brick-red precipitate forms. What does the solution contain?
Mission 1

Proteins and lipids

  • Biuret test: add biuret reagent (sodium hydroxide, then dilute copper(II) sulphate).
  • Protein present: blue → lilac (purple). The reagent detects peptide bonds.
  • Free amino acids give no lilac: they have no peptide bonds.
  • Emulsion test: shake the sample with ethanol; any lipid dissolves.
  • Pour the ethanol into water. Lipid present: a milky-white emulsion.
  • The lipid comes out of solution as tiny droplets that scatter light.
Mission 1

Glucose-specific test strips

  • Benedict's detects any reducing sugar. A glucose test strip detects glucose only.
  • The pad holds the enzyme glucose oxidase, plus peroxidase and a dye.
  • Glucose oxidase acts only on glucose: hydrogen peroxide forms, and the dye changes colour.
  • Compare with the colour chart on the pot: an estimate of glucose concentration.
  • Used to check urine for glucose.
  • OCR calls these "reagent test strips for reducing sugars".
Quick check
?In the emulsion test, a sample is shaken with ethanol and the ethanol is then poured into water. A milky-white emulsion forms. Why?
Mission 1

From colour to number: colorimetry

  • A colorimeter shines light of one colour through a sample and measures absorbance.
  • After Benedict's: more glucose → more Cu²⁺ used up → less blue left in solution.
  • Remove the precipitate (filter or centrifuge). Use a red filter: the blue solution absorbs red light.
  • Zero the colorimeter with distilled water first.
  • Make standards of known concentration, treat them identically, and plot a calibration curve.
  • PAG5: colorimetry.

Mission 1

Biosensors (outline)

  • A biosensor: a biological molecule (often an enzyme) fixed to a transducer.
  • Glucose meter: glucose oxidase on an electrode.
  • Only glucose binds the enzyme's active site, so the sensor is specific.
  • The reaction produces a tiny electric current. More glucose, bigger current.
  • The meter converts the current into a reading of glucose concentration.
  • Fast, needs only a drop of blood.
Mission check

?A sample of milk is tested. Heated with Benedict's reagent: an orange-red precipitate. With biuret reagent: lilac. With iodine in potassium iodide: stays orange-brown. Which conclusion is supported?

Each reagent answers one question. Choose the tests, read every result, and claim no more than they show.

Mission 2: One spot of a mixture goes in; a ladder of spots comes out. How do you work out what each spot is?

Make a guess: One spot of a mixture goes in; a ladder of spots comes out. How do you work out what each spot is?

Mission 2

How chromatography separates

  • (1) Stationary phase: the paper, or a thin layer of silica gel on a plate (TLC). It stays put.
  • (2) Mobile phase: the solvent. It creeps up the stationary phase by capillary action, carrying the mixture.
  • (3) Each molecule spends time dissolved in the solvent and time attached to the stationary phase.
  • More soluble in the solvent, less attracted to the stationary phase: it travels further.
  • So molecules in a mixture separate into spots.
  • TLC runs faster and gives sharper spots than paper.
  • Say: "distance depends on solubility and attraction" — not: "on how much of it there is".
Mission 2

Running a chromatogram

  • Draw an origin line in pencil (ink would itself separate), about 1.5 cm from the bottom.
  • Put a small, concentrated spot of the sample on the origin. Dry it; add more; dry again.
  • Stand the paper in solvent with the origin above the solvent level, so the sample does not wash off into it.
  • Cover the container, so the air inside stays saturated with solvent vapour.
  • Take it out before the solvent reaches the top. Mark the solvent front in pencil at once.
  • Coloured spots show themselves. Colourless ones need a locating agent.
Mission 2

Worked example: Rf values

  • Rf = distance moved by the solute ÷ distance moved by the solvent front.
  • Measure both from the origin; measure to the centre of the spot.
  • Illustrative data: origin 20 mm from the paper's bottom edge; spot centre 56 mm; solvent front 80 mm.
  • Solute: 56 − 20 = 36 mm. Solvent: 80 − 20 = 60 mm.
  • Rf = 36 ÷ 60 = 0.60. Always between 0 and 1; no units.
  • Same solute, solvent and stationary phase: same Rf. Compare with reference values, or run standards beside the sample.
Quick check
?On a paper chromatogram of leaf pigments, the origin line was 20 mm from the bottom edge of the paper, the centre of the carotene spot was 65 mm from the bottom edge and the solvent front was 70 mm from the bottom edge. What is the Rf value of carotene?
Mission 2

Amino acids and other colourless molecules

  • Amino acids are colourless. After the run, dry the chromatogram and spray with ninhydrin; warm it.
  • Amino acids show up as purple spots. (Wear gloves: ninhydrin also stains skin.)
  • To identify a protein's amino acids, hydrolyse the protein first.
  • Run standards (known amino acids) beside the unknown. Matching Rf: same amino acid.
  • PAG6 (chromatography): e.g. identify the amino acids in a protein, or separate carbohydrates or vitamins.

?Sugars in a fruit juice are separated by thin-layer chromatography, with glucose, fructose and sucrose standards run on the same plate. Mission 3

Mission 3: How does water reach the top of the tallest tree with no pump and no energy spent by the tree?

Make a guess: How does water reach the top of the tallest tree with no pump and no energy spent by the tree?

Mission 3

Transpiration: the price of carbon dioxide

  • Stomata open to let carbon dioxide in for photosynthesis.
  • Water evaporates from the walls of mesophyll cells into the air spaces.
  • Water vapour diffuses out through the open stomata: transpiration.
  • Transpiration is an unavoidable consequence of gas exchange.
  • Say: "water vapour diffuses out while stomata are open" — not: "the plant opens stomata to lose water".
Quick check
?Why is transpiration described as a consequence of gas exchange rather than as a process the leaf carries out for its own sake?
Mission 3

Xylem vessels: built for flow

  • Xylem carries water and mineral ions from roots to leaves.
  • Dead cells, no contents: nothing obstructs the flow.
  • End walls lost: cells join into one continuous tube.
  • Lignified walls: rigid, so the vessel does not collapse under tension; also waterproof, and support the plant.
  • Pits: gaps in the lignin. Water moves sideways between vessels and out to leaf cells.
  • Say: "lignin stops the vessel collapsing under tension" — not: "lignin is only for support".
Quick check
?Why are the walls of xylem vessels thickened with lignin?
Mission 3

Where the xylem is

  • Stem: vascular bundles in a ring near the outside. Pith in the centre.
  • In each bundle: xylem inside, phloem outside, cambium between.
  • Cortex between the ring and the epidermis.
  • Root: xylem in the centre, often a star or X; phloem between its arms.
  • Leaf: in the midrib and veins; xylem towards the upper surface, phloem below.
Quick check
?A student is drawing a plan diagram from a micrograph of a transverse section of a dicotyledonous stem. Which description matches what should be drawn?
Mission 3

Cohesion–tension: pulled from the top

  • (1) Water evaporates from leaf cell walls. Water is drawn out of the xylem, through the cell walls, to replace it.
  • (2) This puts the xylem water under tension.
  • (3) Cohesion: hydrogen bonds hold water molecules together, so the whole column is pulled up unbroken.
  • Adhesion: water is attracted to the hydrophilic vessel walls, which helps it creep up narrow spaces.
  • The flow from roots to leaves is the transpiration stream.
  • Passive: no ATP. The energy comes from the Sun evaporating water.
  • Evidence: tree trunks shrink slightly in diameter at midday; cut xylem draws air in, not water out.
  • Say: "water is pulled up under tension" — not: "the roots pump water up".
Quick check
?What generates the force that draws water up the xylem from the roots to the leaves?
Mission check

?Florists cut the stems of fresh flowers under water. A stem cut in air and then put in a vase often wilts, even though its cut end is in water. Which explanation is correct?

The tree and the vase work the same way: evaporation pulls, cohesion passes the pull down, and the column must stay unbroken.

Mission 4

Four outside factors

  • Transpiration rate depends on the water-vapour gradient from air spaces to air, and on how open the stomata are.
Factor risesRateWhy
Light intensity↑stomata open wider
Temperature↑faster evaporation and diffusion; warm air holds more vapour, so the gradient is steeper
Humidity↓less steep gradient
Air movement↑humid air next to the leaf is blown away; gradient steeper
  • Say: "humid air reduces the water-vapour gradient" — not: "humid air makes the leaf wetter, so it loses more".

Mission 4Practical

The potometer

  • PAG5: estimating transpiration rates using a potometer.
  • Cut the shoot under water, at a slant. Fit it to the potometer under water: no air gaps.
  • Seal joints with petroleum jelly. Dry the leaves. Let the shoot settle for 5 minutes.
  • Let in one air bubble. Time how far it moves along the scale. Reset it from the reservoir. Repeat; take a mean.
  • Change one factor (e.g. fan, lamp distance). Keep the others, and the same shoot, constant.
  • Say: "a potometer measures water uptake" — not: "water loss". A little water is used in photosynthesis or kept in cells.
Mission 4

Worked example: rate from the bubble

Illustrative data: capillary radius 0.50 mm; bubble moves 45 mm in 5.0 min.

  • Water taken up = volume of a cylinder = πr²l.
  • = π × (0.50 mm)² × 45 mm = 35 mm³.
  • Rate = 35 mm³ ÷ 5.0 min = 7.1 mm³ min⁻¹.
  • To compare shoots, divide by leaf area (trace leaves on squared paper).
Quick check
?In a potometer, the capillary tube has a radius of 0.40 mm. The air bubble moves 30 mm in 10 minutes. What is the rate of water uptake?
Mission 4Practical

Looking inside a stem

  • PAG2: dissect a stem, or a celery leaf stalk, stood in dye. Cut it transversely: dyed spots show the xylem. Cut it longitudinally: dyed strands run the length of the stem.
  • PAG1: examine and draw stained sections to show where xylem and phloem lie.
  • Stain: toluidine blue turns lignified walls (xylem, sclerenchyma) blue-green and other walls purple. Phloroglucinol with acid turns lignin red.
  • Thin sections let light through: cut with a sharp blade, keep sections wet.
Mission 4

Drawing a plan diagram

  • A plan diagram shows tissue outlines only: no individual cells.
  • Sharp pencil, clear continuous lines, no shading.
  • Keep the proportions of the tissues true.
  • Label with ruled lines that do not cross.
Quick check
?A celery stalk stood for two hours in water containing a red dye. In a transverse section, red spots form a ring near the outside of the stalk. Under the microscope, each red spot is the inner part of a vascular bundle. Which tissue has carried the dye?
Mission check

?A grower's greenhouse plants wilt on hot afternoons. The grower wants to slow their transpiration. Which change would do this?

Warm, dry, moving air steepens the gradient; humid, still air flattens it. The plant does not decide: diffusion does.

Mission 5: Why does beetroot bleed its red pigment into hot water but not into cold?

Make a guess: Why does beetroot bleed its red pigment into hot water but not into cold?

Mission 5

Why the pigment stays in

  • Beetroot's red pigment (betalain) is stored in the cell vacuole.
  • To leave, it must cross two membranes: the tonoplast and the cell-surface membrane.
  • The cell wall is fully permeable: it does not hold the pigment in.
  • Betalain is large and polar. It cannot cross an intact phospholipid bilayer.
  • So pigment in the water = damaged membranes. More pigment = more permeable.
Mission 5

Temperature

  • Warmer: phospholipids gain kinetic energy and move more. The bilayer becomes more fluid; permeability rises slowly.
  • Above about 40 °C: membrane proteins denature. Their shape is lost, leaving gaps in the bilayer.
  • Permeability then rises steeply: pigment pours out.
  • Below 0 °C: ice crystals can pierce membranes; thawed tissue leaks.
  • Say: "membrane proteins denature" — not: "heat breaks the bonds in the fatty acid tails".
Mission 5

Solvents

  • Organic solvents such as ethanol (alcohol) dissolve in the bilayer and disrupt it: lipids dissolve in them.
  • They also denature membrane proteins.
  • Higher solvent concentration → more disruption → more pigment leaks.
Quick check
?Beetroot discs held at 70 °C release far more pigment than discs held at 30 °C. Which explanation is correct?
Mission 5Practical

Beetroot and membrane permeability

  • PAG5 (colorimeter): the effect of temperature on membrane permeability.
  • Cut cylinders with a cork borer; trim to equal length. Same surface area each.
  • Rinse in distilled water until it stays clear: removes pigment from cells cut open.
  • Put each in the same volume of water at one temperature (or ethanol concentration) for the same time.
  • Remove the beetroot, shake the tube, measure the liquid's absorbance in a colorimeter.
  • Control: same beetroot, size, volume and time. Only the independent variable changes.
Mission 5

Using a colorimeter

  • A colorimeter shines light of one colour through a sample in a cuvette.
  • Absorbance: how much light the sample absorbs. More pigment → higher absorbance.
  • Choose a green or blue-green filter: the red pigment absorbs these colours most.
  • Zero the colorimeter with a blank (distilled water) first.
  • Same filter and clean cuvette for every reading; light passes through the clear sides.
  • Some colorimeters give % transmission: more pigment → lower transmission.

Mission 6

Mission 6: How do you grow bacteria on purpose without growing anything else, or infecting yourself?

Make a guess: How do you grow bacteria on purpose without growing anything else, or infecting yourself?

Mission 6

What bacteria need

  • Nutrients: a carbon source (e.g. glucose) for respiration and for building molecules.
  • A nitrogen source (e.g. amino acids, nitrate) for proteins and nucleic acids; plus minerals.
  • Water: for reactions and transport into the cell.
  • A suitable temperature: growth depends on enzymes, each with an optimum.
  • A suitable pH: most bacteria grow best near neutral.
  • Oxygen: obligate aerobes need it; facultative anaerobes grow with or without it; obligate anaerobes are harmed by it.
Mission 6

Agar or broth

  • Nutrient broth: liquid medium. Growth shows as cloudiness (turbidity).
  • Nutrient agar: broth set with agar, a seaweed polysaccharide most bacteria cannot digest.
  • Agar melts near 85 °C and sets below about 40 °C: poured hot, solid at incubation temperature.
  • On agar, one cell (or a small clump) grows into a visible colony.
  • Spread culture evenly over the agar: colonies merge into a lawn.
  • Streak plate: streak in sections, flaming the loop between them. Cells thin out to single colonies: a pure culture.
Mission 6

Aseptic technique

  • Two aims: keep unwanted microbes out of the culture; keep the culture away from people.
  • Before: wash hands; disinfect the bench; sterilise media and glassware in an autoclave (121 °C, 15 min).
  • During: work beside a lit Bunsen burner: its updraught carries airborne microbes up and away.
  • Flame the inoculating loop until red hot; cool it before touching the culture.
  • Flame the neck of each bottle as you open and close it. Lift the dish lid only slightly, at an angle.
  • After: secure the lid with two short strips of tape, not all round: air can still enter, which discourages anaerobic bacteria (including many pathogens).
  • Label the base. Incubate upside down (condensation cannot drip onto the agar) at 25 °C. School labs avoid 30–42 °C: temperatures near body heat (37 °C) favour human pathogens.
  • Never open a plate after incubation. Autoclave cultures before disposal.
Mission 6

Serial dilution

  • Transfer 1.0 cm³ into 9.0 cm³ of sterile water; mix. Total 10 cm³: a 1 in 10 dilution (10⁻¹).
  • Repeat from the new tube: 10⁻², 10⁻³, 10⁻⁴ …
  • Dilution factor = total volume ÷ volume transferred = 10 ÷ 1 = 10.
  • After k tenfold steps, concentration = original × 10⁻ᵏ. Factors multiply; they never add.
  • A fresh sterile pipette for each transfer: no carry-over between tubes.
  • Uses here: a range of antimicrobial concentrations.
  • Uses later: diluting a culture until colonies can be counted (see Mission 7).
Quick check
?A student adds 1.0 cm³ of a bacterial culture to 9.0 cm³ of sterile water and mixes. She transfers 1.0 cm³ of this into a second 9.0 cm³ of sterile water, and repeats until she has made four dilutions. What fraction of the original concentration of bacteria is in the fourth dilution?
Mission 6Practical

Testing antimicrobials

  • PAG7 (microbiological techniques): e.g. the effect of antibiotics on bacterial growth.
  • Spread a known volume of broth culture over agar with a sterile spreader: a lawn.
  • Add sterile paper discs soaked in each antibiotic or plant extract, or fill wells cut with a sterile cork borer.
  • Extract in ethanol? Let the solvent evaporate first: ethanol itself kills bacteria.
  • Control: a disc or well with solvent only.
  • Incubate. Measure the clear zone (zone of inhibition) through the base: two diameters at right angles, take the mean.
Quick check
?A student soaks paper discs in garlic, ginger and mint extracts, all made in ethanol, and places them on a lawn of bacteria. Another disc is soaked in ethanol only. Which of these is a controlled variable in her investigation?

  • Subtract areas, never diameters. Halve before squaring.

Mission 7

Mission 7: Bacteria can go from a handful to billions overnight. How do you count them, and how do you graph that?

Make a guess: Bacteria can go from a handful to billions overnight. How do you count them, and how do you graph that?

Mission 7

Counting what you cannot see

  • Too many cells to count. So count a tiny, known sample, then scale up.
  • Viable count: counts living cells only. Each colony is taken to come from one living cell.
  • Turbidity: a colorimeter reads how cloudy a broth is. Quick, but dead cells cloud it too.
Mission 7

Viable count: dilute, plate, count

  • Serially dilute the culture (see Mission 6): 10⁻¹, 10⁻², … 10⁻⁶.
  • Spread plate: 0.1 cm³ spread over agar with a sterile spreader.
  • Incubate. Count only plates with 30–300 colonies: fewer gives large chance error; more and colonies merge.
  • Assume: each colony grew from one living cell.
  • Cells per cm³ = colonies × dilution factor ÷ volume plated (cm³).
Mission 7

Mission 7Practical

Factors affecting growth

  • PAG7: practical investigation into a factor affecting microbial growth, using broth.
  • Inoculate identical broths with the same volume of the same culture.
  • Change one factor: e.g. glucose concentration or pH. Keep incubation temperature within school limits.
  • Measure growth at intervals: turbidity (colorimeter absorbance) or viable counts by serial dilution.
  • Zero the colorimeter with uninoculated broth; it should stay clear (a sterility check).
  • Control: broth volume and composition, inoculum, temperature, aeration, time.
Mission 7

Growth on a log scale

  • Microbe numbers span many orders of magnitude: 10² to 10⁹ per cm³.
  • On an ordinary axis, the early points squash flat against zero.
  • On a log10 scale, each equal step is × 10. log10(10⁶) = 6.
  • Closed culture phases: lag, log (exponential), stationary, death (decline).
  • Exponential growth plots as a straight line on a log scale.
  • Say: "exponential: multiplied by the same factor each interval" — not: "the same number added each interval".
Mission 7

Worked example: doublings and logs

  • N = N0 × 2ⁿ. N0 = starting number; n = number of generations.
  • N0 = 2.0 × 10³ cells per cm³; one generation every 25 min; 5 h of log phase.
  • Step 1: n = 300 min ÷ 25 min = 12.
  • Step 2: 2¹² = 4096 (calculator: 2 xʸ 12).
  • Step 3: N = 2.0 × 10³ × 4096 = 8.2 × 10⁶ cells per cm³.
  • On a log10 axis: 3.30 → 6.91. Each generation adds log102 = 0.301; 12 × 0.301 = 3.61. ✓
Quick check

?A bacterial culture is in its log phase. Plotted on a log10 scale, its count rises in a straight line from 10³ to 10⁶ cells per cm³ over 3 hours. Approximately how long does one generation take?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Iodine: blue-black = starch. Benedict's, heated: brick-red precipitate = reducing sugar. Biuret: lilac = protein.
  • Non-reducing sugar: acid hydrolysis, neutralise, Benedict's again. Emulsion test: milky = lipid.
  • Colorimeter + calibration curve turns a colour into a concentration.

Mission 2

  • Molecules separate by how they share time between the solvent and the stationary phase.
  • Rf = distance moved by the solute ÷ distance moved by the solvent front, both from the origin; compare with standards or reference values for the same solvent.
  • Colourless amino acids are revealed with ninhydrin and identified against standards.

Mission 3

  • Xylem vessels are dead, hollow, lignified tubes with pits: a continuous path that does not collapse under tension.
  • Evaporation from leaves puts the xylem under tension; cohesion pulls the whole column up. No ATP is spent.

Mission 4

  • Light, temperature and air movement raise transpiration; humidity lowers it. All act through stomatal opening or the water-vapour gradient.
  • A potometer measures water uptake: volume = πr²l, then divide by time.
  • Lignin stains identify xylem (and sclerenchyma); plan diagrams show tissue outlines only.

Mission 5

  • Pigment leaks only when membranes are damaged: heat (more fluid phospholipids, then denatured proteins above about 40 °C) and organic solvents such as ethanol both raise permeability.
  • Measure leakage with a colorimeter (blank first, green filter); convert absorbance to concentration with a calibration curve from a serial dilution.

Mission 6

  • Bacteria need nutrients, water, a suitable temperature and pH; aseptic technique keeps contaminants out and the culture contained.
  • Serial dilution multiplies the dilution factor at each step: four tenfold steps give 10⁻⁴.
  • Antimicrobial tests compare clear zones against a solvent-only control; clear area = zone area − well (or disc) area.

Mission 7

  • Viable count = colonies × dilution factor ÷ volume plated, from a plate with 30–300 colonies.
  • On a log10 scale, exponential growth is a straight line; each generation adds 0.301 (N = N0 × 2ⁿ).
