Mission 1

Avatar: God comes down

  • Avatara (avatar) = "descent": the Supreme comes down into the world in a physical form.
  • Most avatars are of Vishnu, the preserver.
  • Why? Krishna explains: "When Righteousness / Declines, O Bharata! when Wickedness / Is strong, I rise, from age to age, and take / Visible shape" (Bhagavad Gita 4.7–8, Arnold).
  • Avatars come again and again, in every age, to protect the good and restore dharma.
  • An avatar is God's free choice; humans are reborn because of karma.
  • Many Hindus also believe God can be present in a consecrated temple image, the murti ("form"), where the deity is worshipped as an honoured guest.
Mission 1

The ten avatars of Vishnu

  • The best-known list (Dashavatara): Matsya (fish), Kurma (tortoise), Varaha (boar), Narasimha (man-lion), Vamana (dwarf), Parashurama, Rama, Krishna, the Buddha (in some lists Balarama), Kalki (still to come).
  • Each appears when dharma is under threat: e.g. Varaha lifts the earth from the ocean; Vamana's three steps win back the worlds.
  • Kalki is expected at the end of the present age, the Kali Yuga.
  • The two most loved: Rama and Krishna.
Mission 1

Rama: the model of dharma

  • Hero of the Ramayana (traditionally by the sage Valmiki).
  • Prince of Ayodhya. To keep his father's promise, he accepts 14 years' exile with his wife Sita and brother Lakshmana.
  • The demon king Ravana kidnaps Sita; with Hanuman and an army of monkeys, Rama rescues her.
  • His return to Ayodhya is remembered at Diwali in many regions.
  • Called maryada purushottama: the ideal man who keeps every duty: as son, husband, brother and king.
  • Role model today: keeping promises, duty before comfort, fair leadership.
  • Some Hindus question parts of the story, e.g. Sita's ordeal by fire and later banishment; many read these as tests of the hard choices dharma brings.
Mission 1

Krishna: God of love and teacher of the Gita

  • Born in Mathura; grows up among cowherds in Vrindavan.
  • Stories of the child Krishna stealing butter, and the young Krishna playing his flute for the gopis (cowherd girls), especially Radha: pictures of the soul's love for God (bhakti).
  • As an adult, Krishna is Arjuna's charioteer and teaches him the Bhagavad Gita before the great battle of the Mahabharata.
  • Gaudiya Vaishnavas (including ISKCON) go further: Krishna is not just an avatar but the Supreme Person himself, the source of all avatars (Bhagavata Purana 1.3.28, paraphrase).
  • Role model today: devotion, wisdom, doing one's duty without selfish desire.

Mission 1Diversity

Who counts as an avatar?

  • Vaishnavas: avatars are central; Rama and Krishna are fully God.
  • Shaivas: Shiva appears in many forms (e.g. Nataraja), but the avatar idea matters far less.
  • The Buddha: many Hindu lists make him Vishnu's ninth avatar. Buddhists do not accept this.
  • Jesus: some Hindus (e.g. many followers of Ramakrishna and Vivekananda) honour Jesus as an avatar. Most Christians reject this: they believe Jesus is God incarnate once, uniquely; avatars come many times.
  • Arya Samaj (founded by Dayananda Saraswati, 1875) rejects avatars and murti worship: God is formless.
  • So, how far are Rama and Krishna manifestations of the divine? Fully God (most Vaishnavas); great souls and heroes (some reformers); symbols of dharma (some modern readers).

Mission 2

Heard and remembered

  • Hindu scriptures fall into two groups.
  • Shruti ("what is heard"): revealed truth, heard by ancient sages (rishis) in deep meditation. Not composed by humans. The Vedas, ending in the Upanishads.
  • Smriti ("what is remembered"): passed on by human authors and memory. Epics (Ramayana, Mahabharata, which contains the Bhagavad Gita), Puranas, law books.
  • Shruti has the highest authority in principle; smriti explains and applies it.
  • In practice, smriti is what most Hindus hear, read and act out most.
Mission 2

The Vedas

  • Four Vedas: Rig (hymns), Sama (chants), Yajur (ritual formulas), Atharva (hymns and spells for daily life).
  • Each has layers: hymns (Samhitas), ritual guides (Brahmanas), forest texts (Aranyakas), and the Upanishads.
  • Seen as eternal and revealed; passed on orally for many centuries, with memory techniques that preserve every sound.
  • Used today in worship: e.g. the Gayatri Mantra (Rig Veda 3.62.10) recited daily; Vedic mantras at havan, weddings and funerals.
  • Few Hindus study the Vedas in full; their authority is honoured more than their text is read.
Mission 2Read the source

The Upanishads

  • The Upanishads are mystical and metaphorical: they ask what is ultimately real and what the self is.
  • Key teachings: Brahman (ultimate reality); the atman (eternal self); their relationship.

Katha Upanishad 1.2.18 (Swami Paramananda's translation, 1919):

> "This Self is never born, nor does It die. It did not spring from anything, nor did anything spring from It. This Ancient One is unborn, eternal, everlasting. It is not slain even though the body is slain."

Q1 What does this passage say about the self?

Show answer

It is eternal: unborn and undying.

Q2 Why might this comfort a Hindu at a funeral?

Show answer

The real self is not destroyed when the body dies.

Mission 2

Stories as models of dharma

  • Bhagavad Gita ("Song of the Lord"): in the Mahabharata, Krishna teaches the warrior Arjuna before battle. Do your dharma without selfish desire; three paths to mukti (liberation): action, knowledge, devotion.
  • Ramayana (traditionally by the sage Valmiki): Prince Rama's exile, the kidnap of Sita, her rescue, and Rama's just rule. Retold in Hindi by Tulsidas (Ramcharitmanas, 16th century).
  • Rama is the model of dharma as son, husband and king; Sita of loyalty and courage; Hanuman of devoted service.
  • Krishna models devotion and wisdom: the playful child, the divine friend, the teacher of the Gita.
  • These stories are acted out at festivals (Ramlila), sung, and used to teach children.

Mission 2

How texts are used, and how much they count

  • Worship: mantras from the Vedas; hymns and readings from the Gita and Ramayana.
  • Ethical decisions: "What would Rama do?"; the Gita on duty without selfishness.
  • Guidance and teaching: gurus explain texts; families tell the stories to children.
  • Different views of authority:
  • Orthodox schools: the Vedas are the final authority.
  • Arya Samaj (founded 1875 by Swami Dayananda Saraswati): the Vedas alone; later texts are not binding.
  • Vaishnavas (e.g. ISKCON): the Gita and Bhagavata Purana as the clearest word of God.
  • Many Hindus: a living guru's teaching, or personal experience, matters as much as any text.
Mission 2

Shankara: one reality

  • Shankara (Adi Shankaracharya), 8th century CE (traditionally 788–820).
  • Taught Advaita Vedanta (non-dualism): Brahman alone is real; the atman is not different from Brahman.
  • The world as we see it is maya: real for everyday life, not ultimately real.
  • Liberation through knowledge (jnana): realising "I am Brahman" ends ignorance.
  • Contribution: wrote commentaries on the Upanishads, Gita and Brahma Sutras; traditionally founded four mathas (monasteries), in north, south, east and west India, and organised orders of swamis (sannyasis) still active today.
Mission 2

Ramanuja: a personal God

  • Ramanuja, 11th–12th century CE (traditionally 1017–1137); South Indian, a Shri Vaishnava teacher.
  • Taught Vishishtadvaita (qualified non-dualism): Brahman is a personal God, Vishnu (Narayana).
  • Souls and the world are real: they are like God's body, distinct from God yet inseparable.
  • Liberation through devotion (bhakti) and surrender to God, by God's grace.
  • Contribution: gave a philosophical basis to the bhakti movement; organised temple worship; tradition says he shared a sacred mantra openly, beyond the high castes.

Mission 2

Brahmins, gurus, swamis

  • Brahmins (priestly class): traditionally learn, preserve and recite the Vedas; perform rituals as pujaris (temple priests) and family purohits.
  • They lead samskaras (life rites): at birth (name-giving), sacred thread, marriage, funeral.
  • Gurus: spiritual teachers who pass on knowledge and often a personal mantra at initiation; disciples follow their example.
  • Swamis: monks who have renounced family life (sannyasa); they teach, write and run charities (e.g. the Ramakrishna Mission, founded 1897 by Swami Vivekananda).
  • Different views: some Hindus value a priest's birth and training; reform groups stress learning and character over caste, and some train women as priests.
  • Leaders are role models when their actions match their teaching; scandals around some gurus make others cautious.
Mission 2

One important Hindu: Mohandas Gandhi

  • Mohandas K. Gandhi (1869–1948), called Mahatma ("great soul").
  • Teaching: satya (truth) and ahimsa (non-violence); satyagraha ("holding to truth"): resisting injustice without violence.
  • The Gita was his daily guide, read as a call to selfless action (karma yoga).
  • Contribution: led India's non-violent struggle for independence (1947); campaigned against untouchability; promoted village self-reliance.
  • Relevance today: inspired Martin Luther King Jr and others.
  • Critics: B. R. Ambedkar (1891–1956), a Dalit leader, argued that Gandhi did not go far enough against caste.
  • (Your class may study another figure instead, e.g. Swami Vivekananda.)
Mission 2Evaluate

"Living leaders matter more to Hindus today than scriptures."

  • For: few Hindus read the Vedas; a guru or priest explains what the texts mean. Leaders are visible role models.
  • For: many Hindus follow a living guru or sampradaya (teaching lineage) closely.
  • Against: leaders draw their authority from scripture: Shankara and Ramanuja both wrote commentaries on the same texts.
  • Against: shruti is eternal; leaders can fail, but the texts remain.
  • Against: the Gita and Ramayana are known in every Hindu home, with or without a leader.
  • Judge it by: Where do leaders get their authority? What do Hindus actually turn to in daily life? Does it differ between traditions?

Mission 3

What a festival is for

  • A festival (utsava) remembers a sacred story, honours a deity, and renews family and community.
  • Dates follow the lunar calendar, so they move in the Western calendar.
  • Diwali: the new moon of the month Kartika (October or November).
  • Holi: the full moon of the month Phalguna (February or March).
  • Both celebrate the victory of good over evil, and of light or life over darkness.
  • Stories and customs vary by region and tradition.
Mission 3

Diwali and the Ramayana

  • Marriage: Prince Rama wins Sita by stringing the great bow of Shiva at her father's court.
  • Banishment: Queen Kaikeyi claims two promises from King Dasharatha: her son rules, and Rama is exiled for 14 years. Sita and Rama's brother Lakshmana go with him.
  • Kidnapping: the demon king Ravana tricks Rama away with a golden deer and carries Sita off to Lanka.
  • Search: Hanuman and the monkey army find Sita; they build a bridge to Lanka.
  • Rescue: Rama defeats and kills Ravana; Sita is freed.
  • Return: Rama and Sita come home to Ayodhya. People light rows of lamps (diyas) to welcome them: the "row of lights" (Deepavali).
Mission 3

Lakshmi and other Diwali stories

  • Lakshmi, goddess of wealth and good fortune, is believed to visit homes that are clean and lit: Lakshmi puja on the main night.
  • In South India, Diwali (Deepavali) celebrates Krishna's defeat of the demon Narakasura.
  • In Bengal, the goddess Kali is worshipped on Diwali night.
  • For many Gujarati Hindus, the next day is New Year; a "mountain of food" (Annakut) is offered to Krishna.
  • Diwali often lasts five days, ending with Bhai Dooj, when sisters pray for their brothers.
  • Sikhs (Bandi Chhor Divas) and Jains also mark this time, for their own reasons.
Mission 3

Diwali customs

  • Lamps: diyas on doorsteps and windows; electric lights; fireworks.
  • Rangoli: colourful patterns of powder, rice or flowers at the entrance, to welcome guests and Lakshmi.
  • Cleaning the house; new clothes; a visit to the mandir.
  • Food and gifts: sweets (mithai) shared with family, friends and neighbours.
  • Business families bless new account books, starting the financial year with Lakshmi's blessing.
  • Meaning: light over darkness, knowledge over ignorance, good over evil.
Mission 3Read the source

From darkness to light

A prayer from the Brihadaranyaka Upanishad (1.3.28), chanted at Diwali and many other times:

  • asato mā sad gamaya — Lead me from the unreal to the real.
  • tamaso mā jyotir gamaya — Lead me from darkness to light.
  • mṛtyor mā amṛtaṁ gamaya — Lead me from death to immortality.

(Standard rendering.)

Q1 What three movements does the prayer ask for?

Show answer

Unreal → real; darkness → light; death → immortality.

Q2 How does the second line connect with Diwali?

Show answer

Mission 3

Holi: the stories

  • Prahlada and Holika: Prince Prahlada stays devoted to Vishnu, though his father, the demon king Hiranyakashipu, demands worship. Holika, the king's sister, cannot be burned; she sits in a fire holding Prahlada. She burns; he is unharmed. Vishnu, as Narasimha (man-lion), later destroys the king.
  • Krishna and Radha: young Krishna, dark-skinned, playfully colours the face of Radha; the cowherds of Braj join the game of colours.
  • Holi also welcomes spring and the new harvest.
Mission 3

Holi: the customs

  • Holika Dahan: a bonfire on the eve of Holi; people circle it and roast new grain.
  • Next day: throwing coloured powder (gulal) and water; songs and dancing.
  • Normal barriers relax: young and old, rich and poor, different castes play together.
  • A time to forgive, mend friendships and share sweets (e.g. gujiya).
  • In Braj (Mathura, Vrindavan, Barsana) Holi lasts days, with Krishna-themed celebrations.
  • Meaning: devotion protects (Prahlada); joyful love of God (Krishna and Radha); equality and new beginnings.
Mission 3Evaluate

"Diwali and Holi are more about culture than religion."

  • For: fireworks, sweets, gifts and public events are enjoyed by many, Hindu or not.
  • For: customs differ by region, suggesting culture shapes them.
  • Against: both centre on sacred stories (Rama, Lakshmi, Prahlada, Krishna) and on worship: Lakshmi puja, arti, Holika Dahan.
  • Against: their meanings (light over darkness, devotion protects, forgiveness) are religious teachings.
  • Another view: for many Hindus culture and religion cannot be separated; family life is a form of dharma.
  • Judge it by: What do Hindus themselves say the festival is for? Does the meaning survive without the worship?

Mission 4

Three festivals, one idea

  • Navaratri: "nine nights" in the month Ashvin (September or October). Worship of the goddess Durga.
  • Dussehra (Vijayadashami, "the victorious tenth"): the day after the nine nights.
  • Raksha Bandhan: the full moon of Shravana (July or August). Raksha = protection; bandhan = tie.
  • Shared idea: divine power protects dharma and defeats what harms it.
Mission 4

Shakti: the power of the goddess

  • Shakti = divine energy or power, worshipped as feminine: the Devi (Goddess).
  • Many Hindus see Shakti as the active power of God: Shiva and Shakti, like fire and its heat.
  • Shaktas worship the Devi as the supreme reality herself.
  • She has gentle forms (Parvati, Lakshmi, Saraswati) and fierce forms (Durga, Kali).
  • Navaratri honours Shakti in many forms; many temples and families chant the Devi Mahatmya ("Glory of the Goddess", part of the Markandeya Purana).
Mission 4

Durga and Mahishasura

  • The buffalo demon Mahishasura wins a boon: no god or man can kill him (many tellings: he thought no woman could threaten him). He drives the gods from heaven.
  • The gods' anger becomes a blaze of light, which takes form as Durga.
  • Each god gives a weapon: Shiva's trident, Vishnu's discus, Indra's thunderbolt. She rides a lion.
  • She fights him for nine days and nights as he changes shape.
  • On the tenth day she kills him as he comes out of the buffalo's body.
  • Meaning: Shakti defeats pride and evil that no one else can; good protects dharma.
Mission 4

Shailaputra: day one

  • Many Hindus honour nine forms of Durga (Navadurga), one each night.
  • Night one: Shailaputra (also spelled Shailaputri), "daughter of the mountain".
  • Her story: as Sati, she married Shiva. Her father Daksha insulted Shiva at a great sacrifice, and Sati gave up her life in its fire.
  • She was reborn as the daughter of Himavat, king of the Himalayas, and married Shiva again (as Parvati).
  • Shown riding the bull Nandi, with a trident and a lotus.
  • Meaning: devotion that is stronger than death; a new beginning for the nine nights.

Mission 4

How Navaratri is celebrated

  • Fasting or simple food; daily puja and arti to the Goddess; a lamp kept burning.
  • Chanting scriptures: the Devi Mahatmya (also called Durga Saptashati).
  • Dancing (Gujarat): garba in circles around a lamp, and dandiya raas with sticks.
  • Durga Puja (Bengal): huge images of Durga in decorated pandals; on the last day they are carried to a river and immersed.
  • Golu (South India): steps of dolls showing gods and stories.
  • Many honour young girls as forms of the Goddess (kanya puja) on the eighth or ninth day.
Mission 4

Dussehra: Rama's victory

  • On the tenth day many Hindus remember Rama's defeat of Ravana, who had kidnapped Sita.
  • Ramlila: the Ramayana is acted out over the nights of Navaratri.
  • Huge effigies of Ravana, his brother Kumbhakarna and son Meghnad are burned, often packed with fireworks.
  • In Bengal the same day ends Durga Puja: Durga's victory, not Rama's.
  • Rama is said to have prayed to Durga before the battle, so the two stories meet.
  • About 20 days later, Diwali marks Rama's return home.
Mission 4Diversity

Different Hindus, different emphases

  • Shaktas treat Navaratri as one of the holiest times of the year; for many Vaishnavas the Rama story at Dussehra is central.
  • Region shapes it: garba in Gujarat, Durga Puja in Bengal, golu in the South, Ramlila in the North.
  • In Britain: garba nights in community halls and mandirs often draw thousands, including young people and non-Hindus.
Mission 4Evaluate

"Festivals are the most important part of being a Hindu today."

  • For: festivals pass on stories and values to children; they gather families and communities; in Britain they keep identity strong.
  • Against: daily puja, dharma and good actions matter every day, not only at festivals.
  • Against: for those seeking moksha through knowledge or meditation, inner practice matters more than outward celebration.
  • Another view: festivals are where belief becomes action: they make everyday dharma visible.
  • Judge it by: What does a Hindu need most to live by dharma? Does it differ for a child, a householder and a sannyasin? Do festivals change behaviour after they end?

Mission 5

A tirtha: a crossing place

  • Tirtha means a ford: a shallow place to cross a river.
  • A holy place is a crossing between this world and the divine, and between samsara and moksha.
  • Tirtha-yatra = a journey to a tirtha: pilgrimage.
  • Tirthas include rivers, mountains, temples, and places linked with the lives of deities or saints.
  • History: the Mahabharata already lists tirthas; the Puranas praise each place's power; tradition says the teacher Shankara (8th century) founded monasteries at four sacred points of India.
  • Pilgrimage is not compulsory for Hindus. It is a choice, often made after a vow or at a turning point in life.
Mission 5

Why go, and what happens there?

  • Darshan: to see, and be seen by, the deity in a holy place.
  • Purification: bathing in holy water to wash away bad karma.
  • Vows: thanks for an answered prayer, or a request for help.
  • Ancestors: rites (shraddha) for the dead; scattering ashes.
  • Moksha: some places are believed to bring liberation.
  • Practices: bathing, circling a holy place clockwise (parikrama), puja and arti, offerings, giving to the poor, fasting, singing bhajans, meeting holy people.
Mission 5

Ganga, the river goddess

  • Many Hindus honour the Ganges (Ganga) as a goddess: Ganga Ma, Mother Ganga.
  • Story: King Bhagiratha prays for Ganga to come down from heaven to purify his ancestors' ashes. Shiva catches her in his hair so her fall does not shatter the earth.
  • Bathing in her water is believed to wash away sins and bad karma.
  • Pilgrims take Gangajal (Ganges water) home in sealed pots, for puja, for the dying and for rites.
  • Many hope their ashes will be placed in the Ganges.
Mission 5

Varanasi (Kashi)

  • On the Ganges in North India; also called Kashi, "city of light", and Banaras. Shiva's city.
  • Ghats: stone steps down to the river. At dawn pilgrims bathe, pray and offer flowers and lamps.
  • Many believe that to die in Kashi, or be cremated there, brings moksha: Shiva whispers a liberating mantra to the dying.
  • Cremation at Manikarnika Ghat goes on day and night.
  • Each evening a great Ganga arti with lamps is offered at Dashashwamedh Ghat.
  • The Kashi Vishwanath Mandir ("Lord of the universe"): one of the 12 jyotirlingas, sacred shrines of Shiva. Pilgrims also visit other mandirs, e.g. Annapurna and Sankat Mochan (Hanuman).
  • Across the river, Ramnagar Fort (your spec: "Ramgarh Fort") hosts a famous month-long Ramlila.

Mission 5

Kumbh Mela

  • Kumbh = pot. Story: gods and demons churned the ocean for amrita, the nectar of immortality. As they fought over the pot, drops fell at four places.
  • These four host the Kumbh Mela in turn, each about every 12 years: Prayagraj (Allahabad), Haridwar, Ujjain and Nashik.
  • At Prayagraj the Ganga, the Yamuna and the unseen Sarasvati are believed to meet: the Triveni Sangam.
  • Dates are set by the positions of the planets. On the main days, sadhus (holy men) lead the royal bath, then millions bathe.
  • Believed to wash away sins and bring merit, even moksha; also a vast gathering of teachers, debate and devotion.
  • UNESCO listed the Kumbh Mela as intangible cultural heritage in 2017.
Mission 5

Gangotri: to the source

  • Gangotri: a small town and temple high in the Himalayas (Uttarakhand), where the goddess Ganga is worshipped.
  • Tradition says Bhagiratha prayed here for Ganga to descend.
  • The glacier where the river begins, Gaumukh ("cow's mouth"), is a hard trek further on.
  • Here the river is called the Bhagirathi; it becomes the Ganga lower down.
  • Open only from spring to autumn; in winter snow closes the temple.
  • One of the Char Dham ("four abodes") of the Himalayas, with Yamunotri, Kedarnath and Badrinath.
  • Pilgrims take pure water from the source home with them.
Mission 5

Vrindavan

  • A town on the Yamuna in the Braj region, where Krishna is believed to have spent his youth with the cowherds and Radha.
  • A Vaishnava centre: thousands of temples and shrines (e.g. Banke Bihari; ISKCON's Krishna-Balaram Mandir).
  • Pilgrims walk the parikrama around the town and around Govardhan Hill, which Krishna is said to have lifted to shelter the villagers.
  • Kirtan, Holi and Janmashtami are celebrated here with great devotion; pilgrims greet each other "Radhe Radhe".
  • Purpose: bhakti: to love Krishna where he lived, and to be among his devotees.
Mission 5Diversity

Vaishnava and Shaiva sacred sites

  • Vaishnava sites (Vishnu and his avatars): Vrindavan and Mathura (Krishna); Ayodhya (Rama); Puri (Jagannatha); Tirupati; Badrinath.
  • Shaiva sites (Shiva): Varanasi; the 12 jyotirlingas (e.g. Kedarnath, Somnath); Mount Kailash, Shiva's home.
  • Vaishnava pilgrimage often stresses bhakti: darshan, kirtan, the stories of the avatar's life.
  • Shaiva pilgrimage often stresses renunciation and liberation: sadhus, austerity, moksha in Kashi.
  • Shared: both honour the Ganges, said to flow from Vishnu's foot and through Shiva's hair; both crowd the Kumbh Mela.

Mission 5Evaluate

"Pilgrimage is not important for Hindus today."

  • For: pilgrimage is not compulsory; God is believed to be everywhere, including in the heart and the home shrine.
  • For: some texts and teachers say the true tirthas are inner: truth, forgiveness, self-control. The Arya Samaj (a Hindu reform movement founded in 1875) rejects the idea that bathing removes sin.
  • For: cost, crowds, distance from Britain, and pollution of rivers.
  • Against: darshan in holy places is believed to be especially powerful; bathing, rites for ancestors and vows matter to millions.
  • Against: pilgrimage builds faith and community; British Hindus often visit sites when in India, or go to Bhaktivedanta Manor or Neasden.
  • Judge it by: Is the outer journey a help to the inner one, or a replacement for it? Does importance depend on the person's path (bhakti, jnana)? Is "not compulsory" the same as "not important"?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Hindus believe an avatar is a descent of the Supreme (mostly Vishnu) to restore dharma "from age to age" (Gita 4.7–8); Rama and Krishna are the best loved.
  • A murti is a sacred form; after prana pratishtha many Hindus believe the deity is present; others see it as an aid to focus; the Arya Samaj rejects image worship.
  • Who is an avatar is debated: Vaishnavas and Shaivas differ; Hindus may honour the Buddha or Jesus as avatars, which Buddhists and most Christians do not accept.

Mission 2

  • Shruti ("heard": Vedas, Upanishads) and smriti ("remembered": Gita, Ramayana, Puranas); the Gita and Ramayana give models of dharma, with Rama and Krishna as role models.
  • Shankara (Advaita: one reality, liberation by knowledge) and Ramanuja (Vishishtadvaita: personal God, liberation by devotion and grace).
  • Brahmins, gurus and swamis teach and lead rites; Gandhi applied ahimsa and the Gita to public life.

Mission 3

  • Diwali (new moon of Kartika): Rama and Sita's return to Ayodhya, Lakshmi puja, Krishna and Narakasura; lamps, rangoli, sweets and gifts; light over darkness (Brihadaranyaka Upanishad 1.3.28).
  • Holi (full moon of Phalguna): Prahlada and Holika, Krishna and Radha; bonfire, colours, forgiveness and equality.

Mission 4

  • Navaratri: nine nights of Shakti; Durga defeats Mahishasura; fasting, garba, Durga Puja, golu, chanting the Devi Mahatmya.
  • Navaratri honours nine forms of Durga, beginning with Shailaputra (Shailaputri).
  • Dussehra: Rama's victory over Ravana (Ramlila, effigies, fireworks); in Bengal, Durga's victory.

Mission 5

  • A tirtha is a "crossing place"; pilgrimage (tirtha-yatra) is for darshan, purification, vows, ancestors and moksha, and is not compulsory.
  • The Ganges is Mother Ganga; Varanasi is Shiva's city, where many hope to die and be cremated.
  • Kumbh Mela: four sites, about every 12 years each; bathing at the Sangam.
  • Vrindavan: Krishna's Braj, bhakti and parikrama.
