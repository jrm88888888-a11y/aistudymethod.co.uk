Mission 1

Mission 1: What decides how much a job pays, and why can two jobs pay so differently?

Make a guess: Why are some jobs paid much more than others?

Mission 1

A market for workers

  • In the labour market, workers supply labour and employers demand it.
  • The wage is the price of labour.
  • Derived demand: firms want workers because people want the goods and services they make. More online shopping → more delivery drivers wanted.
  • Diagram: wage on the vertical axis, number of workers on the horizontal.
    • Demand for labour slopes down: at a lower wage, firms hire more.
    • Supply of labour slopes up: at a higher wage, more people want the job.
Mission 1

What changes the demand for labour

  • A change in the wage → a movement along the demand curve.
  • The whole curve shifts when:
    • demand for the product changes (a boom in home deliveries → more drivers wanted)
    • productivity changes (each worker produces more, so each is worth more to the firm)
    • the cost of machines that can replace workers changes (cheaper self-checkouts → fewer cashiers wanted)
    • the cost of other inputs changes
  • Say: "a higher wage causes a contraction in demand for labour" — not: "demand for labour falls". The curve has not moved.
Mission 1

What changes the supply of labour

  • A change in the wage → a movement along the supply curve.
  • The whole curve shifts when:
    • population grows or shrinks
    • migration: workers arrive from, or leave for, other areas or countries
    • age structure: more people of working age
    • retirement age or school-leaving age changes
    • female participation: more women in paid work (childcare, attitudes)
    • skills and qualifications: more people trained for a job
    • mobility: how easily people can change job or move
    • non-wage factors of a job: conditions, hours, holidays, training, status
Quick check
?Which of these would shift the supply curve of nurses to the right?
Mission 1

How the wage is set

  • Step 1: demand for labour D and supply of labour S.
  • Step 2: they cross at the equilibrium wage (We) and employment (Le).
  • Above We: more people want the job than firms want to hire (excess supply). The wage is pushed down.
  • Below We: firms cannot fill jobs (excess demand, a shortage). The wage is pushed up.
  • Step 3: a shift in D or S gives a new We and Le.
Mission 1

Worked example: shifts

  • Illustrative data. Warehouse workers in a town: We = £12 an hour, Le = 520 workers.
  • Demand rises (online orders boom): D shifts right.
    • New wage £14, new employment 640.
    • Wage ↑ and employment ↑.
  • Supply rises instead (100 more people want the work at each wage): S shifts right.
    • New wage £11, new employment 560.
    • Wage ↓ but employment ↑.

  • Two jobs pay differently when their demand and supply differ: strong demand for the workers, or few people able and willing to do the job, means a higher wage.

Quick check
?Many people move into a town looking for warehouse work. Other things equal, what happens in the town's market for warehouse workers?
Mission check

?A country raises the age at which young people may leave education from 16 to 18. In the market for workers aged 16 to 17, what is the most likely effect?

Anything that changes how many people can or will do a job shifts supply, and moves pay and jobs in opposite ways.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why is the pay that lands in your bank account smaller than the pay you were offered?

Make a guess: Someone is offered a job paying 30,000 a year. Roughly how much reaches their bank account?

Mission 2

Gross pay

  • Gross pay: total pay before any deductions.
  • Wage: paid by the hour. Weekly gross pay = hourly rate × hours worked.
  • Salary: a fixed amount a year, usually paid monthly (annual salary ÷ 12).
  • Gross pay can also include:
    • overtime: extra hours, often at a higher rate (time-and-a-half = 1.5 × the hourly rate; double time = 2 ×)
    • bonus: an extra payment for good results
    • commission: a % of the value of sales made
  • From 1 April 2026 the UK National Living Wage (age 21 and over) is £12.71 an hour.
Mission 2

Worked example: a week's pay

  • Sam earns £12.71 an hour. Basic week: 40 hours. Plus 4 hours of overtime at time-and-a-half.
  • Basic pay: 40 × £12.71 = £508.40
  • Overtime rate: 1.5 × £12.71 = £19.065 an hour
  • Overtime pay: 4 × £19.065 = £76.26
  • Gross pay: £508.40 + £76.26 = £584.66
  • Tip: work out the overtime rate first, then multiply by the overtime hours.
Quick check
?Ana earns £12.71 an hour. One week she works 20 normal hours plus 4 hours of overtime at double time. What is her gross pay for the week?
Mission 2

From gross to net

  • Deductions: money taken from gross pay before you receive it.
  • Main deductions:
    • Income tax: paid to the government
    • National Insurance (NI): paid to the government; builds a right to the State Pension and some benefits
    • Pension contributions: saved for your own retirement
    • other deductions are possible (for example, student loan repayments)
  • Net pay (take-home pay) = gross pay − deductions.
  • A payslip shows gross pay, each deduction and net pay.
  • Say: "net pay is after deductions" — not: "net pay is before tax".
Mission 2

Income tax

  • Above £100,000, the Personal Allowance shrinks, reaching zero at £125,140.
  • Income tax is progressive: higher earners pay a larger share of their income.
Mission 2

National Insurance and pensions

  • NI (employees), 2026 to 2027:
    • 8% on pay between £242 and £967 a week (£1,048 to £4,189 a month)
    • 2% on pay above £967 a week
  • For a steady salary, that is about 8% of pay between £12,570 and £50,270 a year.
  • Workplace pension: most employees are enrolled automatically.
    • Legal minimum: 8% of earnings between £6,240 and £50,270 a year.
    • At least 3% from the employer; the employee pays the rest (5%, including tax relief).
  • In the worked examples here the pension is a simple 5% of gross pay. Real schemes vary.
Mission 2

Worked example: £30,000 a year

  • Salary £30,000. Illustrative pension: 5% of gross pay (no tax relief shown).
  • Income tax: taxable income = £30,000 − £12,570 = £17,430. Tax = 20% × £17,430 = £3,486
  • NI: 8% × £17,430 = £1,394.40
  • Pension: 5% × £30,000 = £1,500
  • Net pay = £30,000 − £3,486 − £1,394.40 − £1,500 = £23,619.60 a year
  • That is about £1,968.30 a month, out of a gross £2,500.
Quick check
?Use the rates on the earlier screens and the method in the previous screen. Kai earns £20,000 a year and pays 5% of gross pay into a pension. What is his net pay for the year?
Mission 2

A pay rise: how much do you keep?

  • The marginal rate: the share of the next £1 earned that is deducted.
  • Basic-rate taxpayer: 20% tax + 8% NI + 5% pension: 33p of each extra £1 is deducted.
  • So a £1,000 rise on £30,000 adds £670 to net pay.
  • Higher-rate taxpayer (above £50,270): 40% tax + 2% NI: keeps 58p of each extra £1 before pension.
  • The average tax rate = income tax ÷ gross pay:
    • £20,000: £1,486 ÷ £20,000 = 7.4%
    • £30,000: £3,486 ÷ £30,000 = 11.6%
  • The average rate rises with income: that is what makes income tax progressive.
Quick check
?Raj earns £40,000 a year and gets a pay rise of £2,000. He stays in the basic-rate band. Ignoring his pension, how much of the rise does he keep after income tax and NI?
Mission check

?Mia works part-time and earns £12,000 a year. Her pay rises to £14,000 a year. Using the 2026 to 2027 rates, how much more income tax does she pay each year?

Income tax is charged only on income above the Personal Allowance, and only at the rate of each band.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why will a shop hand over real goods for a piece of paper that is worth almost nothing on its own?

Make a guess: Why will a shop hand over real goods for a piece of paper that is worth almost nothing on its own?

Mission 3

Life without money

  • Barter: swapping goods or services directly, with no money.
  • You have eggs and want shoes. You must find someone who has shoes and wants eggs.
  • This is the double coincidence of wants. It is rare, so barter wastes time.
  • More problems:
    • how many eggs is a pair of shoes worth? Every item needs a price in every other item.
    • you can't split a cow to buy a loaf.
  • Money solves these problems.
Mission 3

What money does

  • Medium of exchange: money is accepted in payment for goods and services. Sell eggs for money, then buy shoes with it. No double coincidence needed.
  • A banknote is paper worth almost nothing in itself. It works as money because people accept it, trusting that others will accept it too.
  • The central bank works to keep the value of money stable, so the trust holds.
Mission 3

Commercial banks

  • Commercial banks (high street banks) are businesses owned by shareholders. They aim to make a profit.
  • For savers: a safe place for money, interest on savings, and a way to make payments.
  • For borrowers: loans, overdrafts and mortgages (loans to buy a home).
  • For firms: loans that fund investment in new machines and buildings.
  • Banks bring savers and borrowers together. They pay savers a lower interest rate than they charge borrowers.
  • Illustrative data: a bank pays 3% on 100,000 of deposits (3,000) and lends the 100,000 at 7% (7,000). The difference, 4,000, pays its costs and gives its profit.
Mission 3

Building societies, insurers and others

  • Building society: owned by its members (its savers and borrowers), not shareholders. It is mutual.
    • Focus: savings accounts and mortgages.
    • With no shareholders to pay, it can offer better interest rates to members.
  • Insurance company: people and firms pay a premium; the insurer pays out if something goes wrong (a fire, a crash, a theft).
    • Many pay in; few claim. The risk is pooled.
Quick check
?How does a commercial bank mainly make a profit from savers and borrowers?
Mission 3

Why the financial sector matters

  • Consumers: safe saving, easy payments, mortgages to buy homes, insurance against loss.
  • Producers: loans for investment, insurance for buildings and stock, payments to suppliers and workers.
  • Government: borrows by selling bonds; taxes and benefits are paid through banks; the sector employs many people and pays tax.
  • But it can go wrong. In 2007, savers queued to withdraw money from the UK bank Northern Rock. In the 2008 global financial crisis, governments rescued failing banks.
  • So the importance of the sector depends on trust and stability. That is why the central bank supervises banks.
Quick check
?A small bakery wants new ovens but does not have the money. How does the financial sector most directly help?
Mission check

?In some prisoner-of-war camps in the Second World War, prisoners got food parcels and swapped items. Cigarettes came to be accepted in almost every swap, even by non-smokers. Why did this make trading easier?

Anything that nearly everyone will accept in payment can work as money, even a cigarette.

Mission 4

Mission 4: If a bank pays you more for saving, why might shops across the country start to struggle?

Make a guess: If a bank pays you more for saving, why might shops across the country start to struggle?

Mission 4

What is an interest rate?

  • Interest: the price of borrowing money, or the reward for saving it.
  • Interest rate: interest as a percentage of the amount, usually per year.
  • Savers lend their money to a bank, so the bank pays them interest.
  • Borrowers pay interest to the bank on loans, overdrafts, credit cards and mortgages.
  • One rate change pulls two ways: a higher rate is good news for savers and bad news for borrowers.
Mission 4

Worked example: calculating interest

Illustrative data.

  • Mia saves 2,000 at 4% a year.
  • Interest after 1 year = 2,000 × 4 ÷ 100 = 80. Balance: 2,080.
  • Year 2: interest is paid on 2,080 (interest on interest: compound interest).
    • 2,080 × 4 ÷ 100 = 83.20. Balance: 2,163.20.
  • If the rate rises from 4% to 5%: first-year interest = 2,000 × 5 ÷ 100 = 100, not 80.
  • Borrowing works the same way. A loan of 10,000 at 6% costs 600 in interest a year; at 7%, 700.
  • So a 1 percentage point rise adds 20 a year to Mia's savings, and 100 a year to the loan's cost.
Quick check
?Jo puts 1,500 into a savings account paying 2% a year compound interest and takes nothing out. How much is in the account after 2 years?
Mission 4Graph lab

Illustrative data. You save 5,000. At 3% a year, you earn 150 interest in the first year.

  • Takeaway: interest is proportional to the rate. Over many years, compound interest makes the gap between rates grow: after 10 years, 6,719.58 at 3% but 8,954.24 at 6%.
Mission 4

Interest rates and households

When interest rates rise:

  • Saving is more rewarding, so people save more.
  • Borrowing costs more, so people borrow less, especially for big items bought on credit: cars, furniture, holidays.
  • Households with variable-rate mortgages pay more each month, leaving less to spend.
  • On a fixed-rate loan, the payments change only when the fixed deal ends.
  • Result: consumer spending usually falls.
  • Illustrative data: on a 150,000 mortgage, interest at 2% is 3,000 a year; at 5% it is 7,500 a year.
  • When interest rates fall, the opposite: less saving, more borrowing, more spending.
  • Winners and losers: savers living on interest (often retired people) gain from a rise; borrowers lose.
Mission 4

Interest rates and firms

  • Investment: firms often borrow to buy machines or build factories.
  • Illustrative data: a new machine is expected to earn a return of 6% a year on its cost.
    • Borrowing at 4%: it pays for itself, so the firm invests.
    • Borrowing at 8%: the interest is more than the return, so the firm does not.
  • Higher rates also mean:
    • firms with existing loans pay more interest, so profits fall
    • firms with spare cash may save it rather than invest it
    • customers spend less, so sales fall
  • Lower rates encourage investment and borrowing.
Quick check
?A central bank raises interest rates. Which outcome is most likely?
Mission 4

Monetary policy and its limits

  • Monetary policy: the central bank changes interest rates to influence spending, borrowing, saving and investment, and so growth, jobs and prices.
  • Real world: the Bank of England cut Bank Rate to 0.1% in March 2020 to support spending in the pandemic. It then raised it in steps to 5.25% by August 2023 to bring down high inflation.
  • How strong is the effect? It depends on:
    • fixed vs variable loans: people on fixed-rate mortgages feel a change only when their deal ends, so there are time lags
    • confidence: if people fear losing their jobs, low rates may not make them borrow and spend
    • whether banks pass the change on to their customers
    • other influences on spending, such as income and taxes
Mission check

?Interest rates fall from 5% to 2%. Which household is most likely to increase its spending in the next few months?

The same rate change helps some households and hurts others; who borrows and who saves decides which way spending moves.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The wage is set where the demand for labour meets the supply of labour.
  • Shifts in demand move wage and employment the same way; shifts in supply move them opposite ways.

Mission 2

  • Gross pay is pay before deductions; net pay is what you take home.
  • Income tax, National Insurance and pension contributions are the main deductions.
  • Tax is charged band by band on income above the Personal Allowance, so it is progressive.

Mission 3

  • Money is a medium of exchange: it ends the need for a double coincidence of wants.
  • Commercial banks link savers and borrowers, and profit from the gap between interest rates.
  • Building societies are owned by their members; insurers pool risk.

Mission 4

  • An interest rate is the price of borrowing and the reward for saving, as a percentage per year.
  • Higher rates encourage saving and discourage borrowing, spending and investment; lower rates do the opposite.
  • Interest = amount × rate ÷ 100; compound interest pays interest on interest.
