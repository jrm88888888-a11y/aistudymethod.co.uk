Mission 1

What is specialisation?

  • Specialisation: concentrating on a limited range of tasks or products.
  • It happens at every level:
    • Individual: a dentist, an electrician, a coder.
    • Firm: a company that only makes bicycle brakes.
    • Region: a city known for finance or for films.
    • Country: one economy exports coffee, another aircraft parts.
  • Division of labour: splitting the making of one product into separate tasks, each done by a different worker.
  • Division of labour is specialisation inside a firm.
Mission 1

Adam Smith's pin factory

  • Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations (1776), described a small pin workshop.
  • Pin-making was split into about 18 distinct operations: one draws the wire, one straightens it, one cuts it, one sharpens the point…
  • 10 workers made upwards of 48,000 pins a day: about 4,800 each.
  • Working alone and untrained, Smith wrote, each "certainly could not" have made twenty pins a day.
  • Smith's three reasons output soared:
    • Dexterity: repeating one task builds skill and speed.
    • Time saved: no switching between tasks and tools.
    • Machinery: a narrow task is easier to mechanise, so machines get invented.
  • Real case, 1913: Ford's moving assembly line cut the time to assemble a car from about 12.5 hours to 93 minutes by early 1914.
Mission 1

Worked example: output and cost per unit

A sandwich bar has 4 workers, each paid 12 per hour (illustrative data).

  • Before: each worker makes whole sandwiches: 30 per hour each, so 120 per hour in total.
  • After: one slices, one fills, one wraps, one serves: 200 per hour in total.
  • Step 1: output per worker after = 200 ÷ 4 = 50 per hour.
  • Step 2: labour productivity rises from 30 to 50: (50 − 30) ÷ 30 × 100 = +66.7%.
  • Step 3: labour cost per sandwich before = 12 ÷ 30 = 0.40.
  • Step 4: labour cost per sandwich after = 12 ÷ 50 = 0.24: down 40%.
  • Same workers, same wage, same hours: more output, lower cost per unit.
Quick check
?A bakery has 6 workers, each paid 90 per day. When each worker bakes whole loaves, the bakery makes 120 loaves a day. After it divides the work into separate tasks, the same 6 workers make 180 loaves a day (illustrative data). What is the labour cost per loaf after the change?
Mission 1

Who gains?

  • Firms: higher labour productivity, lower unit costs, more output from the same inputs.
  • Workers: can build expert skills; easier to train for one task; possibly higher pay as productivity rises.
  • Consumers: lower prices, more output, often better quality from experts.
  • The economy: more output from scarce resources: higher living standards.
  • Countries: specialising and trading lets each focus on what it does best.
Mission 1

The downsides

  • Workers: repetitive work → boredom, lower motivation, more mistakes, absence and staff turnover.
  • Smith himself warned (Book V) that a lifetime of "a few simple operations" can leave a worker's mind dulled.
  • Narrow skills: if a task is automated or demand changes, workers face structural unemployment.
  • Interdependence: if one stage stops, the whole line stops.
  • Countries and regions: over-reliance on a few products; exposed to a shock in one market or supply chain.
  • Real case, 2021: a global shortage of computer chips forced car makers around the world to cut production.
  • Loss of variety: standardised products may cut consumer choice.
Quick check
?A car plant buys a key electronic part from a single specialist supplier abroad. A fire at that supplier's factory halts its output, and within a week the car plant stops its own production line. Which disadvantage of specialisation does this show best?
Mission 1

Evaluate: how far should division of labour go?

It depends on…

  • The size of the market. Smith: division of labour is "limited by the extent of the market". A village baker selling 50 loaves a day cannot keep 6 specialists busy.
  • The type of task. Routine, repeated tasks gain most. Creative or varied work (design, care work) gains less, and repetition can harm quality.
  • How jobs are designed. Job rotation and teamwork can keep productivity gains while cutting boredom and absence.
  • Time period. Gains appear quickly; the risks (deskilling, dependence on one supplier or one export) show up later.
  • Say: "labour productivity rose" (output per worker) — not: "production rose", which could just mean more workers.
Mission 1

Specialise, then exchange

  • A specialist makes far more of one thing than they need, and none of everything else.
  • So specialisation only works with exchange: selling what you make to buy what you need.
  • Individual: a nurse sells her labour for a wage, then buys food, rent and transport.
  • National: a country exports what it specialises in and imports the rest.
  • The more people specialise, the more they depend on trade, and on a cheap, trusted way to trade.
Mission 1

Barter and its problem

  • Barter: swapping goods or services directly, with no money.
  • Problem 1: it needs a double coincidence of wants: each side must want exactly what the other offers, at the same time.
  • Problem 2: no common measure: with 100 goods, every good needs a price in terms of the other 99 (4,950 exchange rates).
  • Problem 3: indivisibility: a cow cannot be split to buy one pair of shoes.
  • Money fixes this as a medium of exchange: anything widely accepted in payment. Sell to anyone for money, then buy from anyone.
  • So money makes specialisation and exchange far cheaper and quicker.
Quick check
?In a village with no money, a farmer has eggs and wants shoes. The shoemaker has shoes but wants a haircut, not eggs. What problem does this show, and how does money solve it?
Mission check

?A car-repair garage keeps the same 8 mechanics on the same hours. Instead of each mechanic doing whole jobs, each now does one type of job: tyres, brakes or servicing. Repairs completed per mechanic rise by a third. Which explanation fits the analysis of the division of labour?

Same workers, same hours, more output: the division of labour raises productivity.

Mission 2

Life without money: barter

  • Barter: swapping goods and services directly. Apples for bread, a haircut for a meal.
  • Problem 1: the double coincidence of wants. You need someone who has what you want and wants what you have.
  • Problem 2: no common measure. How many apples is a haircut worth? Every pair of goods needs its own price.
  • Problem 3: hard to save. Apples rot.
  • Problem 4: hard to split. Half a cow is not much use.
  • Money solves all four. It makes specialisation and trade far easier.
Mission 2

The functions of money

  • Money: anything generally accepted as a means of payment.
  • Its functions:
    • Medium of exchange: accepted in payment for goods and services.
    • Unit of account (or measure of value): prices and incomes are stated in it, so values can be compared.
    • Store of value: holds purchasing power over time.
    • Standard of deferred payment: debts and future payments are stated in it.
  • Say: "money is a medium of exchange" — not: "money is something you buy things with because it is valuable".
Mission 2

When money stops working: Zimbabwe

  • 2007–2008: prices in Zimbabwe rose faster and faster: hyperinflation.
  • Official annual inflation reached 231 million per cent (July 2008).
  • In early 2009 the central bank issued a 100 trillion Zimbabwe dollar note.
  • What failed first: the store of value. Money lost value in days, so people spent it at once.
  • Then the unit of account: shops priced goods in US dollars or changed prices several times a day.
  • Then the medium of exchange: sellers refused Zimbabwe dollars.
  • In 2009 Zimbabwe allowed the US dollar and other currencies to be used instead.
Quick check
?In a country with very high inflation, a family is paid in the local currency. They change their wages into a foreign currency on payday and keep their savings in it. Which function of the local currency has broken down most clearly?
Mission 2

What makes good money?

  • Acceptable: people trust that others will take it.
  • Scarce / limited in supply: if anyone can make more, it loses value.
  • Durable: does not rot or wear out quickly.
  • Portable: easy to carry.
  • Divisible: can be split into small amounts.
  • Uniform: every unit is the same.
  • Hard to forge.
  • Gold scores well on most. Apples score badly on durability and uniformity.
Mission 2

Forms of money

  • Commodity money: has value itself. Gold, silver, salt.
  • Representative money: paper that could be swapped for gold (a gold standard).
  • Fiat money: notes and coins with little value as objects. Accepted because the law makes them legal tender and people trust they will be accepted.
  • Bank deposits: numbers in accounts, spent by card, transfer or cheque.
    • In the UK, bank deposits are about 97% of broad money; notes and coins only about 3% (Bank of England, 2014).
  • Cryptocurrencies (e.g. Bitcoin): a debated case.
    • Their prices swing a lot. Few shops price goods in them.
    • Most central banks say they are not money in the full sense.
Quick check
?A café accepts payment in a cryptocurrency. Its price in US dollars often moves by 10% or more in a week, so the café must rewrite its menu prices in the cryptocurrency every day. Which function of money is failing most directly here?
Mission 2

Liquidity

  • Liquidity: how quickly and cheaply an asset can be turned into spending power without losing value.
  • Most liquid to least: notes and coins → current account deposits → savings deposits → fixed-term deposits → bonds and shares → houses.
  • Money is the most liquid asset. Narrow and broad measures of money are drawn along this ladder.
Mission 2

Narrow money and broad money

  • Narrow money: money that can be spent at once.
    • Notes and coins, plus banks' reserve balances at the central bank.
    • Some measures of narrow money also count current account deposits.
  • Broad money: narrow money plus deposits held in banks and building societies, including savings deposits.
    • UK measure: M4.
  • Most broad money is created by commercial banks when they lend.
  • Say: "broad money includes bank deposits" — not: "money means notes and coins".
Quick check
?Which of these is counted in broad money (M4) but not in narrow money?
Mission check

?In a prisoner-of-war camp in the Second World War, cigarettes became money. Prisoners who did not smoke still accepted cigarettes in payment for food and clothes. What best explains why?

Money is whatever people generally accept in payment, because each person expects others to accept it too.

Mission 3

What demand means

  • Demand: the quantity of a good that buyers are willing and able to buy at each price, over a given time period.
  • Law of demand: as price rises, quantity demanded falls, ceteris paribus (other things equal).
  • A demand schedule lists the quantity demanded at each price. Illustrative data:

  • Demand is the whole curve, not one point on it.

Mission 3

Movement or shift?

  • A change in the good's own price → a movement along D.
  • A change in anything else that affects buyers → a shift of the whole curve.
  • Step 1: price falls from P1 to P2 → slide along D from point a to point b.
  • Step 2: D1 → D2 to the right: more is demanded at every price.
  • Say: "a fall in price causes an extension of demand (a rise in quantity demanded)" — not: "demand increases".
  • A price rise → contraction of demand. A shift right → increase in demand; shift left → decrease.

Mission 4

Joint demand: goods used together

  • Joint demand: goods bought together, such as games consoles and games, or printers and ink. Also called complements.
  • Step 1: price of printers falls → extension of demand for printers.
  • Step 2: more printers in use → demand for ink shifts right.
  • Step 3: ink price and quantity rise.
  • The price of a complement is a shift factor of demand.
  • Say: "demand for ink increases (shifts)" — not: "the demand for printers shifts" (a change in a good's own price is a movement).
Mission 4

Competitive demand: goods that replace each other

  • Competitive demand: goods that are substitutes, such as coffee and tea, or train and coach travel.
  • Price of coffee rises → contraction of demand for coffee → demand for tea shifts right.
  • The closer the substitutes, the bigger the shift.
Quick check
?The price of printers falls sharply. What happens in the market for printer ink?
Mission 4

Composite demand: one good, many uses

  • Composite demand: a good wanted for several different uses.
  • Milk → drinking milk, cheese, butter, cream.
  • If demand for cheese rises, more milk goes to cheese makers.
  • Less milk is left for butter: the supply of butter shifts left and its price rises.
  • Other examples: steel (cars, buildings, cans); land (homes, farms, shops).
  • Say: "composite demand: one good, many uses" — not: "joint demand" (goods used together).
Mission 4

Joint supply: by-products

  • Joint supply: making one good automatically makes another.
  • Cattle → beef and leather. Crude oil refining → petrol, diesel and jet fuel.
  • Step 1: demand for beef rises → more cattle are reared and slaughtered.
  • Step 2: more hides → supply of leather shifts right.
  • Step 3: leather price falls, quantity rises.
  • The two goods move in opposite price directions here.
Mission 4

Case: carbon dioxide, 2021

  • In the UK, food-grade CO₂ is a by-product of making fertiliser from natural gas.
  • In 2021, gas prices nearly quadrupled. In September, the fertiliser firm CF Industries halted its two UK plants.
  • Those plants made about 60% of the UK's food-grade CO₂.
  • CO₂ supply fell sharply. Poultry processors (which use it to stun birds) and drinks makers (for the fizz) warned of disruption. The government told food firms to expect CO₂ prices to rise about 400%.
  • The government agreed short-term financial support to restart one plant, to give the market time to adjust.
  • A gas market shock cut the supply of CO₂ and raised its price, in a market that seems to have nothing to do with gas.
Mission 4

Competitive supply: one resource, two uses

  • Competitive supply: firms can make one good or another with the same resources.
  • A farmer's field: wheat or barley. A factory line: cars or vans.
  • Price of barley rises → farmers switch land to barley → supply of wheat shifts left.
  • Wheat price rises, quantity falls.

Mission 4

Knock-on effects

  • One change can ripple through several markets. Illustrative chain:
  • More people buy electric cars.
  • Joint demand: demand for home chargers rises.
  • A factor market too: demand for electricians who fit them rises; their wages rise.
  • Competitive demand: demand for petrol cars falls.
  • Composite demand: more lithium for car batteries leaves less for other batteries.
  • How big is each knock-on? It depends on how closely the goods are linked (cross elasticity, a later lesson) and on the elasticity of supply in each market.
Mission check

?Crushing sunflower seeds produces sunflower oil and sunflower meal, which is sold as animal feed. Demand for sunflower oil rises sharply. What is the most likely effect in the market for sunflower meal?

When goods come from the same process, a boom in one makes the other more plentiful.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Specialisation and the division of labour raise labour productivity (Smith: dexterity, time saved, machinery) and cut unit costs.
  • Costs: monotony, narrow skills, interdependence and over-reliance; how far to go depends on market size and the task.
  • Specialists must exchange; money as a medium of exchange removes barter's need for a double coincidence of wants.

Mission 2

  • Money is anything generally accepted as payment. It removes the double coincidence of wants.
  • Functions: medium of exchange, unit of account, store of value.
  • A fourth: standard of deferred payment.
  • Narrow money is notes, coins and reserves; broad money adds bank deposits.

Mission 3

  • Demand is the whole schedule or curve; its own price moves you along it, every other factor shifts it.
  • Price fall: extension of demand; non-price change: increase or decrease in demand.

Mission 4

  • Joint demand (complements) move together; competitive demand (substitutes) move in opposite directions.
  • Joint supply: more of one product means more of its by-product.
  • Competitive supply: more of one product means less of the other.
