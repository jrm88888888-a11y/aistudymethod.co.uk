Mission 1

Births, deaths and natural change

  • Crude birth rate (CBR): births per 1,000 people per year.
  • Crude death rate (CDR): deaths per 1,000 people per year.
  • Natural increase: births exceed deaths. Natural decrease: deaths exceed births.
  • Natural increase rate (% a year) = (CBR − CDR) ÷ 10.
  • Population change = natural change + net migration (immigrants minus emigrants).

Mission 1

Fertility and replacement

  • Total fertility rate (TFR): the average number of children a woman would have at today's rates.
  • Replacement level is about 2.1: "the level required for a population to maintain a constant size in the long run without migration".
  • World TFR, 2024: 2.25. Niger 5.94; Japan 1.22.
  • At 3.27% a year, Niger doubles in about 70 ÷ 3.27 ≈ 21 years (the "rule of 70").
Mission 1

Global population growth

  • World population: 2.49 billion (1950) → 8.16 billion (2024).
  • The growth rate peaked at about 2.3% a year in 1963 and was 0.86% in 2024.
  • UN projection: a peak of about 10.3 billion in 2084, then a slow fall.
  • Most future growth is in sub-Saharan Africa: 1.24 billion (2024) → 2.09 billion projected for 2050.
Mission 1

The demographic transition model (DTM)

  • Stage 1: high birth and death rates; little growth.
  • Stage 2: death rates fall (food, clean water, vaccination); births stay high; rapid growth.
  • Stage 3: birth rates fall (fewer child deaths, education, contraception, cities); growth slows.
  • Stage 4: low birth and death rates; little growth.
  • Stage 5: births below deaths; the population falls.
Mission 1

The DTM and development

  • Stage tends to rise with development: low-income countries in stages 2–3, high-income countries in stages 4–5.
  • Niger (low income) fits late stage 2: deaths low, births still very high.
  • Japan (high income) fits stage 5: deaths (12.4) exceed births (6.1).
Quick check
?A country has a birth rate of 28 per 1,000, a death rate of 6, and its birth rate has fallen for 20 years. Which stage fits best?
Mission 1

How useful is the DTM?

  • Strengths: simple; fits the history of Europe and Japan; helps predict that falling child deaths come before falling births.
  • Limitations: based on Western Europe and North America; in many poorer countries death rates fell faster, with imported medicine.
  • It ignores migration and policy (such as China's birth limits).
  • It does not say when a country moves on; stage 5 was added later.
Mission 1

  • Economic: city jobs and the cost of raising children reduce family size; poverty keeps it high.
  • Political: government family-planning and health programmes; conflict slows progress.
Mission 1

Putting it together

  • Niger: many births, a young population, few deaths → rapid natural increase.
  • Japan: few births, many older people, more deaths → natural decrease.
  • Age structure matters: a young population has few deaths even where health care is weaker.
Mission check

?Bangladesh: birth rate 48.2 and death rate 17.1 in 1972; 20.0 and 5.1 in 2024. Why has its growth slowed?

(48.2 − 17.1) ÷ 10 = 3.11% in 1972; (20.0 − 5.1) ÷ 10 = 1.49% in 2024. The gap between births and deaths, shaped by fertility and age structure, decides growth.

Make a guess: From 1980 China limited most couples to one child. By 2021 it allowed three. Why the U-turn, and can governments change how many children people have?

Specification: what this mission covers

Cambridge IGCSE Geography (0460)

  • 6.2.3 named country (China): reasons for population growth or decline

Mission 2

Key terms

  • Anti-natalist policy: tries to lower the birth rate.
  • Pro-natalist policy: tries to raise the birth rate.
  • Youth dependency: many children (0–14) relying on people of working age (15–64).
  • Aged dependency: many older people (65+) relying on people of working age.
Mission 2

China's anti-natalist policy

  • A public letter of 25 September 1980 is often cited as the one-child policy's "official" start.
  • But fertility had already fallen: 6.09 children per woman (1970) → 2.75 (1979).
  • By 2000 it was 1.63, well below replacement (about 2.1).
  • The 1970s "Longer, Later, Fewer" campaign (later marriage, longer gaps, fewer children) came first: researchers estimate it cut fertility by about 0.88 children per woman.
  • So the one-child policy added to a fall already under way.
Mission 2

Impacts of the one-child policy

  • Fewer births, faster: fertility stayed below 2.1 from the 1990s.
  • More boys than girls: China's sex ratio at birth reached 117 boys per 100 girls in 2000; the world figure is about 105.
  • Ageing: people aged 65+ rose from 4.4% (1980) to 14.7% (2024) of China's population, and are projected to reach 30.9% by 2050.
  • Fewer young workers to support each older person.
Quick check
?Which evidence shows that China's fertility was falling before the one-child policy?
Mission 2

The U-turn: pro-natalist China

  • 2016: two children for all couples; the law was revised in 2015 "when China's two-child policy was fully implemented".
  • 2021: the law changed "to permit all couples to have three children".
  • Why: "the fertility rate in 2020 dropped to 1.3 children per woman"; the shift "addresses the challenges of a graying populace and declining fertility rates".
Mission 2

Did the U-turn work?

  • Births rose from 17.5 million (2015) to 18.3 million (2016), then fell to 8.8 million (2024).
  • 2025: 7.92 million births and 11.31 million deaths; population 1,404.89 million, down 3.39 million in a year.
  • Fertility was 1.01 in 2024.
  • So the two-child rule gave a brief rise; it did not reverse the fall.
Mission 2

What the evidence says

  • UN review: child and family allowances "appear to have a positive but weak effect on fertility".
  • Baby bonuses "seem to have a limited effect, mostly affecting the timing of childbearing".
  • Elsewhere, South Korea's fertility was 0.72 in 2023 and 0.75 in 2024: a small rise, still far below 2.1.
  • Verdict: anti-natalist policy worked where families already wanted fewer children; pro-natalist policy finds it hard to raise very low fertility.
Quick check
?Which conclusion do China's births and the UN's review of cash incentives best support?
Mission 2

China: why its population now falls

  • UN estimates show deaths above births every year since 2021; in 2025, 7.92 million births against 11.31 million deaths.
  • Fertility (1.01 in 2024) is far below 2.1.
  • Net migration is small and slightly negative, so it does not offset this.
  • Result: natural decrease and an ageing population, the reverse of 1980's worry.
  • Exam move: name the country, date the policies, give figures for births and fertility, then judge.
Mission check

?A country's fertility rate is 0.8. Its government offers cash for each new baby. What is most likely after five years?

China turned pro-natalist because fertility fell far below replacement (1.3 in 2020) and the population aged; deaths now exceed births. Policies can nudge the timing of births, but rarely reverse very low fertility.

Mission 3

Population structure

  • Population structure: how a population is divided by age and sex.
  • A population pyramid shows it: % in each 5-year age group, males left, females right.
  • Two things shape it: natural increase (births and deaths) and net migration (arrivals minus departures).
Mission 3

Natural increase shapes the pyramid

  • High births give a wide base. Niger, 2024: fertility 5.94; 46.6% under 15.
  • Low births and long lives give a narrow base and a wide top. Japan, 2024: fertility 1.22; life expectancy 84.9; 29.8% aged 65+.
  • A youthful population: a large share of children. An ageing population: a rising share of older people.
Mission 3

Net migration shapes the pyramid

  • Qatar, 2024: net migration +46,105 people in one year.
  • Most migrants are working-age men: men aged 20–49 are 52.7% of all residents; women aged 20–49 are 16.4% (our calculation).
  • Result: about 248 males per 100 females (our calculation), and a pyramid with a huge bulge of working-age men.
  • Emigration does the opposite: the origin loses young adults, so its pyramid narrows in those ages.
Quick check
?A pyramid shows a very wide bar for men aged 25–40 but not for women of the same age. What most likely caused this?
Mission 3

The dependency ratio

  • Dependency ratio = (people aged 0–14 + 65+) ÷ people aged 15–64 × 100.
  • It estimates how many dependants each 100 people of working age support.
  • Niger, 2024: (46.6 + 2.6) ÷ 50.8 × 100 ≈ 97 (our calculation).
  • Japan, 2024: (11.4 + 29.8) ÷ 58.8 × 100 ≈ 70 (our calculation).
  • Limitation: many 15–19-year-olds are still in school, and many over-65s work.
Mission 3

Youthful structures: causes and impacts

  • Causes: high fertility (5.94 in Niger); falling infant deaths, so more children survive.
  • Impacts now: huge demand for schools, health care and food; few workers per child.
  • Impacts later: a big wave of young adults needing jobs and homes.
  • Evaluation: a burden today, but a potential workforce tomorrow if they are healthy and skilled.
Mission 3

Ageing structures: causes and impacts

  • Causes: low fertility (1.22 in Japan) and long lives (84.9 years).
  • Impacts: rising costs of pensions, health and long-term care; a shrinking workforce; empty rural homes and schools.
  • WHO: "All countries face major challenges to ensure that their health and social systems are ready".
  • Evaluation: older people also work, volunteer, care and spend; ageing reflects success in health.
Quick check
?Which pair best explains why Japan's population is ageing?
Mission 3

When fertility falls: a window

  • As fertility falls, there are fewer children for each worker; the working-age share rises.
  • UNFPA calls this a potential demographic dividend: "the economic growth potential that can result from shifts in a population's age structure".
  • More workers per dependant can mean more saving, more women in paid work, and more spending on each child's schooling.
  • It is a window: it "gradually closes as the working generation ages".
Mission 3

Example: Bangladesh

  • Fertility: 6.82 (1972) → 2.14 (2024).
  • Working-age share (15–64): 51.8% (1980) → 65.5% (2024) (our calculation).
  • Dependency ratio: about 93 in 1980 (our calculation). Bangladesh is now lower-middle income.
  • GDP per person (constant 2015 US$): $405 (1980) → $1,941 (2024), almost 5 times (our calculation).
  • Other changes helped too, so this shows a link, not proof.
Quick check
?Bangladesh, 2024: 28.0% aged 0–14, 6.5% aged 65+, 65.5% aged 15–64. What is its dependency ratio?
Mission 3

Not automatic: evaluation

  • UNFPA: "Dividends will be constrained without simultaneous investments in decent job creation".
  • Young people "need the chance to gain the education and experience to succeed".
  • There is "a real possibility of countries missing their chance at a dividend".
  • Later, the large working generation retires, and the structure ages.
Mission check

?Which country is most likely to gain economically from its age structure over the next 20 years?

Yes, for a while. Falling fertility raises the working-age share, which can boost growth if there are jobs, health and education. Then the window closes as the population ages.

Mission 4

Moving across borders

  • International migration: moving away from where you usually live "and across an international border".
  • Emigration: leaving a country (seen from the country left behind).
  • Immigration: arriving in a new country to live (seen from the country of arrival).
Mission 4

Who is who?

  • Economic migrant: moves mainly for work or higher pay. This is voluntary: the person chooses to go.
  • Refugee: outside their own country "owing to well-founded fear of being persecuted" for their race, religion, nationality, social group or political opinion. This is forced migration.
  • Asylum seeker: has asked another country for protection but the claim is not yet decided; "every recognized refugee is initially an asylum seeker".
  • About 9 million people were asylum seekers awaiting a decision at the end of 2025.
Quick check
?Amira leaves her country because she is threatened with prison for her political views. She is granted protection in another country. What is she?
Mission 4

Push and pull

  • Push factors drive people away: few jobs, low wages, poor schools or healthcare, conflict, persecution, natural disasters.
  • Pull factors draw people towards a place: jobs, higher wages, safety, family already there, education, healthcare.
Mission 4

Example: Indians working abroad

  • In 2024 the UK was home to about 1.04 million people born in India.
  • Indian migrants abroad send money home (remittances): about $0.43 billion in 1975, about $150.7 billion in 2025. That is roughly 350 times as much in current dollars, not adjusted for inflation (our calculation).
  • Pull: jobs and higher pay abroad. Push: fewer well-paid jobs at home. Family networks make the move easier.
Quick check
?Which of these is a pull factor?
Mission 4

Impacts: migrants, origin, destination

  • Migrants: higher income and new skills, but may face separation from family, poor housing or exploitation.
  • Origin country: gains remittances (India: about $150.7 billion in 2025); loses young, skilled workers (brain drain).
  • Destination: fills labour shortages. About 190,000 of 1.35 million NHS staff in England reported a non-British nationality (March 2021). Population growth also adds demand for housing and services.
Quick check
?Many young doctors leave a country to work abroad. Which is the most likely negative impact on the origin country?
Mission 4

Managing international migration

  • Points-based visas (UK, from 2021): entry depends on skills, salary and a job offer. The higher salary threshold announced for 2024 was part of a plan to cut net migration.
  • International agreements: the EU–Turkey statement (2016) returned new irregular arrivals on Greek islands to Turkey and, for each Syrian returned, resettled another Syrian from Turkey to the EU, with extra EU funding of €3 billion.
  • Evaluate: who gains (employers, migrants, origin, destination), how much it costs, and whether it protects people with a right to asylum.
Quick check
?A country wants more nurses but fewer low-paid arrivals. Which strategy fits best?
Mission check

?Maria, a nurse, leaves the Philippines for a hospital job in the UK at higher pay. She sends money home every month. Which statement is correct?

Economic migrants move by choice, pulled by jobs and pay; refugees are forced out by persecution. Both change their origin and destination countries.

Mission 5

The example: the Rohingya, 2017

  • Origin: northern Rakhine State, Myanmar. Destination: Cox's Bazar District, Bangladesh.
  • This is forced international migration: people were compelled to leave, and crossed a border.
  • People who flee persecution across a border are refugees.
Mission 5

Push factors: long-term causes

  • A UN fact-finding mission found: "Most Rohingya have become de facto stateless, arbitrarily deprived of nationality".
  • Their travel between villages and towns "has long been restricted", with serious consequences for the rights "to food, health and education".
  • These long-term causes left the community vulnerable.
Mission 5

Push factors: the trigger

  • On 25 August 2017, an armed group (ARSA) attacked a military base and up to 30 security posts.
  • Security forces responded with what the authorities called "clearance operations".
  • The UN mission found the response "immediate, brutal and grossly disproportionate".
  • By mid-August 2018, nearly 725,000 Rohingya had fled to Bangladesh.
Mission 5

Pull factors

  • In forced migration, push factors dominate; the pull is safety close at hand.
  • Bangladesh shares a border with northern Rakhine, so it was the nearest place of safety.
  • Bangladesh "generously hosts" the refugees, with UN agencies and partners providing shelter, food and services.
Quick check
?Which of these is a pull factor for the Rohingya's move to Bangladesh?
Mission 5

Impacts on the migrants

  • On 31 October 2026, Bangladesh hosted 1,208,018 Rohingya refugees.
  • Most live in "33 highly congested camps in Cox's Bazar District".
  • They depend on aid: the prioritised 2025 response plan required US$455.6 million for priority activities.
  • Safety from violence, but crowded camps, few jobs and an uncertain future.
Mission 5

Impacts on the origin (Myanmar)

  • At least 392 villages (40% of all settlements in northern Rakhine) were partly or totally destroyed.
  • "Bulldozers flattened burned, damaged and even surviving structures and vegetation".
  • In government rebuilding plans, "almost all houses to be constructed for displaced communities are designated for non-Rohingya".
  • Communities, homes and farmland were lost, making return harder.
Mission 5

Impacts on the destination (Cox's Bazar)

  • Environment: forest reserve around the camps was cleared, according to the Forest Department as reported by UNDP.
  • Economy: wages for farm and other unskilled work fell, and UNDP estimated that poverty rose in Teknaf and Ukhiya as a result.
  • Not everyone lost: large farmers who hire labourers, traders, and hotels, restaurants and transport firms gained from extra demand.
Quick check
?Which impact was felt in the origin area, not the destination?
Mission 5

Managing the migration

  • Bangladesh and the UN run the response through a Joint Response Plan. The 2025–26 plan, launched on 24 March 2025, addresses "Rohingya refugee and host community needs".
  • Bhasan Char: since 2021, "some 35,000 refugees relocated" to this island in Noakhali district. On 31 October 2026, 33,043 lived there.
  • Most refugees (1,174,975) remain in Cox's Bazar.
Mission 5

Return and sustainable management

  • The UN mission said returns must be "safe, voluntary and dignified", and that "in the current circumstances, such returns are not possible" (2018).
  • Sustainable management meets people's needs now without harming the future: support for the host community, limits on forest loss, education and skills for refugees.
  • Exam move: name origin and destination, give dated figures, and judge how far the management is sustainable.
Quick check
?Which response is the most sustainable?
Mission check

?A student writes: "The Rohingya moved to Bangladesh for better jobs, and Myanmar now looks after them." Which correction is best?

A political push: long-term statelessness, then "clearance operations" that the UN found "immediate, brutal and grossly disproportionate". The pull was safety across the nearest border. Bangladesh, with UN agencies, hosts 1,208,018 refugees (31 Oct 2026), mostly in 33 camps in Cox's Bazar.

Mission 6

Urbanisation

  • Urbanisation: a rising share of a country's people living in towns and cities.
  • Urban growth: a rising number of urban people.
  • Cities grow in three ways:
    • natural increase: more births than deaths;
    • migration into the city;
    • reclassification: villages and towns grow big enough to be counted as part of a city.
Mission 6

The world since 1950

  • 1950: 20% of the world's 2.5 billion people lived in cities.
  • 2025: 45% of 8.2 billion lived in cities and 36% in towns.
  • Using each country's own definition of "urban", the world's urban share rose from 37.4% in 1974 to 57.6% in 2024.
Mission 6

Contrasting trends, 1974 to 2024

Urban share of the population, by income group:

  • High income: 69.2% → 80.1%
  • Upper-middle income: 28.8% → 67.8%
  • Lower-middle income: 22.9% → 41.6%
  • Low income: 18.6% → 34.7%
  • HICs are mostly urban already; LICs are still mostly rural but urbanising.
Mission 6

Different times, different speeds

  • High-income countries (HICs) urbanised first, with industrialisation. On the UN's new city definition, their city populations passed their rural populations by 1957.
  • LICs started later: on the UN definition they reached urban–rural parity only in 2017. (On national definitions, they are still mostly rural.)
  • Urban population growth in 2024:
    • low income 3.99% a year · lower-middle 2.27% · upper-middle 0.90% · high income 0.84%.
  • Fast growth comes from high birth rates and migration; slow growth where most people already live in cities.
Quick check
?From 1974 to 2024, the urban share rose by 10.9 percentage points in high-income countries but by 39.0 in upper-middle-income countries. Why the difference?
Mission 6

Why people move to cities

  • Push (rural): poverty, few jobs, mechanised farming, poor schools and clinics.
  • Pull (city): jobs, higher wages, education, health care, bright lights.
  • Group the factors:
    • economic: jobs, wages, failing farms;
    • social: schools, hospitals, joining family;
    • environmental: drought, floods, soil erosion;
    • political: conflict, land disputes, government policies.
  • Natural increase: young people are the most likely to move to cities, so cities gain births as well as migrants.
Mission 6

Shenzhen

  • Established in March 1979; China's first special economic zone from August 1980.
  • 17.98 million permanent residents at the end of 2024.
  • Its government calls it "a young immigrant city": average age 32.5 at the seventh national census.
Mission 6

China: what drove city growth?

  • China's urban share (national definition): 20.6% in 1982 → 63.8% in 2020.
  • Of China's urban growth between the 2010 and 2020 censuses:
    • 56% rural-to-urban migration
    • 26% administrative changes (reclassification)
    • 18% natural increase.
  • Worldwide, natural increase and reclassification drive most city growth; migration matters most in Eastern and South-Eastern Asia and sub-Saharan Africa.
Quick check
?Use the figures earlier in this mission. What share of China's urban growth in 2010–2020 did not come from rural-to-urban migration?
Mission check

?Between 1975 and 2025, Ghana's total population grew 3.5 times, but its city population grew 13.5 times, from just under 2 million to 27 million. Where did most of the extra city dwellers come from?

When cities grow much faster than the whole country, people must be moving in from rural areas and towns. In China, 56% of urban growth in 2010–2020 came from rural-to-urban migration; Shenzhen calls itself "a young immigrant city".

  • 7.2.2 opportunities and challenges of rapid urban growth (inequality, services, housing, jobs, transport, waste, unplanned settlements)
Mission 7

Mumbai: a fast-growing city

  • Mumbai is on India's west coast, on the Arabian Sea.
  • Census 2011: 12,442,373 people in Greater Mumbai.
  • The UN, counting the whole built-up city, gives 15.95 million in 2000 and 20.20 million in 2025: a rise of about 4.25 million in 25 years (our calculation).
  • In 2025 it was the most densely populated city of a million or more in the world: about 27,000 people per km².
  • India is a lower-middle-income country.
Mission 7

Why people move to Mumbai

  • Cities grow by natural increase (more births than deaths) and migration.
  • Push from rural areas: low farm incomes, few jobs, drought, poor services.
  • Pull to the city: jobs, wages, schools and hospitals.
  • Since the 1960s, M/East ward "has given shelter to migrants from different parts of India, as well as those who have been 'resettled' from slums in the inner recesses of the city".
Mission 7

Opportunities of city life

  • Economic development: India's central bank has had its Central Office in Mumbai since 1937.
  • Global business: in 2024 GaWC ranked Mumbai an Alpha world city, a major hub for finance and business services.
  • Services: more schools, colleges and hospitals than rural areas.
  • Culture, leisure and consumption: cinemas, sport, shops and restaurants.
  • Housing: more choice, though much of it is informal (next screens).
Mission 7

Slums: unplanned housing

  • A slum is crowded housing that lacks basic services such as water, toilets and drains; much of it is built without permission.
  • Census 2011: 6,534,460 of Greater Mumbai's 12,442,373 people lived in slums: 52.5% (our calculation).
  • Praja Foundation, citing the same census, gives 41.84%.
  • Cambridge calls them unplanned (informal) settlements.
Mission 7

Where the slums are

  • Slums grow on land others do not want or cannot use: beside railways, on marshy or flood-prone ground, next to landfill, at the city edge.
  • D ward: 34,699 of 346,866 people in slums (10.0%).
  • M/East ward: 685,994 of 807,720 (84.9%).
  • M/East contains "Asia's second largest and oldest open land-fill".
  • Formal housing dominates where land is most valuable.
Quick check
?Two sources, both using the 2011 census, give Mumbai's slum share as 52.5% and 41.84%. What is the best explanation?
Mission 7

Life in M/East

Praja Foundation, citing a 2017 study by the charity Apnalaya:

  • The landfill still receives about 4,500 tonnes of garbage a day.
  • Infant mortality: 66 per 1,000 live births, against a national average of 41.
  • Average age at death: 39.
  • Air and groundwater are contaminated; basic amenities "are conspicuous by their absence".
  • Average family income in its slum clusters: Rs 7,802 a month.
Quick check
?Which M/East figure is the clearest sign of poor health and living conditions?
Mission 7

Informal work

  • Formal jobs: contract, regular pay, taxes paid, legal protection.
  • Informal jobs: no contract, sick pay or pension. Examples: street vending, waste picking, domestic work, small workshops, building-site day labour.
  • In 2024, informal jobs were 81.5% of all jobs in lower-middle-income countries such as India, against 12.9% in high-income countries.
  • Causes: migrants arrive faster than formal jobs are created; many lack qualifications; informal work is easy to start.
  • In M/East, "Every second male and six out of seven females are unemployed".
Quick check
?Which worker is in the informal sector?
Mission 7

Challenges of rapid growth

  • Housing: about half the city lived in slums at the 2011 census.
  • Water, sanitation and energy: in M/East, families won legal water and electricity connections only after organising; many homes share or lack toilets.
  • Waste and pollution: about 4,500 tonnes a day to M/East's open landfill; air and groundwater contaminated.
  • Services and transport: schools, clinics, roads and trains struggle to keep up with demand.
  • Jobs: high unemployment and informal work (jobs with no contract, sick pay or pension).
Mission 7

One city, two worlds

  • Wealth: an Alpha world city and India's central bank.
  • Poverty: M/East ranks bottom of Mumbai's 24 wards for human development (Mumbai Human Development Report 2009, cited by Praja).
  • Reasons for the gap in quality of life:
    • land values: the rich live where land is valuable; the poor where it is cheap;
    • history: M/East absorbed migrants and people "resettled" from inner-city slums;
    • access to formal jobs, water, schools and health care.
Mission check

?In a made-up city, Ward A sits by the port and business district, where land is expensive. Ward B is at the edge, beside the city's landfill; people cleared from central slums were resettled there, and new migrants keep arriving. Which ward will have the higher slum share, and why?

Land values and history explain the gap: D ward 10.0% in slums, M/East 84.9%. Cheap marginal land beside a landfill, resettlement from inner-city slums and new migrants concentrated slums in M/East.

Mission 8: Detroit had 1,849,568 people in 1950 and 639,111 in 2020. What emptied the Motor City?

Make a guess: Detroit had 1,849,568 people in 1950 and 639,111 in 2020. What emptied the Motor City?

Specification: what this mission covers

Mission 8

The Motor City empties

  • Detroit, USA, the home of the American car industry, had 1,849,568 people in 1950 and 639,111 in 2020.
  • Historian Thomas Sugrue told the Associated Press that Detroit lost 140,000 jobs between 1947 and 1963.
  • Factories closed or moved out; people who could, often the better-off, moved to the suburbs and beyond.
  • Jobs left the city, and people followed.
Mission 8

Four stages of urban change

  • Urbanisation: people move into the city; it grows.
  • Suburbanisation: people move from the inner city to the suburbs at its edge.
  • Counter-urbanisation: people move beyond the city to small towns and villages.
  • Re-urbanisation: people move back into the inner city.
  • As suburbs spread outwards, the city's built-up area grows: urban sprawl.
Mission 8

London's population story

Census counts and estimates for London:

  • Urbanisation: 1.1 million (1801) → 8.6 million (1939).
  • Inner and outer London: inner London fell from 4.90 million (1901) to 2.43 million (1981), while outer London grew from 1.61 million (1901) to 4.52 million (1951).
  • Decline and counter-urbanisation: Greater London fell from 8.62 million (1939) to 6.61 million (1981): down 23% (our calculation).
  • Re-urbanisation: back up to 8.80 million (2021); inner London grew 40% from 1981 (our calculation).
Quick check
?Between 1901 and 1951, inner London's population fell while outer London's nearly tripled. Which process does this show?
Mission 8

Why people moved out, and back

  • Suburbanisation: trams, railways and then cars; cheaper land; space and gardens; clearance of crowded inner-city housing.
  • Counter-urbanisation: quieter, cleaner places; retirement; better roads and remote working let people commute or work from home.
  • Re-urbanisation: regeneration of old docks and factories; service jobs in the centre; students and young professionals; international migrants.
Mission 8

Urban sprawl and the rural–urban fringe

  • Worldwide, built-up land per person rose from 43 m² to 63 m² between 1975 and 2025: cities spread faster than they grew in people.
  • About 60% of land turned into urban space since 1970 was productive farmland.
  • Impacts on the fringe: loss of farmland and wildlife habitat; more traffic and commuting; retail parks, business parks and new estates; rising land prices; noise and pollution.
  • Some gains: new homes, jobs and services for people who live there.
Quick check
?A city's new housing estates spread onto surrounding farmland. Which impact on the rural–urban fringe is most likely?
Mission check

?Greater London lost about 2 million residents between 1939 and 1981, then gained about 2.2 million by 2021. What best explains the loss?

In Detroit, as in London, jobs left the city (deindustrialisation) and people followed them outwards (suburbanisation and beyond). Detroit lost about 65% of its 1950 population by 2020; London lost about 23% by 1981, then grew again as it re-urbanised.

Mission 9

Mission 9: Between 42% and 52% of Mumbai's people lived in slums in 2011. Who should decide how the city fixes that?

Make a guess: Between 42% and 52% of Mumbai's people lived in slums in 2011. Who should decide how the city fixes that?

Specification: what this mission covers

Cambridge IGCSE Geography (0460)

  • 7.3.1 evaluate strategies and techniques to manage urban growth, including sustainable ones
Mission 9

Mumbai: a megacity under pressure

  • About 20.2 million people in 2025.
  • One of the densest cities on Earth: about 27,000 people per km².
  • About 42% to 52% of residents lived in slums in 2011, depending on which census table is used.
  • India's financial capital: home of the Reserve Bank of India since 1937.
Mission 9

Top-down and bottom-up

  • Top-down: governments or big organisations plan and pay; large and fast, but local people may have little say.
  • Bottom-up: residents and community groups decide and act; cheap and trusted, but small.
  • Mumbai uses both.
Mission 9

Top-down 1: transport

  • Mumbai Urban Transport Project: World Bank loan of US$542 million (2002–2011) for "an efficient and sustainable urban transport system".
  • A later phase added 864 suburban rail cars, taking the fleet to about 3,124.
  • Metro Line 3: 33.5 km, "fully underground", Colaba–Bandra–SEEPZ.
  • For: faster journeys to jobs; electric trains instead of cars.
  • Against: people affected by the project had to be resettled; huge cost.
Mission 9

Top-down 2: slum rehabilitation

  • The Maharashtra state government set up the Slum Rehabilitation Authority (SRA) in December 1995.
  • The deal: developers build free flats for slum dwellers, and in return may build extra floor space (incentive FSI) to sell on the open market.
  • Residents who move out during building can receive rent.
  • For: new, permanent homes at no cost to residents or the state.
  • Against: depends on developer profit; the SRA can take "punitive action on non-participating slum-dwellers".
Mission 9

Bottom-up: community action

  • SPARC began in 1984 in Byculla, Mumbai; it works in an alliance with the National Slum Dwellers Federation (NSDF) and Mahila Milan (women's groups).
  • Women's savings groups manage loans and help families get identity documents.
  • The alliance has built "many hundreds of community toilets" with city governments.
  • In M/East, citizen-led work with the charity Apnalaya helped families get "legal water connections, electricity connections, cooking gas".
  • For: fits local needs; residents stay in place. Against: slow; still needs government land and permission.
Quick check
?Which Mumbai project is bottom-up?

Mission 9

Is it sustainable?

  • Sustainable urban living: meets needs now (homes, jobs, clean air) without harming the future.
  • Metro and rail: + low-carbon transport; − cost and resettlement.
  • SRA flats: + permanent homes; − may move people from their work and community.
  • Community toilets: + health and dignity; − small scale.
  • Most sustainable: upgrading in place, as the UN advises, linked to good public transport.
Quick check
?Which strategy best meets social, economic and environmental goals at once?
Mission check

?City Z's government, without consulting residents, plans to clear an informal settlement near the city centre and rehouse everyone in free flats 25 km away, beside a new metro station. Which is the strongest criticism of this top-down plan?

Mumbai combines top-down projects (railways, metro, SRA flats) with bottom-up action (SPARC, Mahila Milan, Apnalaya). Top-down gives scale; bottom-up gives residents a say in where and how they live.

Mission 10: Mumbai keeps drawing people in from across India. Who really gains from its growth?

Cambridge IGCSE Geography (0460)

  • 7.3.2 strategies and techniques to manage growth, including sustainable
Mission 10

Where is Mumbai?

  • Site: on India's west coast, on the Arabian Sea; built on a group of former islands, with a sheltered natural harbour.
  • Situation: capital of Maharashtra state; India's main gateway to the Arabian Sea and the Gulf.
  • India is lower-middle income.
  • India is a middle-income country (MIC).
Mission 10

Why Mumbai matters

  • Global: a world city at the Alpha level, linked to firms worldwide (GaWC, 2024). In the 19th century it became "a global trading centre".
  • National: home of India's central bank; the Reserve Bank's central office "permanently moved to Mumbai in 1937".
  • Regional: the main centre for jobs, ports, trade and services in Maharashtra and the wider city-region.
Mission 10

How big, and how fast?

  • UN, same boundary method each year:
    • 2000: 16.0 million · 2025: 20.2 million · 2050 (projected): 23.1 million.
    • 12th-largest city in the world in 2025; growth 0.94% a year (2000–25).
  • Census 2011, Greater Mumbai's municipal area: 12,442,373 people.
  • Different boundaries, different totals: always name the source.
  • It is the world's densest city of a million or more: 27,000 people per km².
Mission 10

Why it grows

  • Natural increase: births exceed deaths in a young population.
  • Rural–urban migration from across India:
    • Push: low farm incomes, few jobs, poor services in rural areas.
    • Pull: jobs, wages, schools, hospitals in the city.
  • Since the 1960s M/East ward "has given shelter to migrants from different parts of India".
Mission 10

A clue in the census

  • Greater Mumbai, 2011: 6,715,931 males and 5,726,442 females.
  • That is about 853 females per 1,000 males (our calculation).
  • Many migrants are young men who come alone to work, sending money home.
Quick check
?A young man leaves his village in Bihar because farm work pays too little, and takes a job on a Mumbai building site. Which factors are at work?
Mission 10

Opportunities

  • Economic: jobs in finance, trade, ports, film, manufacturing and services; urban industry stimulates growth across Maharashtra.
  • Social: more schools and hospitals than in most rural areas. In 2011, about 90% of Greater Mumbai's people aged 7+ could read and write (calculated from census).
  • Resources: piped water and electricity reach far more homes than in villages.
  • For migrants, wages sent home help rural families.
Mission 10

Challenge: housing

  • Greater Mumbai, Census 2011 (municipal tables): 6,534,460 of 12,442,373 people lived in slums: 52.5% (our calculation).
  • By the census definitions used by Praja Foundation the figure is 5,206,473, or 41.84%.
  • Shares differ hugely by ward: D ward 10.0%; M/East 84.9% (our calculation).
  • Slums lack secure tenure, space, toilets and reliable water.
Quick check
?In MCGM's tables, M/East ward had 685,994 slum residents and Greater Mumbai had 6,534,460. What share of the city's slum residents lived in this one ward?
Mission 10

Challenge: services, jobs, health and waste

  • In M/East ward (studies cited by Praja Foundation, 2018):
    • Waste: the Deonar landfill "still receives around 4500 metric tons of garbage every day".
    • Health: infant mortality 66 per 1,000 births, against India's 41 at the time.
    • Jobs: "Every second male and six out of seven females are unemployed."
    • Water: M/East logged the city's highest number of water-supply complaints (2014–16).
Mission 10

Managing growth: transport

  • Mumbai Urban Transport Project (World Bank finance of US$542 million, loan and credit, 2002–2011): aimed at "an efficient and sustainable urban transport system".
  • Phase 2A added 864 suburban rail cars, taking the fleet to about 3,124.
  • Metro Line 3: a 33.5 km, "fully underground" line, Colaba–Bandra–SEEPZ.
  • Costs: families living beside the tracks had to be resettled; the project paid for a resettlement plan.
Mission 10

Managing growth: housing and services

  • Upgrading slums: the UN calls for slums to be upgraded "by ensuring their access to basic services", "preventing displacement".
  • Bottom-up: in M/East, citizen-led action helped families get "legal water connections, electricity connections, cooking gas".
  • Top-down redevelopment can replace slums with flats, but may move people far from their work.
Quick check
?Which approach best meets needs now without harming the future?
Mission check

?Which evidence best shows that the costs of Mumbai's growth fall unevenly?

Many migrants gain jobs and wages; the city gains as a national and global hub. But wards such as M/East carry the costs: slums, a landfill, high infant mortality and joblessness.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Natural change = births − deaths. Niger: 3.27% a year, doubling in about 21 years. Japan: −0.63%.
  • DTM: Niger late stage 2 (low income); Japan stage 5 (high income).

Mission 2

  • China: anti-natalist (1980) → two children (2016) → three (2021). Fertility had fallen before 1980; now 1.01 (2024).
  • Evidence: brief rise in 2016; UN finds cash effects "weak".

Mission 3

  • Structure is shaped by natural increase (Niger young, Japan old) and net migration (Qatar: 248 males per 100 females).
  • Youthful: schools, health, future jobs. Ageing: pensions, care, fewer workers. A falling birth rate opens a demographic dividend window (Bangladesh), but only with jobs and skills.

Mission 4

  • Migration is caused by push and pull factors; economic migrants choose to move, refugees are forced to flee persecution; asylum seekers are waiting for a decision.
  • Impacts fall on migrants, origin (remittances, brain drain) and destination (workers, demand for services); strategies such as points-based visas and international agreements must be evaluated.

Mission 5

  • Rohingya, Myanmar → Bangladesh, from August 2017: push (statelessness, violence), pull (nearby safety).
  • Impacts: crowded, aid-dependent camps; destroyed villages in Rakhine; forest loss and lower wages, with some winners, in Cox's Bazar. Management: Joint Response Plan, Bhasan Char, and return only when safe.

Mission 6

  • The world's urban share rose from 37% to 58% in 50 years.
  • HICs urbanised early and are now growing slowly; LICs and lower-middle-income countries are urbanising fastest, through migration and natural increase.

Mission 7

  • Mumbai grew fast through migration and natural increase. About half its people lived in slums in 2011, concentrated where land was cheap and marginal, as in M/East.
  • Rapid growth brings opportunities (jobs, services) and challenges (housing, water, waste, pollution, informal work).

Mission 8

  • London shows the whole sequence: urbanisation to 1939, suburbanisation, decline to 1981, then re-urbanisation. Sprawl takes farmland and adds traffic at the rural–urban fringe.

Mission 9

  • Mumbai: about 20 million people; 42–52% in slums (2011, census tables differ); informal work, waste and health gaps.
  • Top-down: World Bank-funded railways, Metro Line 3, SRA flats: big and fast, but displacement and developer-led.
  • Bottom-up: SPARC, NSDF, Mahila Milan, Apnalaya: local and trusted, but small and slow.

Mission 10

  • Mumbai: west-coast port city, India's financial centre, an Alpha world city; 20.2 million (UN, 2025).
  • Growth: natural increase + rural–urban migration. Gains: jobs, services. Costs: slums (52.5% in MCGM's tables), waste, health, unemployment, unevenly spread.
  • Management: suburban rail upgrades and Metro Line 3; slum upgrading.
