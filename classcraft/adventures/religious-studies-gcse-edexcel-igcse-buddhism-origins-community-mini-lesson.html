Mission 1: A prince had everything he could want. Why did four ordinary sights make him walk away from it all?

Make a guess: A prince had everything he could want. Why did four ordinary sights make him walk away from it all?

Mission 1

Who was Siddhattha?

  • Buddhists call him the Buddha: "the awakened one". Before his awakening he was Siddhattha Gotama (Sanskrit: Siddhartha Gautama).
  • Ancestry: born into the Sakya (Shakya) clan, in the kshatriya class of rulers and warriors.
  • His father, Suddhodana, ruled the Sakyas from Kapilavatthu, in the foothills of the Himalayas (the border of modern Nepal and India).
  • His mother was Queen Maya (Mahamaya).
  • Dates: traditionally 6th–5th century BCE; many historians now prefer the 5th–4th century BCE.
  • Significance: Buddhists see the Buddha as a human being, not a god. They believe anyone can follow the path he found.
Mission 1

Before he was born

  • Buddhists believe Siddhattha had lived many lives before. The Jataka stories tell of them: in each he built up qualities such as generosity, patience and wisdom.
  • Ages earlier, as a hermit named Sumedha, he met an earlier Buddha, Dipankara, and vowed to become a Buddha himself (told in the Buddhavamsa).
  • In his last life before birth he lived in the Tusita heaven and, the stories say, chose his time, place and parents.
  • Queen Maya dreamed that a white elephant entered her side. Wise men said she would bear a great son.
Mission 1

The birth in Lumbini

  • Maya set out to give birth at her parents' home. On the way, in the Lumbini grove, she gave birth standing, holding the branch of a sal tree.
  • The stories say the baby took seven steps, a lotus springing up at each, and said this was his last birth.
  • Maya died seven days later. Her sister, Mahapajapati Gotami, raised the boy.
  • He was named Siddhattha: "one who achieves his aim".
  • Lumbini is today a place of pilgrimage. Many Buddhists celebrate the birth at Wesak (Vesak).
Mission 1

The prophecy

  • A sage, Asita, came to see the baby. He smiled, then wept: the child would become a Buddha, but Asita would die before hearing him teach.
  • At the naming, eight wise men (brahmins) examined the baby. Seven said: he will be either a great world ruler or, if he leaves home, a Buddha.
  • The youngest, Kondanna, said he would certainly become a Buddha. (Kondanna later became one of his first five followers.)
  • Suddhodana wanted a king, not a wandering holy man. So he decided to keep all signs of suffering away from his son.

Mission 1

A life of luxury

  • Suddhodana gave his son three palaces, one for each season: cold, hot and rainy.
  • Music, fine clothes, the best food; servants everywhere. Sick and old people were kept out of sight.
  • Siddhattha was trained in the skills of a prince.
  • At about 16 he married his cousin Yasodhara. Their son was Rahula.
  • Significance: he had everything the world can offer. So when he later left, Buddhists see it as proof that wealth and pleasure alone cannot end suffering.
Mission 1

The Four Sights

  • At 29, Siddhattha went out of the palace with his charioteer, Channa. On four trips he saw:
  • 1. an old man: everyone grows old;
  • 2. a sick man: everyone can fall ill;
  • 3. a corpse (a dead body): everyone dies;
  • 4. a holy man (a wandering ascetic), calm and content.
  • Each time he asked Channa: "Will this happen to me?" Channa: yes, to everyone.
  • The first three showed him suffering. The fourth showed a possible way out: leaving home to search for the truth.
Quick check
?Which of the Four Sights gave Siddhattha hope that suffering could be overcome?
Mission 1

The Great Renunciation

  • That night Siddhattha looked at his sleeping wife and baby son, then left the palace.
  • He rode his horse Kanthaka, with Channa, to a river. There he cut off his hair and changed into simple robes.
  • He became a samana: a wandering seeker with no home.
  • Buddhists call this the Great Renunciation ("giving up").
  • Significance: Buddhists see it as the start of the search that led to the Dhamma. Monks and nuns who shave their heads and wear robes follow the same pattern.
Mission 1Diversity

How Buddhists read the stories

  • Theravada Buddhists stress that Siddhattha was a human being who reached Enlightenment by his own effort, after many lives of preparation.
  • Mahayana Buddhists (e.g. the Lotus Sutra) teach that the Buddha was enlightened long ages ago. His life on earth was a skilful means (upaya) to teach beings.
  • Some Buddhists take the wonders (seven steps, white elephant) literally. Others read them as symbols: the lotus for purity, seven steps for his greatness.
  • The oldest texts (e.g. Anguttara Nikaya 3.38) describe the Buddha reflecting on old age, sickness and death, with no story of four outings. Many Buddhists say the meaning matters more than the exact events.
  • All agree on the message: suffering touches everyone, and there is a way out.
Mission 1Worked answer

Belief, source, development

Question: Explain two reasons why the Four Sights are important to Buddhists today.

  • Reason 1: they show that suffering (old age, sickness, death) affects everyone, even a protected prince. Development: so Buddhists try to face suffering honestly rather than hide from it; this leads to the First Noble Truth.
  • Reason 2: the holy man shows a way out: Siddhattha left home to seek the truth. Source: Buddhavamsa XXVI says that, having seen the four signs, he left home and strove for six years. Development: many Buddhists see the monastic life, or regular meditation, as following his example.
Mission check

?A student says: "The Four Sights only mattered because Siddhattha was a protected prince. Ordinary people see old age and illness every day, so the story has nothing to teach them." How would most Buddhists respond?

Old age, sickness and death come to everyone. Siddhattha's response, to search for a way out, is where Buddhism begins.

Mission 2

Learning from teachers

  • After leaving home at 29, Siddhattha became a samana: a wandering seeker.
  • He studied meditation with two famous teachers, Alara Kalama and Uddaka Ramaputta.
  • He quickly reached the deep, calm states of mind they taught.
  • But when he came out of meditation, old age, sickness and death were still there. These states did not end suffering.
  • So he left both teachers to search on his own.
Mission 2

Six years as an ascetic

  • Many seekers in India believed that punishing the body would free the mind. This is asceticism.
  • With five companions, Siddhattha tried the hardest practices: holding his breath, standing for hours, living on almost no food.
  • He grew so thin that, in his own later account, he could feel his spine through his stomach (Majjhima Nikaya 36, paraphrase).
  • He nearly died. He had pushed harder than anyone, and still had not found the answer.
  • His conclusion: extreme self-denial weakens the mind; it does not lead to awakening.
Mission 2

The Middle Way

  • He remembered a day in childhood: sitting under a rose-apple tree, he had slipped into calm, happy meditation, without any pain (Majjhima Nikaya 36).
  • Perhaps calm, clear meditation was the way.
  • He accepted a bowl of milk-rice from a young woman, Sujata, and grew strong again.
  • His five companions thought he had given up, and left him.
  • He had found the Middle Way: neither a life of luxury (the palace) nor harsh self-punishment (the forest).
  • The Middle Way runs through all Buddhist practice, from diet to meditation.

Mission 2

The night under the Bodhi tree

  • At Bodh Gaya (north-east India), at about 35, he sat beneath a fig tree, later called the Bodhi tree ("tree of awakening").
  • He vowed not to get up until he had found the truth.
  • Mara, the tempter (in Buddhism, the power of desire, fear and death), tried to stop him:
  • armies and storms to frighten him; Mara's daughters to tempt him; doubt: "who are you to claim this seat?"
  • Buddhist tradition tells that Siddhattha touched the ground. The earth bore witness to his many lives of good deeds, and Mara fled.
  • Many statues show this moment: the earth-touching gesture (bhumisparsha mudra).
Mission 2

Enlightenment

  • Buddhists believe that through the night, in deep meditation, he came to three knowledges (one in each watch of the night):
  • 1. he remembered his past lives;
  • 2. he saw beings dying and being reborn according to their kamma (karma: intentional actions);
  • 3. he understood the Four Noble Truths and was freed from craving and ignorance.
  • At dawn he was Enlightened: the Buddha, "the awakened one". Buddhists believe he was now free from suffering and would not be reborn.
Quick check
?What did Siddhattha understand in the final watch of the night, which made him the Buddha?
Mission 2

The first sermon and the Sangha

  • At first the Buddha doubted anyone could understand. Buddhist texts say the god Brahma Sahampati begged him to teach (Samyutta Nikaya 6.1).
  • He went to the Deer Park at Sarnath, near Benares (Varanasi), and found his five old companions.
  • He taught them the Middle Way and the Four Noble Truths. This first sermon is called "Setting in motion the Wheel of the Dhamma" (Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta).
  • Kondanna understood first. The five became the first bhikkhus (monks): the start of the Sangha (community).
  • Soon the Buddha sent followers out to teach "for the welfare of many".
  • Later, his foster-mother Mahapajapati became the first bhikkhuni (nun).
  • Significance: the Sangha is one of the Three Jewels and still passes on the Dhamma today.
Mission 2Read the source

The first sermon

Samyutta Nikaya 56.11, Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta (paraphrase):

  • There are two extremes a seeker should not follow: chasing sense pleasures, which is low and unprofitable, and tormenting oneself, which is painful and unprofitable.
  • Avoiding both, the Buddha found the Middle Way. It gives vision and knowledge, and leads to peace, awakening and nibbana.
  • This Middle Way is the Noble Eightfold Path.

Q1 Which two extremes does the sutta reject?

Show answer

Sense pleasure; self-torment.

Q2 Which two stages of Siddhattha's life match those extremes?

Show answer

The palace; six years as an ascetic.

Mission 2

Teaching, death and parinibbana

  • The Buddha taught across northern India for about 45 years, to rulers, farmers, outcastes and women, in the local languages.
  • At about 80, after a meal given by a smith named Cunda at Pava, he fell ill.
  • He travelled on to Kusinara (Kushinagar), where he lay down between two sal trees and asked his monks if they had any final questions.
  • His last words (Mahaparinibbana Sutta, Digha Nikaya 16, paraphrase): all conditioned things decay; strive on with care.
  • He passed into parinibbana (parinirvana): final nibbana at death, with no further rebirth.
  • Mahayana Buddhists remember the parinirvana at Nirvana Day; Theravada Buddhists remember birth, Enlightenment and death together at Wesak.
Mission 2Diversity

Different emphases

  • Theravada: the Buddha was a human being who found the path by his own effort; his example matters most. Followers aim to become arahants (fully freed ones) by the path he taught.
  • Mahayana: the Buddha shows what every being can become, since all have Buddha-nature. Many teach that he was enlightened long before his life on earth (Lotus Sutra) and that his death was a skilful lesson about impermanence.
  • Zen: stresses that he woke up by meditation, not by study; Zen practice centres on sitting meditation.
  • Pure Land: stresses the help of another Buddha, Amitabha, as well as Gotama's example.
  • All honour the Enlightenment as the moment the Dhamma began.
Mission 2Evaluate

"The Enlightenment is the most important event in the Buddha's life."

  • Reasons for: it made Siddhattha a Buddha; without it there would be no Dhamma to teach. Bodh Gaya is the most important pilgrimage place for many Buddhists. Wesak celebrates it.
  • Reasons against: without the Four Sights there would have been no search. Without the first sermon and the Sangha, no one else would have heard the Dhamma.
  • Also against: parinibbana shows the goal reached in full.
  • Another view: Buddhists honour three jewels: the Buddha, the Dhamma and the Sangha. Each event served all three.
  • Judge it by: Which event changed the most for other people? Which do Buddhists remember most in practice? Can one event be separated from the rest of the story?
Mission check

The Buddha found freedom not in the palace and not in starvation, but in a calm, clear middle way.

Mission 3

Mission 3: The Buddha wrote nothing down. So where do Buddhists find his teaching, and whose voice still guides them?

Make a guess: The Buddha wrote nothing down. So where do Buddhists find his teaching, and whose voice still guides them?

Mission 3

Three baskets

  • The Buddha taught by speaking. After his death, his followers met at the First Council at Rajagaha.
  • Tradition says Ananda, his attendant, recited the discourses, and Upali recited the monastic rules.
  • For centuries monks passed the teaching on by memory and chanting.
  • It was written down on palm leaves in Sri Lanka in the 1st century BCE, in Pali.
  • The collection is the Tipitaka (Pali) or Tripitaka (Sanskrit): "three baskets".
  • Sutta Pitaka: the discourses. Vinaya Pitaka: the discipline. Abhidhamma Pitaka: the higher teaching.
  • Theravada Buddhists call it the Pali Canon.
Mission 3

Sutta Pitaka and the Dhammapada

  • Sutta Pitaka: thousands of discourses (suttas) of the Buddha and some of his leading disciples.
  • Five collections, e.g. the long, middle-length and connected discourses.
  • Famous suttas: the first sermon (Four Noble Truths), the Mangala Sutta (blessings), the Metta Sutta (loving kindness).
  • The Dhammapada ("verses on the Dhamma") is in the fifth, "minor", collection: 423 verses in 26 chapters.
  • Short, memorable sayings on the mind, kamma, the path and nibbana.
  • Many Buddhists learn verses by heart; it is often the first text a new Buddhist reads.
Mission 3Read the source

The Dhammapada

"For hatred does not cease by hatred at any time: hatred ceases by love, this is an old rule." (Dhammapada 5, Müller)

"Not to commit any sin, to do good, and to purify one's mind, that is the teaching of (all) the Awakened." (Dhammapada 183, Müller)

Q1 According to verse 183, what are the three parts of all Buddhas' teaching?

Show answer

Avoid wrong, do good, purify the mind.

Q2 How could verse 5 guide a Buddhist making an ethical decision after being hurt?

Show answer

Answer hatred with love, not revenge.

Mission 3

Vinaya and Abhidhamma

  • Vinaya Pitaka: the rules for monks and nuns, with the story of why each rule was made.
  • The core list (Patimokkha) has 227 rules for Theravada monks and 311 for nuns; monks recite it twice a month.
  • It also sets out ordination, the Rains Retreat and how the Sangha settles disputes.
  • Abhidhamma Pitaka: a detailed analysis of the mind: every type of consciousness and mental factor, matter and nibbana.
  • Studied by monks and scholars, especially in Myanmar; it helps meditators understand what they observe.
Quick check
?A new monk wants to know the rule about not eating after midday, and the story of why the Buddha made it. Where should he look?
Mission 3

How Buddhists use the texts

  • Chanting: protective chants (paritta) such as the Mangala and Metta Suttas at puja, blessings and funerals.
  • Meditation: instructions for mindfulness of breathing and the foundations of mindfulness come from the suttas.
  • Guidance: e.g. the Sigalovada Sutta sets out duties between parents and children, teachers and pupils, husbands and wives.
  • Ethical decisions: verses such as Dhammapada 5 and 183 guide choices about anger, honesty and harm.
  • The search for enlightenment: study leads to understanding; understanding is then tested in practice.
  • Monks study and teach the texts; lay people hear them in Dhamma talks.
Mission 3Diversity

How much authority?

  • Theravada: the Pali Tipitaka is the word of the Buddha (Buddhavacana) and the final authority.
  • Mahayana: accepts early teachings too, but also later Mahayana sutras (e.g. the Lotus, Heart and Diamond Sutras), collected in Chinese and Tibetan canons.
  • Zen: speaks of "a special transmission outside the scriptures": direct experience, passed from teacher to student, matters more than words.
  • Test it yourself: in the Kalama Sutta the Buddha tells people not to accept a teaching just because of tradition or scripture, but to test it by experience (paraphrase).
  • The raft: the Buddha compares his teaching to a raft: for crossing the river, not for carrying on your head afterwards (Majjhima Nikaya 22, paraphrase).
  • So even the scriptures are a means, not the goal.
Quick check
?Which Buddhists most often treat the Pali Tipitaka alone as the authoritative word of the Buddha?
Mission 3

Thich Nhat Hanh: his life

  • Thich Nhat Hanh (1926–2022): a Vietnamese Zen monk, ordained as a teenager.
  • During the Vietnam War he refused to take sides. He set up a school that trained young people to rebuild bombed villages, and in 1966 founded the Order of Interbeing.
  • He travelled abroad calling for peace; Martin Luther King Jr nominated him for the Nobel Peace Prize (1967). He was barred from returning home for nearly 40 years.
  • In 1982 he founded Plum Village in France, now a worldwide community of monastics and lay people.
  • He wrote over 100 books, e.g. The Miracle of Mindfulness (1975).
  • He returned to Vietnam and died there, in Hue, in 2022.
Mission 3

Thich Nhat Hanh: his teaching

  • Engaged Buddhism: a term he made popular. Meditation is not an escape: compassion must act on war, poverty and injustice.
  • Interbeing: nothing exists alone. A sheet of paper "contains" the cloud, the rain, the tree and the logger (his own example, paraphrase). This retells dependent arising and anatta in everyday words.
  • Mindfulness in daily life: washing dishes, walking, breathing: each can be meditation.
  • The Five Mindfulness Trainings: the Five Precepts restated for modern life (e.g. mindful use of media and consumption).
  • He taught simply, for lay people and people of other faiths, and in dialogue with Christians.
Mission 3Evaluate

"Thich Nhat Hanh's teaching is still relevant to Buddhists today."

  • Reasons for: Plum Village communities continue across the world. Interbeing speaks to climate change and conflict. His mindfulness and Five Trainings make the path practical for lay people today.
  • Reasons against: some Buddhists think popular mindfulness has become a wellness product, cut off from ethics and nibbana. He represents one lineage; many Theravada and Tibetan Buddhists follow other teachers and texts. After his death, his influence may fade.
  • Judge it by: Relevant to which Buddhists? Do his teachings agree with the Tipitaka? Is popularity the same as relevance?
Mission check

?Lin reads that the Buddha compared his teaching to a raft. Her friend says: "So Buddhists should throw the scriptures away." Which reply fits the view of most Buddhists best?

For Buddhists, the Tipitaka carries the Buddha's voice, and teachers like Thich Nhat Hanh bring it into new lives; both are seen as rafts, a means and not the goal.

Mission 4: Why would Buddhists hold a festival to remember the day their teacher died?

Make a guess: Why would Buddhists hold a festival to remember the day their teacher died?

Mission 4

Why festivals?

  • Festivals remember key events in the Buddha's life and teaching.
  • They renew commitment: to the Three Jewels and to the precepts.
  • They gather the Sangha: monks, nuns and lay people together.
  • They are a time for merit: giving (dana), keeping precepts, meditating.
  • Most follow the lunar calendar, so dates move. Traditions date them differently.
Mission 4

Wesak

  • The most important festival for many Buddhists, at the full moon of the month of Vesakha (usually May).
  • Theravada Buddhists believe all three happened on the same full-moon day in different years.
  • Customs: cleaning and decorating homes and viharas; lanterns and lights (light = enlightenment); bathing the buddharupa; offerings; listening to the Dhamma.
  • Many lay Buddhists keep eight precepts for the day and give dana to monks.
  • The Three Jewels are honoured; some Buddhists release caged birds or animals as an act of compassion, though others criticise this when animals are caught just to be freed.
Mission 4

Parinirvana Day

  • A Mahayana festival, usually on 15 February.
  • It marks the Buddha's death at Kushinagar, aged 80, and his passing into final nirvana (parinirvana): no more rebirth.
  • Your spec calls it Nirvana Day.
  • Customs: readings about the Buddha's last days; meditation; reflection on impermanence; remembering those who have recently died.
  • Passages from the Nirvana Sutra are read; people visit viharas and monasteries; temples may share food and gifts.
  • Why celebrate a death? Buddhists believe the Buddha reached what all beings seek: freedom from dukkha.
Quick check
?On 15 February, a Mahayana group reads about the Buddha's last days, remembers friends who died this year and meditates. Which festival are they keeping?
Mission 4Diversity

Who keeps what?

  • Theravada (Sri Lanka, Thailand, Myanmar, UK viharas): Wesak (birth, enlightenment, death), Vassa (the Rain Retreat), Kathina (robe-cloth giving after it), Uposatha (moon observance days).
  • Mahayana: Parinirvana Day; in Japan, Hanamatsuri (the Buddha's birth) and Obon (welcoming ancestors' spirits).
  • Tibetan: the Buddha's enlightenment and parinirvana in the fourth Tibetan month (Saga Dawa); Lama Tsong Khapa Day (remembering a great Tibetan teacher; especially the Gelug school).
  • Different attitudes: some Buddhists see festivals as vital for merit and community; others, including many Western Buddhists, give them less weight than daily meditation and ethics.
Mission 4Evaluate

"Festivals are not important for Buddhists in Britain today."

  • Reasons for: the Buddha did not command festivals; the path is about training the mind. Many British Buddhists are converts who focus on meditation. Dates and customs vary, so there is no shared calendar.
  • Reasons against: in a minority setting, festivals gather the Sangha and pass the tradition to children. Wesak recalls enlightenment, the goal itself. Parinirvana Day teaches impermanence. Dana and precepts at festivals make merit.
  • Judge it by: Does importance mean "required" or "valuable"? Does it differ between heritage and convert communities? Can retreats do the same job?
Mission check

?A visitor reads a UK vihara's calendar: in May, lanterns, bathing the buddharupa and eight precepts; in February, readings about the Buddha's last days. She says: "So Buddhists celebrate his birthday and mourn his death, like any famous person." Which reply fits Buddhist teaching best?

Buddhist festivals remember the Buddha's life, but they point beyond it: to enlightenment, impermanence and the practice that leads there.

Mission 5

Why go on pilgrimage?

  • A pilgrimage is a journey to a sacred place for a religious purpose.
  • Buddhist pilgrims go to places linked to the Buddha's life, mostly in north India and Nepal.
  • Inspiration: standing where the Buddha stood makes his life and teaching vivid.
  • Gratitude and devotion: offerings, prostrations, chanting.
  • Meditation: many pilgrims meditate at the sites, sometimes for days.
  • Merit: the effort and generosity of the journey are believed to bring good kamma.
  • Pilgrimage is not required: no Buddhist rule says everyone must go.
Mission 5Read the source

Four places to see

In the Mahaparinibbana Sutta (Digha Nikaya 16), near his death, the Buddha names four places that a person of faith should see with reverence (paraphrase):

  • where the Buddha was born (Lumbini, in Nepal);
  • where he became enlightened (Bodh Gaya);
  • where he first taught, setting the wheel of Dhamma in motion (Sarnath);
  • where he passed into parinibbana (Kushinagar).
  • In the same sutta he says he is honoured best not by flowers and music but by those who practise the Dhamma (paraphrase).

Q1 What do the four places have in common?

Show answer

Each marks a turning point in the Buddha's life and the Dhamma.

Q2 How might the second teaching affect how a pilgrim behaves?

Show answer

The visit should lead to practice, not only sightseeing or ritual.

Mission 5

Kapilavastu

  • Kapilavastu was the capital of the Shakya clan, where Prince Siddhartha grew up in his father's palace.
  • Two places claim the ancient city: Tilaurakot in Nepal, with ruins of walls and gateways; and Piprahwa with nearby Ganwaria, in India.
  • Palace site: pilgrims walk among the remains and recall the Buddha's early life of luxury and his leaving home.
  • Stupa complex: at Piprahwa, a stupa where a casket of relics, inscribed as the Buddha's, was found in 1898.
  • Kapilavastu Buddha Mahotsav: a festival held in the Kapilavastu area, with chanting, talks and cultural events.
  • Importance: the Buddha's renunciation began here; it shows the path starts by letting go.
Mission 5

Bodh Gaya

  • In Bihar, India: where Siddhartha sat under a Bodhi tree and became the Buddha (enlightened).
  • The Mahabodhi Temple, a tall tower beside the tree, is a UNESCO World Heritage Site. An earlier shrine is linked to the emperor Ashoka (3rd century BCE).
  • The present Bodhi tree is believed to descend from the original.
  • Pilgrims walk clockwise round the temple, make prostrations, offer flowers, lamps and robes, chant and meditate under the tree.
  • Monasteries from many Buddhist countries stand nearby: a meeting point for every tradition.
  • The Great Buddha Statue: a seated image about 25 metres high, unveiled in 1989.
  • The Sujata temple and stupa recall the young woman Sujata, who offered Siddhartha milk-rice, ending his extreme fasting: the Middle Way.

Mission 5

Sarnath

  • Near Varanasi: the Deer Park (Isipatana), where the Buddha gave his first sermon to five ascetics, his former companions.
  • He taught the Four Noble Truths and the Middle Way: "setting the wheel of Dhamma in motion" (Dhammacakkappavattana).
  • The first monks were ordained here: the Sangha began.
  • Pilgrims chant the first sermon, meditate and walk round the stupas.
  • Dhameka Stupa: a great brick and stone stupa (rebuilt around 500 CE) marking where the sermon is believed to have been given.
  • Ashoka Pillar: set up by Ashoka in the 3rd century BCE; its four-lion capital, now in the Sarnath museum, is India's national emblem.
  • Chaukhandi Stupa: marks where the Buddha is believed to have met the five ascetics; an eight-sided tower was added on top in 1588.
Quick check
?Which monument at Sarnath is believed to mark the place where the Buddha gave his first sermon?
Mission 5Diversity

Different attitudes

  • Theravada pilgrims from Sri Lanka, Thailand and Myanmar often travel in groups led by monks, with chanting and dana.
  • Tibetan pilgrims may circle sites many times or make full-length prostrations along the route.
  • Japanese and other Mahayana pilgrims also visit their own sacred mountains and temples at home.
  • Some Buddhists see pilgrimage as very valuable: merit, inspiration, a once-in-a-lifetime act of devotion.
  • Others see it as optional or a distraction: the Buddha said he is honoured by practice, and Zen teachers warn against seeking the Buddha outside one's own mind.
  • Modern concerns: cost, crowds and commercialisation at busy sites.
Mission 5Evaluate

"A Buddhist does not need to go on pilgrimage."

  • Reasons for: the Buddha said practising the Dhamma honours him most (Digha Nikaya 16). Enlightenment is found in the mind, not a place. Many Buddhists cannot afford to travel. Retreats offer intensive practice nearer home.
  • Reasons against: the Buddha himself recommended the four places. Pilgrimage builds devotion, gratitude and merit. Meditating at Bodh Gaya can deepen practice; it unites Buddhists of every tradition.
  • Judge it by: Is "need" the same as "value"? What did the Buddha recommend and what did he require? Can a retreat at home do the same work?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Siddhattha Gotama was a Sakya prince, son of Suddhodana and Maya, born at Lumbini; Kondanna foretold he would become a Buddha.
  • He grew up in luxury, married Yasodhara and had a son, Rahula; at 29 the Four Sights (old age, sickness, death, a holy man) led to the Great Renunciation.

Mission 2

  • After his teachers and six years of harsh asceticism, Siddhattha chose the Middle Way and meditated under the Bodhi tree at Bodh Gaya.
  • Buddhists believe he defeated Mara, understood the Four Noble Truths and became the Buddha; at Sarnath he taught the first sermon (SN 56.11), and the five companions became the first Sangha.
  • After 45 years of teaching he died at Kusinara and passed into parinibbana.

Mission 3

  • The Tipitaka ("three baskets"): Sutta (discourses, including the Dhammapada), Vinaya (monastic discipline), Abhidhamma (analysis of mind).
  • Used in chanting, meditation, ethical guidance and study; Theravada treats it as the Buddha's word, Mahayana adds later sutras, Zen stresses direct experience.
  • Thich Nhat Hanh (1926–2022): engaged Buddhism, interbeing, mindfulness in daily life; his relevance today is debated.

Mission 4

  • Wesak (May full moon) recalls the Buddha's birth and, for Theravada Buddhists, his enlightenment and death; Parinirvana Day (Mahayana, February) marks his final nirvana.

Mission 5

  • The Buddha named four places to visit with reverence: Lumbini (birth), Bodh Gaya (enlightenment), Sarnath (first sermon), Kushinagar (parinibbana).
  • Pilgrims meditate, make offerings and prostrations; pilgrimage is valued but not required.
  • Kapilavastu (palace and stupas), Bodh Gaya (Mahabodhi, Great Buddha, Sujata) and Sarnath (Dhameka, Ashoka Pillar, Chaukhandi) each have key monuments.
