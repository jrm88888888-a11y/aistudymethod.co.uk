Mission 1

What a wave carries

  • A progressive (travelling) wave transfers energy from place to place.
  • It does not transfer matter. Each particle oscillates about a fixed position.
Mission 1

Transverse and longitudinal

  • Transverse: oscillations perpendicular to the direction of energy transfer. Waves on a string; all EM waves.
  • Longitudinal: oscillations parallel to the direction of energy transfer. Sound; a slinky pushed along its length.
  • Longitudinal waves have compressions (particles close, pressure higher) and rarefactions (spread out, pressure lower).
Mission 1

Describing a wave

  • Displacement: distance and direction of a particle from its rest position.
  • Amplitude A: maximum displacement.
  • Wavelength λ: distance between neighbouring points that move in step, e.g. crest to crest.
  • Period T: time for one complete oscillation. Frequency f: oscillations per second, in Hz.
  • f = 1/T.
Mission 1

The wave equation

  • In one period the wave moves on one wavelength: v = λ/T, so v = fλ.
  • Written c = fλ.
  • For sound in air and waves on a string, the medium sets v and the source sets f.
  • Change f in the same medium: λ changes, v stays the same.
  • Example: sound at 340 m s⁻¹, f = 680 Hz → λ = 340/680 = 0.50 m.
Mission 1

Two graphs, two meanings

  • Displacement–position graph: a snapshot of the whole wave. Read λ and A.
  • Displacement–time graph: one particle over time. Read T and A.
  • Same shape, different axis: always check the x-axis label.
  • Particle direction: for a wave moving right, a point copies what the point just to its left did a moment ago.
Quick check
?A transverse wave travels along a stretched string. Adjacent crests are 1.2 m apart. A graph of displacement y against time t for one point on the string is a sine curve: y = 0 at t = 0, y is a maximum at t = 5 ms, y = 0 at t = 10 ms, y is a minimum at t = 15 ms and y = 0 at t = 20 ms, after which the pattern repeats. What is the wave speed?
Mission check

?A vibrator sends waves along a stretched string at constant tension. At 25 Hz the wavelength is 0.80 m. The frequency is raised to 100 Hz. What is the new wavelength, and how long does a wave now take to travel 4.0 m along the string?

The medium sets the speed. A higher note has a higher frequency and a shorter wavelength, but the same speed: it arrives no sooner.

Mission 2

What an EM wave is

  • An electromagnetic (EM) wave is an oscillating electric field and an oscillating magnetic field, travelling together.
  • The two fields are perpendicular to each other and to the direction of travel: EM waves are transverse.
  • Energy travels along the direction of travel; the fields oscillate across it.
  • EM waves need no medium: they cross a vacuum.
  • In a vacuum all EM waves travel at the same speed, c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹, whatever their wavelength.
  • Examples: the radio waves from an FM radio station, visible light, the X-rays used in medical imaging.
Quick check
?Predict first. The diagram shows the regions of the electromagnetic spectrum on a logarithmic scale of wavelength λ (wavelength increases to the right). A wave has a wavelength of 2 × 10⁻⁵ m. In which region of the spectrum shown does it lie?
Mission 2

The spectrum, in order

  • Seven regions, by decreasing wavelength: radio, microwave, infrared, visible, ultraviolet, X-ray, gamma.
  • Same order: increasing frequency (f = c/λ) and increasing photon energy.
  • Visible light, longest to shortest: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, violet.
  • Visible runs from about 700 nm (red) to 400 nm (violet).
  • The regions differ only in wavelength (and so frequency). Same nature, same c.
  • Boundaries are not sharp. X-rays and gamma rays overlap: they are named by origin (X-rays from electrons, gamma rays from nuclei).
Mission 2

Orders of magnitude

  • Typical wavelengths (learn the power of ten):
  • Radio: 1 m (from about 0.1 m up to kilometres)
  • Microwave: 10⁻² m (1 mm to 0.1 m)
  • Infrared: 10⁻⁵ m (700 nm to 1 mm)
  • Visible: 5 × 10⁻⁷ m (400–700 nm)
  • Ultraviolet: 10⁻⁷ m (10–400 nm)
  • X-ray: 10⁻¹⁰ m (about 10⁻⁸ to 10⁻¹² m)
  • Gamma: 10⁻¹² m (below about 10⁻¹¹ m)
Mission check

?In a vacuum, the microwaves in a speed camera have a wavelength of 3.0 cm, and the light from a yellow lamp has a wavelength of 600 nm. How do their frequencies compare?

Microwaves and light, like radio waves and X-rays, are the same kind of wave, with the same speed c in a vacuum. Only the wavelength, and so the frequency, tells them apart.

Make a guess: Why can a few minutes of weak ultraviolet damage skin cells' DNA when hours of bright red light can't?

Mission 3

Light comes in packets: photons

  • Electromagnetic radiation is emitted and absorbed in discrete packets of energy: photons.
  • A photon is a quantum of electromagnetic energy.
  • Energy of one photon: E = hf = hc/λ.
  • h = Planck constant = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s.
  • Higher frequency (shorter wavelength) → more energy per photon.
  • A brighter beam of the same colour has more photons, not bigger ones.
Mission 3

The electronvolt

  • Photon energies in joules are tiny. Use the electronvolt.
  • 1 eV: the energy gained by an electron accelerated through a potential difference of 1 V.
  • 1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. J → eV: divide by 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹.
  • Example, blue light, λ = 450 nm:
    • E = hc/λ = (6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.00 × 10⁸)/(450 × 10⁻⁹) = 4.42 × 10⁻¹⁹ J
    • = 4.42 × 10⁻¹⁹/1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 2.76 eV.
Quick check
?A laser pointer emits light of wavelength 633 nm with a power of 5.0 mW. How many photons does it emit each second? Use h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s and c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹.
Quick check
?Predict first. Which observation from photoelectric experiments cannot be explained by the classical wave theory of light?
Mission 3

The photoelectric effect

Shine electromagnetic radiation on a clean metal surface. Electrons may be emitted: photoelectrons.

  • Below a threshold frequency f0: no emission, however intense the light.
  • Above f0: emission immediately, even in very dim light.
  • Maximum kinetic energy of the electrons rises with frequency; it does not depend on intensity.
  • Number emitted per second is proportional to intensity (above f0).
  • f0 depends on the metal (e.g. zinc needs ultraviolet).
Mission 3

Einstein's explanation

  • One photon gives all its energy to one electron: a one-to-one interaction.
  • Work function φ: the minimum energy needed to remove an electron from the metal's surface.
  • If hf < φ: no electron can escape. Threshold: hf0 = φ.
  • If hf is bigger than the work function, the extra energy becomes kinetic energy. Electrons from just below the surface lose some on the way out, so this is a maximum.
  • Ek max = hf − φ
Mission 3

Why waves can't explain it

Wave theory: energy arrives spread over the wavefront and builds up with intensity and time. It predicts:

  • Any frequency should work, given bright enough light. ✗ Observed: a sharp threshold f0.
  • Dim light should cause a delay while energy builds up. ✗ Observed: instant emission.
  • Brighter light should give faster electrons. ✗ Observed: the maximum kinetic energy is set by f alone.
  • Photon model explains all three: energy comes in lumps of hf, one lump per electron.
  • So the photoelectric effect is evidence for the particle (photon) nature of light.
Mission 3

Worked example

Light of wavelength 400 nm falls on potassium, work function 2.30 eV.

  • Photon energy: hf = hc/λ = (6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.00 × 10⁸)/(400 × 10⁻⁹) = 4.97 × 10⁻¹⁹ J = 3.11 eV.
  • Maximum kinetic energy = 3.11 − 2.30 = 0.81 eV = 1.29 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.
  • Threshold frequency: f0 = (work function)/h = (2.30 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹)/(6.63 × 10⁻³⁴) = 5.55 × 10¹⁴ Hz.
Mission 3

Stopping potential

  • Emitting plate and collecting plate in a vacuum tube; monochromatic light on the emitter.
  • Make the collector negative relative to the emitter: electrons are slowed as they cross.
  • Increase this reverse p.d. until the current just falls to zero: the stopping potential Vs.
  • Then even the fastest electrons are turned back: eVs = maximum kinetic energy.
  • In the worked example: maximum kinetic energy = 0.81 eV, so Vs = 0.81 V.
Quick check
?In a photoelectric experiment, monochromatic light whose photons each have energy 4.1 eV illuminates a metal plate in a vacuum tube. The graph shows the current I as a function of the potential difference V of the collecting plate relative to the illuminated plate. What is the work function of the metal?
Quick check
?Light whose photons each have energy 4.0 eV shines on three metals: X, with work function 2.0 eV; Y, with work function 3.0 eV; and Z, with work function 4.5 eV. Which statement about the maximum kinetic energy of the electrons emitted from each metal is correct?
Mission check

?Ultraviolet light above the threshold frequency shines on a metal plate and electrons are emitted. The ultraviolet is replaced by ultraviolet of a shorter wavelength, with the same intensity (the same power per square metre). What happens?

Energy per photon is set by the frequency; the number of photons by the power. The same holds in skin: each ultraviolet photon can damage a molecule on its own; red photons cannot, however many arrive.

Mission 4

Energy levels

  • An electron bound in an atom can have only certain energies: energy levels.
  • Lowest level: the ground state (n = 1). Higher levels: excited states.
  • Zero energy = electron just free of the atom (ionised).
  • So every bound level is negative: energy must be supplied to free the electron.
  • Hydrogen: −13.6 eV, −3.40 eV, −1.51 eV, −0.85 eV, … crowding towards 0.
  • 1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. Levels may be given in eV or J.
Mission 4

Emitting a photon

  • An electron drops from a higher level to a lower one. The atom emits one photon.
  • Photon energy = energy lost: hf = ΔE.
  • With c = fλ: λ = hc/ΔE.
  • Bigger drop → higher frequency → shorter wavelength.
  • Each transition gives one frequency: one spectral line.
Mission 4

Worked example

  • Hydrogen: n = 2 (−3.40 eV) → n = 1 (−13.6 eV).
  • ΔE = −3.40 − (−13.6) = 10.2 eV.
  • In joules: 10.2 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 1.63 × 10⁻¹⁸ J.
  • f = ΔE/h = 1.632 × 10⁻¹⁸ ÷ 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ = 2.46 × 10¹⁵ Hz.
  • λ = c/f = 1.22 × 10⁻⁷ m (122 nm, ultraviolet).
  • Always use the difference between levels, never one level's value.
Quick check
?A hypothetical atom has only four energy levels below its ionisation energy: −8.00 eV, −5.00 eV, −3.50 eV and −1.00 eV. In a hot gas of these atoms, collisions put atoms into all four levels, and none of the atoms is ionised. What is the longest wavelength of the photons that the gas emits in transitions among these levels? Take h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹ and 1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.
Mission 4

How many lines?

  • An excited electron can drop straight down or in steps.
  • Every pair of levels gives one possible line.
  • 3 levels → 3 lines: 3→2, 3→1, 2→1.
  • Different gaps → different wavelengths. The largest gap gives the shortest wavelength.
Quick check
?An atom has only four energy levels: −1.50 eV, −2.50 eV, −4.00 eV and −6.00 eV. What is the energy of the emitted photon that has the SHORTEST wavelength?
Mission 4

Absorbing a photon; ionisation

  • A photon is absorbed only if its energy exactly equals a gap from the electron's level. All or nothing.
  • Any other photon passes straight through.
  • Ionisation energy: energy to free the electron from the ground state = 0 − E1. Hydrogen: 13.6 eV (2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸ J).
  • Any photon with at least this energy can ionise; the extra becomes kinetic energy of the freed electron.
  • From an excited level, less is needed: the ground state needs the most.
Mission 4

Three kinds of spectrum

  • Continuous: all wavelengths. Hot, dense source: a lamp filament, a star's surface.
  • Emission line: bright lines on a dark background. Hot gas at low pressure.
  • Absorption line: a continuous spectrum crossed by dark lines. White light through a cooler gas.
  • The dark lines sit at the same wavelengths as that gas's emission lines.
  • They look dark because the absorbed energy is re-emitted in all directions, so little continues forward.
Quick check
?Predict first. Two discharge tubes, P and Q, each contain a single gas. The emission spectrum of each gas shows exactly four bright lines in the visible region, but none of the wavelengths of the lines from P matches any of the wavelengths of the lines from Q. A student claims that P and Q contain the same element. Which statement is correct?
Mission 4

Evidence and fingerprints

  • Sharp lines, not a smear: atoms change energy only by fixed amounts. Evidence for discrete levels.
  • Each element has its own set of levels, so its own set of lines.
  • Match the lines with laboratory spectra → identify the element.
  • Works in a lamp, a gas cloud or a star's outer layers.
  • Helium was discovered in 1868 from a yellow line in the Sun's spectrum that matched no known element.
Mission check

?An atom has energy levels at −10.0 eV (the ground state), −5.0 eV and −3.0 eV. An atom in its ground state is struck by a photon of energy 3.0 eV. What happens?

An atom can only take in or give out energy equal to a gap between its levels. Neon's gaps are fixed, so its photons, and its colours, are too.

Make a guess: Starlight has dark gaps in its rainbow. What do the gaps reveal about the star?

Mission 5

Making line spectra

  • Emission: a discharge tube (low-pressure gas, high p.d.), or a salt in a flame.
  • The gas is excited, then emits photons only at its own wavelengths: bright lines.
  • Absorption: shine white light through a cooler gas (for example sodium vapour in a flame).
  • The gas absorbs its own wavelengths: dark lines on a continuous spectrum.
Mission 5

Starlight

  • A star's hot, dense surface emits a continuous spectrum.
  • The light then passes through the star's cooler, tenuous (thin) outer atmosphere.
  • Atoms there absorb their own wavelengths → dark absorption lines.
  • Match each set of lines with laboratory spectra → the elements in the star's atmosphere.
  • Every element's lines are different, so several elements can be identified in one spectrum.
Mission check

?A cool cloud of gas lies in front of a hot star, and the star's spectrum seen through the cloud has dark lines. A second telescope views the same cloud from the side, against dark sky, with no star behind it. What does the second telescope see?

One gas absorbs and emits the same wavelengths. Seen against a hot source: dark lines. Seen against dark sky: bright lines. In a star, the dark lines list the elements in its cooler atmosphere.

Mission 6

Mission 6: How can we read the surface temperature of a star from its light alone?

Make a guess: How can we read the surface temperature of a star from its light alone?

Mission 6

A perfect absorber and emitter

  • All matter above absolute zero turns some of its internal energy into electromagnetic radiation.
  • A black body absorbs all the radiation that falls on it. None is reflected or transmitted.
  • It is also the best possible emitter at its temperature.
  • Its spectrum depends only on its temperature, not on what it is made of.
  • Stars are very good approximations to black bodies.
Quick check
?Predict first. A black body is heated to a higher temperature. How does its emission spectrum change?
Mission 6

Spectrum curves and temperature

  • Plot intensity per unit wavelength against wavelength: a smooth, continuous curve.
  • Hotter body: more at every wavelength. The peak is higher.
  • Hotter body: the peak moves to shorter wavelength.
  • The total power (area under the curve) rises steeply with temperature.
  • Colour shows this: red-hot (cooler) → yellow → white → bluish (hotter).
Mission 6

Wien's displacement law

  • Peak wavelength is inversely proportional to absolute temperature.
  • λmax = W/T, Wien constant W = 2.90 × 10⁻³ m K.
  • T must be in kelvin: T(K) = θ(°C) + 273.15, usually rounded to + 273.
  • The Sun: λmax ≈ 500 nm, so T ≈ 5800 K.
Mission 6

Worked example: a red star

  • A star's spectrum peaks at 850 nm (infrared, just beyond red light).
  • Use metres: 850 nm = 850 × 10⁻⁹ m.
  • T = 2.90 × 10⁻³ ÷ 850 × 10⁻⁹ = 3.4 × 10³ K
  • Longer peak wavelength → cooler surface. This star looks red.
Mission 6

Stefan–Boltzmann law

  • Total power radiated ∝ surface area × T⁴.
  • P = AσT⁴; for a star, P is its luminosity and A = 4πr².
  • σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W m⁻² K⁻⁴ (Stefan constant).
  • T⁴ matters: double T → 16 × the power.
Quick check
?A small metal sphere of radius 5.0 cm is heated to 527 °C. Treat it as a black body and take σ = 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ W m⁻² K⁻⁴. What power does the sphere emit as thermal radiation?
Mission 6

Worked example: the size of a star

  • A star's spectrum peaks at 290 nm. Its luminosity is 1.0 × 10²⁸ W.
  • Step 1, temperature (Wien):
  • T = 2.90 × 10⁻³ ÷ 290 × 10⁻⁹ = 1.0 × 10⁴ K
  • Step 2, radius (Stefan): P = AσT⁴ with A = 4πr² and P = L → r = √(L / 4πσT⁴)
  • r = √(1.0 × 10²⁸ ÷ (4π × 5.67 × 10⁻⁸ × (1.0 × 10⁴)⁴)) = 1.2 × 10⁹ m, about 1.7 times the Sun's radius.
Quick check
?A red giant has a luminosity of 100 L⊙ and a surface temperature of 4000 K. A white dwarf has a luminosity of 0.010 L⊙ and a surface temperature of 16 000 K. Both are modelled as black bodies. What is (radius of the red giant)/(radius of the white dwarf)?
Mission check

?The graph shows the intensity per unit wavelength emitted by a black body as a function of wavelength λ. The absolute temperature of the black body is then doubled. At what wavelength does the spectrum of the heated black body peak?

Peak wavelength ∝ 1/T. So the colour of a star (where its spectrum peaks) gives its surface temperature: bluish stars are hotter than reddish ones.

Mission 7

Mission 7: Two stars look equally bright in the night sky. How can we tell which one really gives out more light?

Make a guess: Two stars look equally bright in the night sky. How can we tell which one really gives out more light?

Mission 7

Luminosity

  • Luminosity, L: the total power a star emits, in watts.
  • Stars are close to black bodies.
  • P = AσT⁴: Stefan's law. For a star, P is its luminosity L and A = 4πr².
  • Same temperature, twice the radius → 4 × the luminosity.
Mission 7

What we receive

  • The star's power spreads over a sphere of area 4πd² at distance d.
  • Intensity, I: the power received per unit area, in W m⁻².
  • I = L/(4πd²)
  • Inverse-square law: twice as far → ¼ as much per square metre.
  • A faint star may be weak, or just far away.
Quick check
?A star has 40 times the Sun's luminosity and is 25 ly from the Earth. Take the Sun's luminosity = 3.83 × 10²⁶ W and 1 ly = 9.46 × 10¹⁵ m. What is the intensity of the star's light at the Earth?
Mission check

?At the Earth, the intensity of star A's light is 4 times that of star B's. Star B is 3 times as far from the Earth as star A. What is the ratio (luminosity of A) ÷ (luminosity of B)?

Brightness alone cannot tell you a star's luminosity. Measure its distance too; then L = 4πd² × (power received per square metre).

Mission 8

Mission 8: A smudge of light in a telescope: how do astronomers work out what they are looking at?

Make a guess: A smudge of light in a telescope: how do astronomers work out what they are looking at?

Mission 8

One region, many pictures

  • Multiwavelength astronomy: observe the same region at many wavelengths. Each photon energy reveals a different process.
  • Example: a star-forming nebula such as Orion.
  • Radio: cold molecular gas, the raw material for the next stars.
  • Infrared: warm dust, and young stars still hidden inside dusty cocoons.
  • Visible: gas glowing at about 10⁴ K, lit by hot young stars.
  • X-ray: flares in the hot outer layers of young stars, millions of kelvin.
  • No single wavelength shows the whole story.
Mission 8

Why wavelength tracks process

  • Hot objects emit most strongly at λmax = W/T, with W = 2.90 × 10⁻³ m K (Wien's law).
  • Dust at 29 K peaks at 100 μm (far infrared). A star surface at 5800 K peaks at 500 nm (visible). Gas at 2.9 × 10⁶ K peaks at 1 nm (X-ray).
  • Some emission is not thermal: e.g. radio from fast electrons spiralling in magnetic fields.
  • The atmosphere passes visible and most radio. It blocks gamma rays, X-rays, most UV and much IR.
  • So X-ray, gamma-ray, UV and most IR telescopes go into space.
Quick check
?A white dwarf has a surface temperature of 2.5 × 10⁴ K. At what wavelength is its emission strongest? (W = 2.90 × 10⁻³ m K)
Quick check
?Astronomers want to study stars that are forming deep inside a dense cloud of dust. Which instrument is the best choice?
Mission check

?An image of a distant galaxy taken in X-rays shows a bright point source. A visible-light image shows nothing at that point. What is the most likely explanation?

What you see depends on how you look. Each wavelength band shows a different process: cold gas in radio, dust in infrared, star surfaces in visible, million-kelvin gas in X-rays.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • A progressive wave transfers energy, not matter. Transverse: oscillation ⟂ travel; longitudinal: oscillation ∥ travel.
  • f = 1/T and v = fλ. For sound and waves on strings, the medium sets v and the source sets f.

Mission 2

  • EM waves: oscillating E and B fields, perpendicular to each other and to the direction of travel (transverse); no medium; all travel at c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹ in a vacuum.
  • Radio → microwave → infrared → visible → UV → X-ray → gamma: wavelength falls, frequency rises.

Mission 3

  • Photon energy E = hf = hc/λ; 1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. Intensity sets the number of photons, frequency the energy of each.
  • One photon frees at most one electron; threshold hf0 = φ; maximum kinetic energy = hf − φ, independent of intensity. Evidence for photons.
  • Stopping potential: eVs = maximum kinetic energy.

Mission 4

  • Levels are negative (0 = ionised). A transition emits or absorbs one photon: hf = ΔE, λ = hc/ΔE.
  • Line spectra are evidence of discrete levels, and each element's lines identify it, in a lamp or a star.

Mission 5

  • A star's spectrum is continuous light from its surface with dark lines from its cooler atmosphere; the lines identify its elements.

Mission 6

  • A black body absorbs all incident radiation; its spectrum depends only on T. Hotter: more at every λ, peak at shorter λ.
  • Wien: λmax = W/T, W = 2.90 × 10⁻³ m K.
  • P = AσT⁴.
  • Star's radius: T from Wien, then r from Stefan.

Mission 7

  • Luminosity is a star's total power output: P = AσT⁴, with A = 4πr².
  • Intensity I = L/(4πd²): a faint star may be weak or distant.

Mission 8

  • Multiwavelength astronomy: each wavelength band reveals a different process; X-ray, gamma and most UV and IR need space telescopes.
