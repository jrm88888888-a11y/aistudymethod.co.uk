The Age of the Crusades c1071-1204

This is an optional route. AQA A-level History is option-based. 1A The Age of the Crusades is one of eleven Component 1 breadth studies (1A–1L). It is a non-British option, so you would pair it with a British Component 2 depth study, plus the Component 3 Historical Investigation.

This mini-lesson covers both halves of 1A: the crusader states and outremer, c1071–1149 and decline and fall, 1149–1204. Across 130 years the question is the same — why did Latin Christians go east, how did they hold on, and why did they lose?

The option · what 1A actually asks

A breadth study, not an adventure story

AQA's key questions for 1A run right across the period:

  • How effective was the leadership of the crusades and the crusader states?
  • How important were religious ideas and beliefs as a motive and a justification?
  • How significant were Muslim and Byzantine responses?
  • What was the impact of the crusades on Western Europe, Byzantium and the Islamic world?
  • How far did the crusader states change and develop?
  • How important were key individuals and groups?

The definitional problem comes first. What is a crusade? Traditionalists restrict the term to expeditions to the Holy Land. Pluralists — following Jonathan Riley-Smith — define it institutionally: papal authorisation, a vow, the cross, and the indulgence, wherever the target lay (Spain, the Baltic, southern France). Your definition shapes your whole essay. State it.

Origins · c1071–1099

Manzikert, Clermont and the First Crusade

Two events set everything in motion.

  • Manzikert, 1071: the Seljuk Turks destroyed a Byzantine army and captured Emperor Romanos IV. Byzantine Anatolia — the empire's recruiting ground — unravelled. Two decades later Alexios I Komnenos appealed west for mercenaries.
  • Clermont, November 1095: Pope Urban II preached an armed pilgrimage to aid eastern Christians and liberate Jerusalem, offering a remission of penance — the origin of the crusade indulgence. What Alexios wanted (troops) and what Urban launched (a mass armed pilgrimage) were not the same thing, and that mismatch poisoned Latin–Byzantine relations for a century.
  • The disorderly People's Crusade (1096) and the anti-Jewish massacres in the Rhineland revealed what popular crusade enthusiasm could become.
  • The princes' crusade took Nicaea (1097), survived Dorylaeum, endured the terrible siege of Antioch (October 1097 – June 1098), and stormed Jerusalem on 15 July 1099 amid a massacre. Godfrey of Bouillon took the title Advocate of the Holy Sepulchre; his brother Baldwin became King of Jerusalem in 1100.

Why did it succeed? Not superior arms. The decisive factor was the fragmentation of the Islamic Near East — Seljuk succession disputes, and the Sunni–Shia split between Seljuk Syria and Fatimid Egypt. Any answer that credits only crusader faith or Muslim disunity alone is half an answer.

Settlement · the crusader states

Outremer: holding the land beyond the sea

Four Latin states were carved out — Edessa (1098), Antioch (1098), Jerusalem (1099) and Tripoli (1109). Most crusaders went home; the settlers were always a tiny minority.

  • Manpower was the permanent crisis. The answer was castles — Krak des Chevaliers, Kerak, Saphet — and the military orders: the Templars (recognised at the Council of Troyes, 1129) and the Hospitallers, who became standing professional armies and great landholders.
  • The Italian maritime cities — Genoa, Pisa, Venice — supplied sea power and siege support in return for trading quarters. Outremer's survival was commercial as much as military.
  • Coexistence: Latin lords ruled a majority population of Muslims, Jews and eastern Christians. Practical accommodation was routine, even while the ideology remained one of holy war.

Interpretation: older writing (notably Steven Runciman, whose three-volume A History of the Crusades appeared 1951–54) treated the settlers as a brutal colonial intrusion into a superior eastern civilisation. Later scholars — Jonathan Riley-Smith, Christopher Tyerman, Thomas Asbridge — restored the settlers' own religious logic and stressed the states' adaptation and durability. Nearly two centuries of survival is not obviously a story of failure.

Decline · 1144–1187

Zengi, Nur ad-Din, Saladin — and Hattin

Outremer's fate was decided less by crusader failings than by the reunification of the Muslim Near East.

  • 1144: Zengi captured Edessa — the first crusader state to fall. The shock prompted the Second Crusade (1145–1149), preached by Bernard of Clairvaux and led by Louis VII of France and Conrad III of Germany. It ended in the humiliating failure of the siege of Damascus (July 1148) — an attack on a city that had been Jerusalem's ally.
  • Nur ad-Din united Muslim Syria and pursued the ideology of jihad with real consistency.
  • Saladin (Salah ad-Din) took power in Egypt, becoming vizier in 1169 and ending the Fatimid caliphate in 1171. After Nur ad-Din's death in 1174 he spent a decade uniting Egypt and Syria — encircling the kingdom of Jerusalem.
  • 4 July 1187: at Hattin, Saladin destroyed the field army of Jerusalem. Jerusalem surrendered in October 1187 — this time without a massacre.

Causation, layered: long-term — Muslim unification and settler manpower shortage; medium-term — the failure of the Second Crusade and the Latin succession crisis after Baldwin IV; short-term — the decision to march from the springs at Saffuriya into waterless country in July 1187. Examiners reward exactly this layering.

1189–1204 · the last acts

The Third Crusade and the disaster of the Fourth

The Third Crusade (1189–1192) was the largest western response of the century, and it recovered a coastline but not a city.

  • Frederick Barbarossa drowned in Anatolia in 1190, and his army largely dissolved. Philip II of France and Richard I of England took Acre in July 1191; Philip then went home.
  • Richard won at Arsuf (September 1191), twice advanced towards Jerusalem and twice turned back — a defensible strategic judgement, since a captured Jerusalem could not have been held once the crusaders sailed home.
  • The Treaty of Jaffa (September 1192) left the Latins a coastal strip from Tyre to Jaffa and guaranteed Christian pilgrim access to Jerusalem. Saladin died in 1193.
  • The Fourth Crusade (1202–1204) never reached the Holy Land. Unable to pay Venice for its fleet, the army sacked the Christian city of Zara (1202) and then, drawn into a Byzantine succession dispute, stormed and sacked Constantinople in April 1204, founding the Latin Empire. Pope Innocent III had forbidden it and condemned it.

The verdict on 1204: it crippled the one Christian power capable of shielding the eastern Mediterranean, and it made the schism between Latin and Orthodox Christendom permanent in feeling as well as in doctrine. AQA ends the option here for a reason.

Interpretations · the crusade debate

Runciman, Riley-Smith and the Islamic perspective

The historiography of the crusades has been rewritten twice in living memory. Know who says what.

Steven Runciman (A History of the Crusades, 1951–54). Elegantly written, deeply hostile: crusading as western barbarism and greed, destructive above all of Byzantium. Enormously influential — and now the standard target.
Jonathan Riley-Smith (The First Crusade and the Idea of Crusading, 1986; The First Crusaders, 1997). Used charters and mortgages to show that crusaders impoverished themselves to go east. That evidence demolishes the 'landless younger sons seeking plunder' thesis: motivation was overwhelmingly penitential and pious. Riley-Smith is also the leading pluralist on definition.
Carole Hillenbrand (The Crusades: Islamic Perspectives, 1999) insisted the story be told from Arabic sources — and showed that the crusades loomed far smaller in the medieval Islamic imagination than modern rhetoric assumes.
Christopher Tyerman (God's War, 2006) reads crusading as a mainstream institution of Latin Christendom — organised, financed, legally defined — rather than an aberration.

Test, don't recite. Riley-Smith's charter evidence is strong for the knightly class who left records. It says less about the poor. That is how you evaluate an interpretation: identify its evidence base, then find its edge.

Exam technique · Component 1

Sources from a medieval world

AQA Component 1 is 80 marks, 2 hours 30 minutes, 40% of the A-level: one compulsory three-source question (30) and two essays from three (25 each).

  • Source evaluation in 1A is distinctive. Your sources are chronicles — the Gesta Francorum, William of Tyre, Ibn al-Athir, Anna Komnene's Alexiad, Usama ibn Munqidh. None is a neutral report. Each is written to justify, lament or instruct.
  • So ask: who is this writer serving? Anna Komnene writes to defend her father Alexios. William of Tyre writes to explain the kingdom's decline to itself. Ibn al-Athir writes decades later, in a tradition shaped by the triumph of Saladin.
  • Value is not the same as reliability. A partisan chronicle is enormously valuable evidence for the attitudes of the group that produced it — say so explicitly.

Key question · Byzantium

The empire the crusades were meant to save

Byzantium is the strand students most often drop — and it frames the whole option, since 1A opens with a Byzantine defeat and closes with a Byzantine catastrophe.

  • 1071: Manzikert shattered Byzantine Anatolia and, two decades later, produced Alexios I's appeal to the West.
  • The mismatch: Alexios wanted mercenaries under imperial command. Urban II launched a mass armed pilgrimage under its own leaders. The oaths sworn at Constantinople in 1097 were bitterly disputed almost immediately — above all over Antioch, which Bohemond kept for himself.
  • The recriminations compounded: Latins accused the Greeks of treachery; Greeks regarded the Latins as barbarians and, worse, as a standing threat. Anna Komnene's Alexiad is written from inside that resentment.
  • 1204: the Fourth Crusade sacked Constantinople and installed a Latin Empire. The eastern bulwark of Christendom was crippled by its supposed allies.

The judgement: Runciman's most durable claim is not that crusaders were barbarians but that the crusades were, for Byzantium, an unmitigated disaster. That is much harder to refute than the rest of his case — and conceding a strong point in an interpretation you otherwise reject is a mark of a serious historian.
