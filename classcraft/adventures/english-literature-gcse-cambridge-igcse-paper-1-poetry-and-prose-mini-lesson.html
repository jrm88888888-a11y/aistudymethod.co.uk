The paper

How Paper 1 is built

Paper 1 is compulsory: 1 hour 30 minutes, 50 marks, and it carries 50% of the qualification.

  • You answer two questions: one from Section A (Poetry) and one from Section B (Prose).
  • All questions carry equal marks — 25 each. So you have roughly 45 minutes per answer, including planning.
  • There is a choice of two questions on each text, and the relevant passages and poems are printed on the question paper.

The four assessment objectives (equally weighted, 25% each): AO1 show detailed knowledge of the content of literary texts, supported by reference to the text; AO2 understand the meanings of literary texts and their contexts, and explore texts beyond surface meanings; AO3 recognise and appreciate ways in which writers use language, structure and form to create and shape meanings and effects; AO4 communicate a sensitive and informed personal response.

Set texts rotate. Cambridge publishes a new set-text list for each examination year, so this mini-lesson teaches the analytical skills for the genre rather than the plot of any one book. Apply every method here to your set text — the one your teacher has given you.

The AOs

What the examiner is actually rewarding

Every mark on this paper is one of four things. Learn what each one looks like in a sentence.

The same idea, written four ways

AO1 (knowledge): The poem ends with the speaker still standing at the gate.

AO2 (meaning beyond the surface): The gate is never opened, and the poem quietly makes that failure the point — the speaker is a person who arrives but does not enter.

AO3 (writer craft): The final line is one syllable shorter than every line before it, so the poem stops early, as though the sentence itself could not finish.

AO4 (personal response): What stays with me is how ordinary this is: the poem refuses tragedy and gives us something worse, which is a life that simply does not begin.

The single most useful habit: after every point you make, ask have I said how the writer does it? If not, you have written AO1 and stopped short of AO3 — where half the marks live.

Poetry

Section A — reading a poem properly

Work through a poem in this order. It stops you from grabbing the first metaphor you see and writing 400 words about it.

  • Voice — who is speaking, to whom, and what do they want? The speaker is not the poet unless you can prove it.
  • Situation — what is literally happening? Get this right before you get clever.
  • Form — sonnet, free verse, dramatic monologue, ballad? A form carries expectations the poet can meet or betray.
  • Structure — where does the poem turn? Most poems have a hinge (a volta): find it and you have found the argument.
  • Imagery — what are the patterns, not just the individual pictures?
  • Sound — rhyme, rhythm, alliteration, sibilance, caesura, enjambment. Sound is meaning you can hear.
  • The ending — poems put their weight at the end. Always account for the last line.
Poetry

Form and structure carry meaning

Weak answers treat form as trivia (this is a sonnet, which has fourteen lines). Strong answers show form doing something.

  • Enjambment — a sentence spilling over the line break can create urgency, breathlessness, or the sense of something that cannot be contained by the shape it is in.
  • Caesura — a hard pause inside a line can enact hesitation, interruption or a mind stopping short.
  • Regular rhyme can feel like control, inevitability, or a lid held down on feeling — and a broken rhyme scheme is that control failing.
  • Stanza shape — a poem in neat quatrains that ends with a single isolated line has put a person somewhere alone, on the page, where you can see it.

The test: could your sentence about form be pasted into an essay about a different poem? If yes, it is a label, not an analysis. Make it specific to this poem meaning.

Prose

Section B — reading prose properly

Prose questions are answered badly for one reason above all: candidates retell the story. Retelling is AO1 at its lowest, and it is worth almost nothing on its own.

  • Narrative voice — first person or third? Limited or omniscient? Can we trust them? A narrator who tells us too much is hiding something.
  • Characterisation — a character is built: through speech habits, through what others say, through what they notice, through what they never mention.
  • Setting — never just scenery. Places in fiction carry mood, class, history and power.
  • Structure — chapter endings, time shifts, flashback, foreshadowing, and where the writer chooses to cut away.
  • Withholding — what the writer refuses to tell you is a technique. Ask why the door was closed on that scene.

Question types

Passage-based and essay questions

You will meet two kinds of question across the Literature papers, and they need different plans.

  • Passage-based: an extract is printed and you explore it closely. Work through the passage in order, but keep asking how each moment fits the whole text. Depth first; breadth as support.
  • Essay: no extract is given, so you supply the evidence from memory. Choose three or four well-spread moments and quote briefly and precisely.

The trap in each: a passage-based answer that never leaves the extract, and an essay answer that never touches the words on the page. Both lose AO3 marks — the first for missing the wider shape, the second for having nothing concrete to analyse.

Writing it

The paragraph that scores

Twenty-five marks, roughly 45 minutes: that is about five or six developed paragraphs plus a real introduction and a real conclusion.

The four-move paragraph

1 Claim — an arguable point that answers the question asked (not the topic).

2 Evidence — a short, precise quotation or a specific reference.

3 Method — how the writer does it: the word, the image, the structure, the form.

4 Effect and judgement — what it does to the reader, and what you make of it.

Quote briefly. Two words you can dissect are worth more than four lines you copy out. If you cannot say something about the words in your quotation, choose a different quotation.

Personal response

AO4 — having something to say

Cambridge asks for a sensitive and informed personal response. Both words matter: personal (it is your reading) and informed (it is built on evidence, not on feelings alone).

  • Take a position in your introduction. Not this essay will discuss... but a claim someone could disagree with.
  • Use the evaluative sentence. The most disturbing thing about this scene is not the violence but how quickly everyone returns to their tea.
  • Acknowledge complexity. Words like although, and yet, which complicates are the grammar of a sophisticated reader.
  • Never fake emotion. Examiners can spot manufactured passion instantly. Precision is more moving than adjectives.

What AO4 is not: it is not I really liked this poem. It is an argued reading that only you could have written, because it is grounded in what you noticed.
