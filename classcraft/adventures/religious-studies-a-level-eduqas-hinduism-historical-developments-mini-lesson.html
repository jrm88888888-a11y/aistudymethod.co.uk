Mission 1

Roots: Chaitanya and Gaudiya Vaishnavism

  • ISKCON: the International Society for Krishna Consciousness, founded in New York in 1966. Popularly "the Hare Krishnas".
  • It belongs to Gaudiya Vaishnavism, the Bengal ("Gauda") devotional tradition.
  • Founder of that tradition: Chaitanya Mahaprabhu (1486–1534), who led ecstatic group chanting of Krishna's names (sankirtan) in the streets.
  • Key texts: the Bhagavad Gita and the Bhagavata Purana (Srimad Bhagavatam).
  • ISKCON sees itself as part of an unbroken chain of teachers (parampara) back to Chaitanya and Krishna.
Mission 1

Prabhupada: a mission at seventy

  • A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada (1896–1977), born Abhay Charan De in Calcutta.
  • 1922: met his guru Bhaktisiddhanta Sarasvati (1874–1937), who asked him to preach Krishna consciousness in English.
  • Family man and pharmacist for decades; founded the magazine Back to Godhead (1944); took sannyasa (renunciation) in 1959.
  • 1965: sailed to America on a cargo ship, aged 69, with very little money and a trunk of his translations.
  • 1966: founded ISKCON in a New York shopfront; chanting in Tompkins Square Park drew young Americans.
  • By his death in Vrindavan (1977): over 100 centres, many books (Bhagavad-gita As It Is, 1968/1972), a Governing Body Commission (1970).
Mission 1

Krishna consciousness

  • Krishna consciousness: every soul's original nature is to love and serve Krishna. We forget this and identify with the body.
  • The soul (jiva) is eternal, both one with Krishna in quality and different from him: achintya-bhedabheda ("inconceivable oneness and difference", Chaitanya's teaching).
  • So ISKCON rejects Advaita's impersonal Brahman as the highest truth: God is a person.
  • Goal: pure loving devotion (prema bhakti) and return to Krishna's eternal realm, not merging into Brahman.
  • In this age of quarrel (Kali Yuga) the easiest path is chanting God's names.
  • The maha-mantra: "Hare Krishna, Hare Krishna, Krishna Krishna, Hare Hare / Hare Rama, Hare Rama, Rama Rama, Hare Hare".

Mission 1

A regulated life

  • Four regulative principles for initiated devotees:
  • no meat, fish or eggs · no intoxicants (including alcohol, tobacco, tea and coffee) · no gambling · no sex outside marriage.
  • Chant 16 rounds of the maha-mantra a day on 108-bead japa mala.
  • Temple day begins about 4:30 am with mangala arati; worship of the deities Radha and Krishna.
  • Food cooked for Krishna and shared as prasadam; free meals (Food for Life).
  • Public chanting (harinama), book distribution, festivals such as Rathayatra (chariot procession, London since 1969).
  • Your spec: "regulation of life, an austere code of behaviour".
Mission 1

Why did it grow?

Religious reasons

  • A clear, personal God and a simple daily practice (chanting) open to anyone.
  • An authority: an ancient lineage, a guru, and English translations with commentary.

Cultural reasons

  • 1960s counterculture: young Westerners looking beyond materialism and drugs; communal living.
  • Celebrity: George Harrison produced the "Hare Krishna Mantra" single (1969) and gave Bhaktivedanta Manor (Hertfordshire, 1973).
  • Diaspora: from the 1970s, Indian Hindus in Britain and America joined temples in large numbers: Vaishnava worship they knew, explained in English to their children.

Mission 1

After Prabhupada

  • 1977: authority passed to the Governing Body Commission (GBC) and initially eleven initiating gurus.
  • 1980s: several gurus fell from their vows; reforms opened the guru role to more devotees; some breakaway groups disputed who could initiate.
  • 1990s: ISKCON publicly admitted abuse of children in its 1970s–80s boarding schools (gurukulas) and set up a child-protection office.
  • Western converts fell; Indian (and later Russian, African, Latin American) members grew.
  • Today: large temples in India (e.g. Mayapur, Bengaluru), and in Britain Bhaktivedanta Manor is a major Hindu pilgrimage site at Janmashtami.
Mission 1Evaluate

"ISKCON is a divergence from traditional Hinduism."

  • For divergence: converts from outside Indian birth and caste; it seeks converts, unlike most Hindu traditions; one God (Krishna) is supreme and other deities are servants of Krishna; strict rules most Hindus do not follow; a global, centrally governed organisation; it has sometimes avoided the word "Hindu".
  • Against: its theology is 500 years old (Chaitanya); its texts are classic Hindu scriptures; Gaudiya temples in India recognise it; most of its members in Britain are Hindus of Indian origin; Hindu bodies in Britain treat it as Hindu.
  • Deeper question: is there one "traditional" Hinduism to diverge from? (.)
  • Judge it by: beliefs, practice, membership, organisation: which matters most for "traditional"?
Mission 1Evaluate

How important was Prabhupada?

  • Crucial: without his decision to go West at 69, his translations and his discipline, there would be no ISKCON. Devotees regard him as the founder-acharya whose books remain the main authority.
  • Other factors: the 1960s counterculture; the Beatles' publicity; Indian migration, which supplied money and congregations; leaders after 1977.
  • Note: the crises after his death show both his central role (no successor of equal authority) and its limits.
  • Judge it by: origin vs growth; growth in which period and where?

Mission 2

Why science is a test for any religion

  • Modern science asks for evidence and reason: claims are tested, and can be revised.
  • Religions often rest on revelation (for Hindus, shruti, "what is heard": the Vedas) and on tradition.
  • So science can challenge a tradition in three ways:
  • Method: is faith reasonable without evidence?
  • Discoveries: an old universe (Big Bang), evolution (Darwin).
  • Ethics: new powers (genetic engineering) raise new moral questions.
  • Hindus have answered in very different ways, from "no conflict at all" to firm rejection of parts of science.
Mission 2

Science inside the tradition

  • Many Hindus point out that Indian thinkers did science long before modern Europe.
  • Astronomy: Aryabhata (476–c. 550 CE), Aryabhatiya (499 CE): the earth turns on its axis; a value of π close to 3.1416; explained eclipses by shadows.
  • Brahmagupta (598–c. 668 CE): rules for calculating with zero and negative numbers.
  • Medicine: Ayurveda ("knowledge of long life"). The Charaka Samhita (internal medicine) and the Sushruta Samhita (surgery), compiled in the centuries around the turn of the era.
  • Astronomy grew partly from religious need: jyotisha set the right times for Vedic rituals.
  • Your spec spells him Aryabhatta and names Charaka: rishis seen as philosophers, mathematicians and scientists.
Mission 2

Samkhya: classifying matter

  • Samkhya ("enumeration") is one of the six classical schools (darshanas); its key text is the Samkhya Karika (c. 4th–5th century CE).
  • Two ultimate realities: purusha (pure consciousness) and prakriti (matter, nature).
  • Prakriti is made of three gunas: sattva (clarity), rajas (energy), tamas (inertia).
  • It unfolds into twenty-four principles (tattvas), ending in the five gross elements: space, air, fire, water, earth.
  • Some Hindus see this as an early, reasoned classification of matter: an ancestor of science.
  • Others note it is a philosophy of liberation, not experiment: it classifies matter to free purusha from it.
Mission 2

"It was all in the Vedas"

  • Some Hindus hold that modern discoveries were revealed in the Vedas long ago.
  • Dayananda Saraswati (1824–1883), founder of the Arya Samaj, Satyarth Prakash (1875): the Vedas are the source of all true knowledge, including science (paraphrase).
  • Popular claims today: flying machines (vimanas) in the epics; nuclear weapons; "Vedic mathematics".
  • Critics, including many Hindu scientists and historians: these readings put modern ideas back into ancient texts; they can make Indian science look like myth and hide its real achievements.
  • Supporters: the Vedas are eternal knowledge, so modern science rediscovers what the rishis saw.
  • Many Hindus see science as a legitimate but incomplete step towards knowing reality: it can describe the material world (prakriti), but in Hindu teaching knowing Brahman or the atman needs shruti and direct spiritual experience.
Mission 2

Radhakrishnan: a scientific religion

  • Sarvepalli Radhakrishnan (1888–1975), philosopher; President of India 1962–1967. The Hindu View of Life (1927); An Idealist View of Life (1932).
  • His claim (paraphrase): Hinduism rests on experience, not dogma. The rishis were explorers who tested their insight in meditation, as scientists test theories.
  • Religious truth is reached by intuition (anubhava, direct experience), checked by reason and by others' experience.
  • Hinduism is open: it can absorb new knowledge without crisis, because it has no fixed creed.
  • So science and Hinduism are compatible: both seek the truth about one reality.
  • Critics: religious experience cannot be repeated and checked as a lab result can; and his "Hinduism" is mostly Advaita Vedanta, not the whole tradition.
Mission 2Read the source

Days and nights of Brahma

Bhagavad Gita 8.18 (Edwin Arnold, 1885):

> "When that vast Dawn doth break, th' Invisible / Is brought anew into the Visible; / When that deep Night doth darken, all which is / Fades back again to Him Who sent it forth"

  • Hindu cosmology is cyclical: universes emerge and dissolve again and again.
  • A kalpa (a "day of Brahma") is usually reckoned at 4.32 billion years.
  • Many Hindus say the Big Bang fits: it can be the dawn of the present cycle, and the vast scale of time matches modern cosmology.

Q1 According to the passage, where does everything go at "Night"?

Show answer

Q2 Name one difference between this picture and a single Big Bang.

Show answer

Cycles without first beginning, against one beginning about 13.8 billion years ago.

Mission 2

Darwin: three Hindu responses

  • Acceptance: many Hindus accept evolution. There was no Hindu "Genesis" debate: no fixed six-day creation to defend.
  • Avatar reading: some see a parallel in the ten avatars of Vishnu (fish → tortoise → boar → man-lion → dwarf → human forms). Critics: the avatars are divine descents, not species changing over time.
  • Spiritual evolution: Sri Aurobindo (1872–1950), The Life Divine (1939–40): evolution is real, but it is spirit unfolding in matter towards higher consciousness.
  • Hindus differ on natural selection: acceptance of common descent is wider than acceptance that blind chance explains everything.
  • A soul (atman) passes through many bodies (samsara); for many Hindus that is about the self, not about biology.
Mission 2

ISKCON: devolution, not evolution

  • ISKCON (International Society for Krishna Consciousness), founded 1966 by A. C. Bhaktivedanta Swami Prabhupada (1896–1977).
  • Prabhupada rejected Darwinian evolution: life comes from life, and consciousness cannot come from matter.
  • Species are bodies through which souls move by karma: traditionally 8,400,000 forms of life (from the Puranas).
  • The Bhaktivedanta Institute (founded 1976) argues against materialist science. Michael Cremo and Richard Thompson, Forbidden Archeology (1993), argue humans are far older than evolution allows.
  • Cremo's Human Devolution (2003): souls descend from a spiritual world into matter.
  • Most scientists reject these claims; ISKCON sees them as defending the Vedas' account against materialism.
  • Big Bang: ISKCON reads creation as Krishna's cyclical creation through Maha-Vishnu, not a chance event.

Mission 2Evaluate

How effective are Hindu responses to science?

  • Claim: Hinduism has met the challenge of science more easily than other religions.
  • For: no fixed creation date; cyclical vast time fits cosmology; experience-based religion (Radhakrishnan) can absorb new knowledge.
  • For: a real history of Indian science (Aryabhata, Ayurveda) gives Hindus pride, not suspicion, towards science.
  • Against: "It was all in the Vedas" claims may avoid the challenge rather than meet it.
  • Against: ISKCON and others reject evolution: there is real conflict for some Hindus.
  • Against: easy fit may come from making Hinduism vague: if anything fits, does belief say anything?
  • Judgement criteria: Which Hindus? (conservative and liberal differ.) Fit with science, or fit with the tradition? Does the response face evidence fairly?
  • One common grouping: more conservative responses defend scripture's authority over science ("all in the Vedas"; ISKCON's rejection of Darwin); more liberal responses reinterpret the tradition in the light of science (Radhakrishnan, Aurobindo, acceptance of evolution).
  • Spec issue: "the effectiveness of Hindu responses to the challenges of science".

Mission 3

Savarkar: "Hindu-ness"

  • Hindutva = "Hindu-ness". Set out by V. D. Savarkar (1883–1966) in Essentials of Hindutva (1923), written in prison.
  • Savarkar, himself a rationalist, separated Hindutva (a people's culture, history and nation) from Hinduism (religious belief).
  • His definition (paraphrase): a Hindu is someone who regards India (Bharatvarsha) as both pitribhumi (fatherland) and punyabhumi (holy land).
  • So Sikhs, Jains and Buddhists count: their faiths began in India.
  • Muslims and Christians, even if Indian for generations, do not fully belong: their holy lands are elsewhere.

Mission 3

Hindutva and secularisation

  • India is a secular state (Constitution 1950; "secular" added to the Preamble in 1976). Indian secularism means equal respect for all religions, not strict separation (see Mission 4).
  • Hindutva critics call this "pseudo-secularism": they argue it treats minorities better than Hindus (e.g. separate Muslim personal law, state control of Hindu temples).
  • Their response to secularisation (the weakening of religion in public life): reassert Hindu culture in public: temples, festivals, symbols, school history, the name Bharat.
  • So Hindutva is both a response to secularism (the state's policy) and to secularisation (social change).
  • Your spec asks whether this is the only effective response.
Mission 3Diversity

Hindus on Hindutva

  • Supporters: Hindutva is cultural, not theocratic; it includes all Indic faiths; it corrects centuries of foreign rule and an unfair secularism. India's Supreme Court in 1995 described Hindutva as indicating a "way of life".
  • Gandhian and Neo-Vedantin critics: true Hinduism is tolerant and universal; Hindutva turns a spiritual tradition into a political identity.
  • Secular and liberal Hindus: Hindutva threatens minorities' equal citizenship; they point to violence in Ayodhya (1992) and Gujarat (2002).
  • Dalit critics: Hindutva speaks for upper-caste Hinduism and hides caste inequality.
  • Many Sikhs, Jains and Buddhists: reject being counted as "Hindu".
  • Scholars, e.g. Christophe Jaffrelot (The Hindu Nationalist Movement and Indian Politics, 1996), study it as ethnic nationalism; supporters reject this label.
Mission 3Evaluate

"Hindutva is the only effective Hindu response to secularisation."

  • For: it has mass support and electoral success; it gives a clear identity in a fast-changing society; it has brought Hindu symbols back into public life.
  • Against: other responses also work: Neo-Vedanta's universal spirituality; devotional movements (ISKCON, Swaminarayan) that grow without politics; reform movements serving the poor (Ramakrishna Mission).
  • Against: "effective" for whom? Critics say it secures power but divides society and narrows Hinduism.
  • Orthodox Hindus may accept some secularisation as part of the decline expected in the Kali Yuga (the present age of decline in dharma; see Mission 4), so not every Hindu thinks it needs a political answer.
  • Judge it by: what counts as "effective" (numbers, depth of faith, social harmony)? Is Hindutva mainly religious at all?

Mission 4

A different kind of secular

  • The Constitution (1950) has no state religion; "secular" was added to its Preamble in 1976.
  • Equality before the law and no discrimination on grounds of religion or caste (Articles 14–15).
  • Everyone may "profess, practise and propagate" religion (Article 25, paraphrase); religious groups may run their own affairs and schools (Articles 26, 30).
  • Not a wall of separation (as in the USA or France): the Indian state engages with all religions: it funds pilgrimages, manages many Hindu temples, and has reformed Hindu practice by law.
  • Political theorist Rajeev Bhargava calls this "principled distance": the state may treat religions differently, but for the sake of equality.
  • So "secular state" (a policy) is not the same as secularisation (religion losing influence in society).

Mission 4

Secularisation in India

  • Signs: cities, education, the internet, consumer culture; more inter-caste marriage; decline of some rituals.
  • Counter-signs: festivals and pilgrimages are larger than ever; television epics; new gurus and temples; religion very visible in politics.
  • So India shows change in religion more than simple decline.
  • Orthodox view: history runs in fixed cycles; this is the Kali Yuga, an age of foretold decline in dharma.
  • So orthodox Hindus can accept some secularisation as expected, not as a defeat, while keeping their own dharma.

Mission 4Evaluate

"India is a genuinely secular state."

  • For: no state religion; equal rights in the Constitution; courts strike down religious discrimination; minorities have held the highest offices.
  • Against (Hindutva critics): the state favours minorities (separate personal law) and controls Hindu temples but not mosques or churches: "pseudo-secular".
  • Against (minority and liberal critics): in practice Hindu majoritarianism is growing: cow-protection and anti-conversion laws; communal violence; exclusion of Dalit Christians and Muslims from SC status.
  • Another view: "secular" in India never meant separation; judged by equal respect, the question is whether respect is in fact equal.
  • Judge it by: which definition of secular? Constitution or practice? Which period?

Mission 5

Four aims of life

  • The purusharthas: "aims of a person", four goals a full human life may pursue.
  • Dharma: right living, duty, virtue.
  • Artha: wealth, security, success, power.
  • Kama: pleasure, love, beauty, enjoyment of the senses.
  • Moksha: liberation from samsara.
  • All four are legitimate. The question is how they fit together.
Mission 5

Artha: wealth and power

  • Earning a living, owning property, supporting a family, building a business, and for rulers, governing well.
  • Without artha, a household cannot give to the poor, support temples or pay for ritual.
  • The Arthashastra (attributed to Kautilya, compiled c. 2nd century BCE–3rd century CE): a manual of statecraft and economics; wealth as the basis of a stable kingdom.
  • Artha is good when gained and used within dharma: honestly, generously, without harm.
  • Your spec links the four aims to Hindu attitudes to wealth and poverty and to the Arthashastra (more in Mission 6).
Mission 5

How the four fit together

  • Trivarga: the first three, the "group of three" for life in the world.
  • Dharma frames artha and kama, like the banks of a river: they give the water its course.
  • Moksha goes beyond the three: it is freedom from the whole cycle in which they are pursued.
  • Two directions of life: pravritti (engagement with the world: dharma, artha, kama) and nivritti (withdrawal: moksha).
  • Most Hindus live engaged lives and turn more towards moksha with age.

Mission 6

Artha among the four aims

  • The purusharthas, four aims of human life (see Mission 5): dharma (righteousness, duty), artha (wealth, prosperity), kama (pleasure, love), moksha (liberation).
  • Artha is a legitimate aim, especially for the grihastha (householder), who supports family, guests, students and renunciants.
  • The rule: artha and kama are pursued within dharma, and all point towards moksha.
  • Wealth gained by adharma (cheating, harm) or hoarded without giving brings bad karma.
  • Dana (giving) is a duty of the householder.
  • So the Hindu attitude: neither "wealth is evil" nor "wealth is everything".

Mission 6

The Arthashastra

  • The Arthashastra ("treatise on artha"): a manual of statecraft and economics attributed to Kautilya (Chanakya), adviser to Chandragupta Maurya (c. 4th century BCE); the text as we have it was probably compiled later (c. 1st–3rd century CE).
  • Rediscovered in 1905; translated by R. Shamasastry (1915).
  • It treats artha as the base on which dharma and kama depend (1.7, paraphrase).
  • The king must provide for the vulnerable: orphans, the old, the sick, the helpless and women without support (2.1, paraphrase); famine relief from state stores (4.3).
  • The king's happiness lies in the happiness of his subjects (1.19, paraphrase).
  • Influence of the purusharthas: wealth is pursued for the welfare of all, under dharma.
  • Critics: it is also ruthless (spies, deception for the state); its welfare is from the top down.
Mission 6

Vinoba Bhave and the gift of land

  • Vinoba Bhave (1895–1982): Gandhi's disciple; first individual satyagrahi (1940); author of Talks on the Gita (given in prison, 1932).
  • Bhoodan ("land gift"), from 1951: he walked across India asking landowners to give land to the landless, asking each owner to treat him as one more son and give a son's share for the poor.
  • Gramdan ("village gift"): whole villages pooling land.
  • Over four million acres were pledged; much less was actually given and farmed.
  • Aim: Sarvodaya (the uplift of all) by changing hearts, not by force or state seizure.
  • Honours: Ramon Magsaysay Award (1958, its first year); Bharat Ratna (1983, after his death).
Mission 6

Practical Vedanta: serving God in the poor

  • Swami Vivekananda (1863–1902) reinterpreted Advaita Vedanta for action.
  • If Brahman is in every being, serving people is serving God: daridra narayana, "God in the poor".
  • From his teacher Ramakrishna: serve living beings as Shiva (shiva jnane jiva seva).
  • Ramakrishna Mission (founded 1897): hospitals, schools, disaster relief, rural development, for all faiths and castes.
  • Motto: for one's own liberation and the welfare of the world.

  • For: Rig Veda 10.117's moving wealth speaks to inequality; contentment (Manu 4.12) answers consumerism.
  • Against: voluntary giving (Bhoodan) changed less than land reform by law.
  • Against: karma has been used by some to blame the poor for their poverty.
  • Judgement criteria: practical for whom: individuals, or a whole economy? Does it change structures or only hearts?
  • Spec issues: "relevance and practicality of the Hindu attitude to wealth and poverty"; "the extent of Vinoba Bhave's influence on Hindu attitudes to social welfare".

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • ISKCON (1966) brings Chaitanya's Gaudiya Vaishnava bhakti to the world: Krishna is the supreme person; chant his names.
  • Practice: four regulative principles, 16 rounds of japa, temple worship, prasadam; growth came from Prabhupada, the counterculture and the Indian diaspora.

Mission 2

  • Science tests religion by its method, its discoveries and its new ethical powers; Hindus respond in many ways.
  • Compatibility (Radhakrishnan; cyclical cosmology and the Big Bang), revelation claims (science "in the Vedas") and rejection (ISKCON's devolution against Darwin) are three distinct responses.
  • Indian astronomy and medicine (Aryabhata, Charaka, Ayurveda) and Samkhya's classification of matter show science within the tradition.

Mission 3

  • Savarkar (1923): a Hindu treats India as both fatherland and holy land; the Sangh Parivar (RSS, VHP, BJP) builds on this.
  • Hindus are divided: supporters see cultural pride and fair treatment; critics see exclusion of minorities and a politicised Hinduism.

Mission 4

  • India's secularism is "principled distance": equal respect and state engagement, not separation.
  • Secularisation in India means change more than decline; orthodox Hindus may see it as part of the Kali Yuga.

Mission 5

  • Four purusharthas: dharma, artha, kama, moksha; dharma frames artha and kama; moksha goes beyond all three.

Mission 6

  • Artha (wealth) is one of the four aims of life: legitimate within dharma, shared through dana.
  • Manu 4.12 and 4.15 teach contentment and right means; Rig Veda 10.117 makes feeding the poor a duty; the Arthashastra sets the king's welfare duties; Vinoba Bhave's Bhoodan sought land for the landless by gift.
