Mission 1

Mission 1: Why can a heater burning in a closed tent kill, even with fuel to spare?

Make a guess: Why can a heater burning in a closed tent kill, even with fuel to spare?

Mission 1

Alkanes as fuels: complete combustion

  • Alkanes burn in oxygen, releasing a lot of energy: they are fuels.
  • Complete combustion, in plenty of O2: all C → CO2, all H → H2O.
  • Balance C, then H, then O last.
  • Propane: C3H8(g) + 5O2(g) → 3CO2(g) + 4H2O(l).
  • Odd number of O atoms? Use ½O2, or double everything.
Mission 1

Background: elements burn too

  • Combustion is not only for fuels. Many elements burn in oxygen.
  • Metals form ionic oxides: 2Mg(s) + O2(g) → 2MgO(s); 4Al(s) + 3O2(g) → 2Al2O3(s).
  • Non-metals form covalent oxides: C(s) + O2(g) → CO2(g); S(s) + O2(g) → SO2(g).
  • Always write oxygen as O2.
  • Sulfur in a fuel burns to SO2, and in a hot engine N2 + O2 → 2NO. You'll meet these as pollutants.
Quick check
?When the equation for the complete combustion of octane, C8H18, is balanced with a coefficient of 2 for octane, what is the coefficient of O2?
Mission 1

Burning alcohols

  • Alcohols burn completely to CO2 and H2O, just like alkanes.
  • An alcohol already contains one O atom: subtract it before finding the O2.
  • Ethanol: C2H5OH(l) + 3O2(g) → 2CO2(g) + 3H2O(l).
  • Butan-1-ol: C4H9OH(l) + 6O2(g) → 4CO2(g) + 5H2O(l). Products have 13 O; 1 comes from the alcohol.
Quick check
?What amount of oxygen, O2, is needed to burn 0.500 mol of propan-1-ol, C3H7OH, completely?
Mission 1

Incomplete combustion

  • In limited O2, some carbon forms carbon monoxide, CO, or carbon (soot), C. Hydrogen still forms H2O.
  • To CO: 2C3H8(g) + 7O2(g) → 6CO(g) + 8H2O(l). To C: C3H8(g) + 2O2(g) → 3C(s) + 4H2O(l).
  • Less energy is released than in complete combustion.
  • Glowing soot particles make a yellow, smoky flame. Plenty of air: a blue flame.
  • The higher a fuel's carbon content, the smokier its flame: methane (75% C by mass) burns cleaner than octane (84% C).
  • In a spirit-burner experiment, soot on the can means incomplete combustion: your ΔcH comes out less exothermic than the true value.
Mission 1

Why carbon monoxide kills

  • CO is colourless, odourless and toxic.
  • It binds to haemoglobin in red blood cells far more strongly than O2 does.
  • So the blood carries less oxygen to the body's tissues.
  • You cannot see or smell it: that is why CO alarms save lives.
  • Soot (carbon particulates) also harms: it damages the lungs.
Quick check
?Which equation represents an incomplete combustion of ethanol?
Mission 1

Other pollutants from burning fuels

  • Carbon monoxide and carbon particulates: from incomplete combustion.
  • Unburned hydrocarbons: fuel that escapes burning.
  • Oxides of nitrogen, NOx: N2 and O2 from the air react at the high temperature in an engine. N2 + O2 → 2NO; then 2NO + O2 → 2NO2.
  • Sulfur dioxide, SO2: from sulfur impurities in the fuel. S + O2 → SO2.
  • SO2 causes air pollution and acid rain.
Mission 1

Biofuels: the carbon-neutral claim

  • A biofuel is a fuel made from recently living material (biomass).
  • Bioethanol: sugars are fermented by yeast, then the ethanol is separated by fractional distillation.
  • Photosynthesis: 6CO2 + 6H2O → C6H12O6 + 6O2.
  • Fermentation: C6H12O6 → 2C2H5OH + 2CO2.
  • Combustion: 2C2H5OH + 6O2 → 4CO2 + 6H2O.
  • 6CO2 taken in; 2 + 4 = 6CO2 given out. So the claim: carbon neutral.
Mission 1

Why the claim fails; other issues

  • Not carbon neutral: fossil fuels power the tractors, make the fertiliser, transport the crop and heat the distillation.
  • For: renewable, as crops regrow in months; their carbon came recently from the air. Fossil fuels are finite.
  • Against: land for fuel crops cannot grow food: higher food prices, an ethical issue.
  • Against: clearing forest for crops releases stored carbon and destroys habitats.
Quick check
?Bioethanol is made by fermenting sugar from sugar cane and is burned as a fuel for vehicles. Which statement about bioethanol as a fuel is correct?
Mission check

?After cooking outdoors, campers carry a still-glowing charcoal barbecue into their closed tent for warmth. Charcoal is almost pure carbon. Why is this deadly, even though there is no flame?

Same chemistry as the heater. Too little O2 turns carbon into CO, and CO stops your blood carrying oxygen.

Make a guess: What does acid do to an alcohol that lets its OH group break away?

Mission 2

Alcohols from alkenes and steam

  • Hydration: water adds across C=C.
  • C2H4(g) + H2O(g) ⇌ C2H5OH(g)
  • Catalyst: phosphoric acid, H3PO4, on a solid support.
  • Typical industrial conditions: about 300 °C, about 6–7 MPa.
  • Only about 5% converts per pass; unreacted ethene is recycled.
  • Unsymmetrical alkenes give two alcohols: propene → mainly propan-2-ol.
  • Continuous process; very pure ethanol; but ethene comes from crude oil (finite).
Mission 2

Hydration mechanism

  • Step 1: curly arrow from the C=C π bond to H⁺ (from the acid). A carbocation forms.
  • Step 2: curly arrow from a lone pair on the O of H2O to the C⁺.
  • Step 3: curly arrow from the O–H bond to the O⁺. H⁺ is released.
  • H⁺ is used, then given back: it is a catalyst.
Mission 2

Fermentation

  • C6H12O6(aq) → 2C2H5OH(aq) + 2CO2(g)
  • Yeast (its enzymes) in aqueous glucose.
  • About 35 °C (30–40 °C): colder is too slow; hotter denatures the enzymes.
  • Air kept out (anaerobic): oxygen would let the ethanol be oxidised to ethanoic acid.
  • Stops at about 15% ethanol: higher concentrations kill the yeast.
  • Separate the ethanol by fractional distillation.
  • Slow batch process, but the raw material is renewable.
Quick check
?Ethanol is made either by fermentation, C6H12O6(aq) → 2C2H5OH(aq) + 2CO2(g), or by hydration of ethene, C2H4(g) + H2O(g) → C2H5OH(g). Which statement about the two routes is correct? Ar: H 1.0; C 12.0; O 16.0
Mission 2

Biofuels and the carbon-neutral claim

  • Biofuel: a fuel made from biological material that has grown recently (biomass).
  • Photosynthesis: 6CO2 + 6H2O → C6H12O6 + 6O2 (6CO2 taken in)
  • Fermentation: C6H12O6 → 2C2H5OH + 2CO2 (2CO2 out)
  • Combustion: 2C2H5OH + 6O2 → 4CO2 + 6H2O (4CO2 out)
  • On paper: 6CO2 in, 6CO2 out. So it is called carbon neutral.
Mission 2

Why the claim fails

  • Fossil fuels are burned to farm, make fertiliser, transport and distil the ethanol.
  • So net CO2 is lower than for petrol, but not zero.
  • Land for fuel crops can push up food prices (food vs fuel).
  • Deforestation for crops releases stored carbon and destroys habitats.
Quick check
?A fuel company says its bioethanol, made by fermenting sugar from sugar cane, is "carbon neutral". Which statement best evaluates the claim?
Mission 2

Why acid lets the OH go

  • OH⁻ is a strong base: it is a poor leaving group. The C–O bond will not break on its own.
  • H⁺ bonds to a lone pair on the O: –OH becomes –OH2⁺.
  • Now the C–O bond breaks and a neutral, stable H2O molecule leaves.
  • Then either:
  • a neighbouring C loses H⁺ → alkene (elimination)
  • a halide ion bonds to the C → halogenoalkane (substitution)
Mission 2

Dehydration: alcohol → alkene

  • Heat the alcohol with concentrated H3PO4 (or conc H2SO4).
  • C2H5OH → CH2=CH2 + H2O
  • Cyclohexanol → cyclohexene + H2O: the alkene distils off as it forms.
  • If the OH carbon has different neighbours, several alkenes can form, including E/Z isomers.
  • Ethene from bioethanol → poly(ethene) without crude oil.
Mission 2

Dehydration mechanism

  • Step 1: curly arrow from a lone pair on the O to H⁺. –OH2⁺ forms.
  • Step 2: curly arrow from the C–O bond to the O⁺. Water leaves; a carbocation forms.
  • Step 3: curly arrow from a C–H bond on the neighbouring carbon to the C–C bond. H⁺ is lost; C=C forms.
  • H⁺ is regenerated: a catalyst.
Quick check
?Butan-2-ol, CH3CH(OH)CH2CH3, is heated with concentrated phosphoric acid. How many different alkenes can form?
Mission check

?Two reactions both need acid:
(1) cyclohexanol, heated with concentrated phosphoric acid → cyclohexene + water
(2) 2-methylpropan-2-ol, shaken with concentrated hydrochloric acid → 2-chloro-2-methylpropane + water
What does the H⁺ do in both reactions?

In both reactions the acid turns –OH into –OH2⁺. Water leaves, and what happens next decides the product.

Mission 3

Primary, secondary, tertiary alcohols

  • Find the carbon bonded to the –OH. Count the carbons attached to it.
  • Primary: one (methanol: none). Ethanol, CH3CH2OH.
  • Secondary: two. Propan-2-ol, CH3CH(OH)CH3.
  • Tertiary: three. 2-methylpropan-2-ol, (CH3)3COH.
  • Primary and secondary alcohols have an H on the C–OH carbon; tertiary alcohols have none.
Quick check
?Which of these is a secondary alcohol?
Mission 3

The oxidising agent

  • Acidified potassium dichromate(VI): K2Cr2O7 in dilute H2SO4.
  • Cr2O7²⁻ is orange. As it oxidises the alcohol it is reduced to Cr³⁺, which is green.
  • In equations, write the oxidising agent as [O].
  • The alcohol loses two H atoms: one from the O–H, one from the C–OH carbon. C=O forms.
  • No H on the C–OH carbon (tertiary) → no oxidation.
Mission 3

What oxidation gives

  • Primary → aldehyde: CH3CH2OH + [O] → CH3CHO + H2O (ethanal).
  • The aldehyde → carboxylic acid: CH3CHO + [O] → CH3COOH (ethanoic acid).
  • Overall: CH3CH2OH + 2[O] → CH3COOH + H2O.
  • Secondary → ketone: CH3CH(OH)CH3 + [O] → CH3COCH3 + H2O (propanone). It stops there.
  • Tertiary: not oxidised. The dichromate stays orange.
Mission 3

Distil or reflux?

  • Boiling points: ethanal 20 °C, ethanol 78 °C, ethanoic acid 118 °C. The aldehyde has no O–H, so no hydrogen bonds between its molecules.
  • Aldehyde: alcohol in excess; warm, and distil the aldehyde off as it forms, so it is not oxidised further. Cool the receiver in ice.
  • Acid: excess oxidising agent; heat under reflux. The condenser is vertical and open at the top, so the aldehyde returns and is oxidised. Then distil off the acid.
  • Ketone: heat under reflux, then distil. It cannot be oxidised further.
  • Use anti-bumping granules; heat with a water bath or electric heater, not a flame.
Quick check
?Propan-1-ol is heated with an acidified oxidising agent in apparatus set up for distillation, and the distillate is collected. What is the main organic product collected, and why?
Quick check
?Pentan-3-ol is heated under reflux with excess acidified potassium dichromate(VI). Which equation is correct?
Mission 3

The dichromate test

  • Warm a few drops with acidified potassium dichromate(VI) in a water bath.
  • Orange → green: a primary or secondary alcohol (or an aldehyde).
  • Stays orange: a tertiary alcohol (or a ketone).
  • To tell an aldehyde from a ketone, use Tollens' reagent or Fehling's solution.
  • Early roadside breath tubes used this test: orange crystals turned green with ethanol on the breath.
Quick check
?Three colourless liquids, X, Y and Z, are butan-1-ol, butan-2-ol and 2-methylpropan-2-ol, in some order. Each is warmed with acidified potassium dichromate(VI). X and Y turn the mixture green; Z leaves it orange. Which conclusion is correct?
Mission 3

Background: adding hydrogen

  • Reduction is the reverse: hydrogen is added. [H] stands for the reducing agent.
  • Aldehyde + 2[H] → primary alcohol: CH3CHO + 2[H] → CH3CH2OH.
  • Ketone + 2[H] → secondary alcohol: CH3COCH3 + 2[H] → CH3CH(OH)CH3.
  • Reducing agents such as NaBH4 do this. LiAlH4 also reduces acids: CH3COOH + 4[H] → CH3CH2OH + H2O.
  • Hydrogenation: H2 adds across C=C over a nickel catalyst. A C≡C triple bond takes two H2.
Mission check

?A few drops of cyclohexanol, a six-carbon ring with an –OH on one ring carbon, are warmed with acidified potassium dichromate(VI). What do you see, and why?

A breath tester works the same way. Ethanol is oxidised. Old tubes showed it as dichromate turning green; most modern devices oxidise ethanol in a fuel cell and measure the current.

Mission 4

Mission 4: A reaction leaves a cloudy mix of product, leftovers and water. How do chemists get the product out pure?

Make a guess: A reaction leaves a cloudy mix of product, leftovers and water. How do chemists get the product out pure?

Mission 4

Heating under reflux

  • Use it for slow reactions with volatile liquids: heat for a long time, lose nothing.
  • Quickfit glassware: ground-glass joints fit together without bungs.
  • Round-bottomed flask + anti-bumping granules (smooth boiling).
  • Condenser vertical: vapour condenses and drips back into the flask.
  • Water in at the bottom, out at the top: the jacket stays full.
  • Top left open. Never heat a sealed apparatus.
  • Heat with an electric heater, water bath or sand bath: organic liquids are flammable.
Mission 4

Distillation

  • Separates liquids by boiling point.
  • Flask + still head + thermometer bulb level with the side arm: it reads the vapour that is distilling.
  • Condenser sloping down, water in at the lower end.
  • Collect the fraction that distils within a degree or two of the product's boiling point.
  • Cool the receiver in ice for a very volatile product.
Mission 4

Reflux or distil? Oxidising ethanol

  • Ethanol + acidified potassium dichromate(VI). Boiling points: ethanal 20 °C, ethanol 78 °C, ethanoic acid 118 °C.
  • Want the aldehyde? Add the oxidant slowly to warm ethanol and distil off ethanal as it forms.
  • It leaves before it can be oxidised further.
  • Want the carboxylic acid? Heat under reflux with excess oxidant. Ethanal can't escape, so it is oxidised to the acid.
  • Then distil to collect the acid.
Quick check
?A student wants to make propanal from propan-1-ol using acidified potassium dichromate(VI). Which method gives the most propanal?
Mission 4

Separating funnel and washing

  • Two liquids that don't mix form layers. The denser layer is at the bottom.
  • Not sure which is which? Add a little water and see which layer grows.
  • Washing: shake the crude product with an aqueous solution, then separate.
  • Water removes water-soluble impurities. Sodium hydrogencarbonate solution removes acid.
  • Stopper, invert, open the tap often to release pressure (NaHCO3 + acid gives CO2).
  • Run off the lower layer through the tap. Keep the layer that holds your product.
Mission 4

Drying and redistillation

  • The washed organic layer is cloudy: it holds tiny drops of water.
  • Add an anhydrous salt: MgSO4, CaCl2 or Na2SO4. Swirl.
  • Keep adding until the salt no longer clumps and the liquid is clear.
  • Decant or filter off the drying agent.
  • Redistil, collecting only near the product's boiling point.
Mission 4

Worked example: 2-chloro-2-methylpropane

  • Shake 2-methylpropan-2-ol with concentrated HCl in a separating funnel (fast at room temperature: no reflux).
  • Two layers form. The product (density 0.84 g cm⁻³) is the upper layer. Discard the lower aqueous layer.
  • Wash with NaHCO3(aq), venting often, to remove acid. Then wash with water.
  • Dry with anhydrous CaCl2 or Na2SO4 until clear.
  • Distil, collecting at about 50–52 °C (bp 51 °C).
Quick check
?A student shakes a crude organic product with sodium hydrogencarbonate solution in a separating funnel. Why must the tap be opened often?
Mission 4

Purifying a solid: recrystallisation

  • Choose a solvent the product dissolves in well when hot, poorly when cold.
  • Dissolve the crude solid in the minimum volume of hot solvent.
  • Filter while hot to remove insoluble impurities.
  • Cool slowly: the product crystallises; soluble impurities, present in small amounts, stay in solution.
  • Filter under reduced pressure (Büchner funnel), wash with a little cold solvent, dry.
Mission 4

Melting point: is it pure?

  • Melting point apparatus: a little solid in a capillary tube, heated slowly near the melting point.
  • Pure: melts sharply (within about 1 °C), at the data-book value. Aspirin: 136 °C.
  • Impure: melts lower and over a wider range.
  • RP10: a melting point is your test of purity for the solid.
Quick check
?Pure aspirin melts at 136 °C. A student's recrystallised aspirin melts over the range 126–131 °C. What does this show?
Mission check

?A student makes 1-bromobutane (bp 102 °C, density 1.27 g cm⁻³) from butan-1-ol (bp 118 °C). The crude product contains water, acid and some unreacted alcohol. Which purification is best?

Each step uses a different property: density (separate), reactivity (wash), water uptake (dry), boiling point (distil). Then a melting or boiling point proves purity.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Plenty of O2: C → CO2, H → H2O. Limited O2: CO or C (soot), and a yellow, smoky flame.
  • CO is toxic: it binds to haemoglobin, so blood carries less O2.
  • Biofuels are renewable, but not carbon neutral: making them burns fossil fuels.

Mission 2

  • Acid protonates –OH; water leaves; then lose H⁺ (alkene) or add a halide (halogenoalkane).
  • Ethanol: steam hydration of ethene (fast, pure, finite) or fermentation (slow, renewable, not truly carbon neutral).

Mission 3

  • Primary → aldehyde (distil) → carboxylic acid (reflux); secondary → ketone; tertiary: no oxidation.
  • Acidified dichromate(VI) turns orange → green with primary and secondary alcohols (and aldehydes).

Mission 4

  • Reflux: heat without loss. Distil: separate by boiling point. Separating funnel: by density. Recrystallise: by solubility, hot v cold.
  • Pure solid: sharp melting point at the data value. Impure: lower and broader.
  • Your practicals: RP5 (distillation of a product) and RP10 (a pure organic solid and liquid).
