Mission 1

Mission 1: Blow an atom up to the size of a stadium. How big is the nucleus at its heart?

Make a guess: Blow an atom up to the size of a stadium. How big is the nucleus at its heart?

Mission 1

The plum-pudding model

  • 1897: Thomson discovers the electron. Atoms must contain positive charge too.
  • Plum-pudding model: positive charge spread evenly through the whole atom, electrons dotted inside.
  • Charge spread out → only weak electric fields anywhere in the atom.
  • Prediction: fast alpha particles pass through with only tiny deflections.
Mission 1

The experiment

  • Geiger and Marsden, in Rutherford's Manchester laboratory (1909–1913).
  • A narrow beam of alpha particles aimed at a very thin gold foil.
  • In a vacuum, so air does not stop the alpha particles.
  • A zinc sulfide screen flashes where an alpha particle hits. Counted through a microscope at many angles.
Quick check
?Predict first. Which statement about the results of the Geiger–Marsden–Rutherford alpha-particle scattering experiment is correct?
Mission 1

Results and conclusions

  • Almost all went straight through → the atom is mostly empty space.
  • Some were deflected through small angles → they are repelled by positive charge (plum pudding allows this too).
  • A very few were deflected by more than 90° → the positive charge and nearly all the mass are concentrated in a tiny core. Plum pudding cannot do this.
  • Rutherford (1911): the nuclear model. A small, dense, positive nucleus.
  • Bouncing back needs a strong electric repulsion from something much more massive than an alpha. Electrons are far too light.
Mission 1

The nuclear atom

  • Nucleus: protons and neutrons. Positive. Tiny.
  • Electrons around it, held by the electric attraction of the nucleus.
  • A proton or neutron has about 1840 times the mass of an electron.
  • So over 99.9% of an atom's mass is in the nucleus.
  • (The neutron was discovered in 1932.)
Mission 1

How small is the nucleus?

  • Atom: about 10⁻¹⁰ m across.
  • Nucleus: about 10⁻¹⁵ to 10⁻¹⁴ m across.
  • Atom ÷ nucleus: roughly 10⁴ to 10⁵ times.
  • Stadium-sized atom (about 200 m) → nucleus a few millimetres: about a pea on the centre spot.
  • Almost all the volume is empty; almost all the mass is in the pea.
Quick check
?A gold nucleus has radius 7.0 × 10⁻¹⁵ m and a gold atom has radius 1.4 × 10⁻¹⁰ m. Roughly what fraction of the atom's volume does the nucleus occupy?
Mission 1

Distance of closest approach

  • Head-on: the alpha slows, stops for an instant at distance d, then returns.
  • At d, all its kinetic energy has become electric potential energy.
  • Eₖ = kqQ/d, with q = 2e and Q = Ze.
  • Gold (Z = 79), Eₖ = 7.7 MeV = 7.7 × 10⁶ × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 1.23 × 10⁻¹² J.
  • d = 8.99 × 10⁹ × 2 × 79 × (1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹)² ÷ 1.23 × 10⁻¹² = 3.0 × 10⁻¹⁴ m.
  • The alpha never touches the nucleus, so d is an upper limit for its radius. Nuclear recoil ignored.
Quick check
?Alpha particles of kinetic energy Ek are fired head-on at gold-197 nuclei (proton number 79), and their distance of closest approach is d. Alpha particles of kinetic energy 2Ek are then fired head-on at silver-107 nuclei (proton number 47). In both cases only the electric repulsion acts and nuclear recoil is negligible. What is the new distance of closest approach?
Mission 1

When the prediction fails

  • Rutherford's prediction assumes only the electric force acts.
  • It fits at low energies. Above a few tens of MeV, fewer alphas scatter through large angles than predicted.
  • Reason: the alpha now gets close enough to feel the short-range strong nuclear force.
  • The energy where this starts → its closest approach ≈ the nuclear radius.
Mission check

?A gold nucleus is about 1.4 × 10⁻¹⁴ m across and a gold atom is about 2.9 × 10⁻¹⁰ m across. In a scale drawing the nucleus is a dot 1 mm across. How wide must the atom be drawn?

The nucleus is about 10⁴ to 10⁵ times smaller than the atom: a pea in a stadium, holding nearly all the mass.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Two atoms of the same element can have different masses. What is different inside them?

Make a guess: Two atoms of the same element can have different masses. What is different inside them?

Mission 2

Nuclear notation

  • Nucleus: protons (charge +e) and neutrons (no charge). Together: nucleons.
  • ᴬZ X. Example: ⁵⁶26Fe.
  • Z = proton number (atomic number): 26 protons.
  • A = nucleon number (mass number): 56 nucleons.
  • Neutrons: N = A − Z = 56 − 26 = 30.
  • Z fixes the element. Every iron nucleus has 26 protons.
  • You will be given chemical symbols: no need to learn them.
Quick check
?A nucleus of potassium-40 is written in nuclear notation as ⁴⁰19K. Which statement about this nucleus is correct?
Quick check
?Predict first. Carbon-12 (¹²6C) and carbon-14 (¹⁴6C) are isotopes of carbon. Which statement about these two nuclides is correct?
Mission 2

Isotopes

  • Isotopes: same proton number, different neutron number.
  • Same element. Same number of electrons, so the same chemistry.
  • Different mass: extra neutrons add mass.
  • Carbon: ¹²6C (6 neutrons), ¹³6C (7), ¹⁴6C (8).
  • Some isotopes are unstable: carbon-14 is radioactive; carbon-12 is not.
Mission 2

What holds the nucleus together?

  • Protons repel electrically. Two protons 1 × 10⁻¹⁵ m apart: about 230 N.
  • Gravity between nucleons is negligible.
  • The strong nuclear force acts between all nucleons: p–p, n–n, p–n.
  • It is attractive at nuclear separations and stronger than the electric repulsion there.
Quick check
?Predict first. Nucleons in a nucleus are about 10⁻¹⁵ m apart, while the nuclei of neighbouring atoms in a solid are about 10⁻¹⁰ m apart. Why does the strong nuclear force bind the nucleons within each nucleus but have no effect between neighbouring nuclei?
Mission 2

A very short range

  • The strong force is very short-range: negligible beyond a few × 10⁻¹⁵ m.
  • Nuclei of neighbouring atoms, about 10⁻¹⁰ m apart, do not feel it.
  • So it holds each nucleus together but does not bind one nucleus to the next.
Quick check
?Predict first. The deuteron, ²1H, is a stable nucleus made of one proton and one neutron; it takes 2.2 MeV to pull them apart. The neutron carries no electric charge. Which conclusion do these facts support?
Mission 2

Evidence for the strong force

  • Stable nuclei exist. In helium-4 the two protons are about 2 × 10⁻¹⁵ m apart: repulsion about 58 N. Something attracts more strongly.
  • The deuteron (²1H: one proton, one neutron) is bound. The neutron is uncharged, so the attraction is not electric.
  • Gravity is about 10³⁶ times too weak to do this.
  • High-energy scattering: α particles that get within a few × 10⁻¹⁵ m of a nucleus no longer follow the Rutherford (electric-only) pattern. A second, short-range force acts.
Mission check

?Which pair are isotopes of the same element?

Same element means the same number of protons. Atoms of one element differ in mass because their nuclei hold different numbers of neutrons.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why can a few minutes of weak ultraviolet damage skin cells' DNA when hours of bright red light can't?

Make a guess: Why can a few minutes of weak ultraviolet damage skin cells' DNA when hours of bright red light can't?

Mission 3

Light comes in packets: photons

  • Electromagnetic radiation is emitted and absorbed in discrete packets of energy: photons.
  • A photon is a quantum of electromagnetic energy.
  • Energy of one photon: E = hf = hc/λ.
  • h = Planck constant = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s.
  • Higher frequency (shorter wavelength) → more energy per photon.
  • A brighter beam of the same colour has more photons, not bigger ones.
Mission 3

The electronvolt

  • Photon energies in joules are tiny. Use the electronvolt.
  • 1 eV: the energy gained by an electron accelerated through a potential difference of 1 V.
  • 1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. J → eV: divide by 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹.
  • Example, blue light, λ = 450 nm:
    • E = hc/λ = (6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.00 × 10⁸)/(450 × 10⁻⁹) = 4.42 × 10⁻¹⁹ J
    • = 4.42 × 10⁻¹⁹/1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 2.76 eV.
Quick check
?Predict first. Which observation from photoelectric experiments cannot be explained by the classical wave theory of light?
Mission 3

The photoelectric effect

Shine electromagnetic radiation on a clean metal surface. Electrons may be emitted: photoelectrons.

  • Below a threshold frequency f0: no emission, however intense the light.
  • Above f0: emission immediately, even in very dim light.
  • Maximum kinetic energy of the electrons rises with frequency; it does not depend on intensity.
  • Number emitted per second is proportional to intensity (above f0).
  • f0 depends on the metal (e.g. zinc needs ultraviolet).
Mission 3

Einstein's explanation

  • One photon gives all its energy to one electron: a one-to-one interaction.
  • Work function Φ: the minimum energy needed to remove an electron from the metal's surface.
  • If hf < Φ: no electron can escape. Threshold: hf0 = Φ.
  • If hf is bigger than the work function, the extra energy becomes kinetic energy. Electrons from just below the surface lose some on the way out, so this is a maximum.
  • Emax = hf − Φ
Mission 3

Why waves can't explain it

Wave theory: energy arrives spread over the wavefront and builds up with intensity and time. It predicts:

  • Any frequency should work, given bright enough light. ✗ Observed: a sharp threshold f0.
  • Dim light should cause a delay while energy builds up. ✗ Observed: instant emission.
  • Brighter light should give faster electrons. ✗ Observed: the maximum kinetic energy is set by f alone.
  • Photon model explains all three: energy comes in lumps of hf, one lump per electron.
  • So the photoelectric effect is evidence for the particle (photon) nature of light.
Mission 3

Worked example

Light of wavelength 400 nm falls on potassium, work function 2.30 eV.

  • Photon energy: hf = hc/λ = (6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.00 × 10⁸)/(400 × 10⁻⁹) = 4.97 × 10⁻¹⁹ J = 3.11 eV.
  • Maximum kinetic energy = 3.11 − 2.30 = 0.81 eV = 1.29 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.
  • Threshold frequency: f0 = (work function)/h = (2.30 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹)/(6.63 × 10⁻³⁴) = 5.55 × 10¹⁴ Hz.
Quick check
?Light whose photons each have energy 4.0 eV shines on three metals: X, with work function 2.0 eV; Y, with work function 3.0 eV; and Z, with work function 4.5 eV. Which statement about the maximum kinetic energy of the electrons emitted from each metal is correct?
Mission check

?Ultraviolet light above the threshold frequency shines on a metal plate and electrons are emitted. The ultraviolet is replaced by ultraviolet of a shorter wavelength, with the same intensity (the same power per square metre). What happens?

Energy per photon is set by the frequency; the number of photons by the power. The same holds in skin: each ultraviolet photon can damage a molecule on its own; red photons cannot, however many arrive.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Why does a neon sign glow in a few sharp shades instead of a full rainbow?

Make a guess: Why does a neon sign glow in a few sharp shades instead of a full rainbow?

Mission 4

Energy levels

  • An electron bound in an atom can have only certain energies: energy levels.
  • Lowest level: the ground state (n = 1). Higher levels: excited states.
  • Zero energy = electron just free of the atom (ionised).
  • So every bound level is negative: energy must be supplied to free the electron.
  • Hydrogen: −13.6 eV, −3.40 eV, −1.51 eV, −0.85 eV, … crowding towards 0.
  • 1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. Levels may be given in eV or J.
Mission 4

Emitting a photon

  • An electron drops from a higher level to a lower one. The atom emits one photon.
  • Photon energy = energy lost: hf = ΔE.
  • With c = fλ: λ = hc/ΔE.
  • Bigger drop → higher frequency → shorter wavelength.
  • Each transition gives one frequency: one spectral line.
Mission 4

Worked example

  • Hydrogen: n = 2 (−3.40 eV) → n = 1 (−13.6 eV).
  • ΔE = −3.40 − (−13.6) = 10.2 eV.
  • In joules: 10.2 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ = 1.63 × 10⁻¹⁸ J.
  • f = ΔE/h = 1.632 × 10⁻¹⁸ ÷ 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ = 2.46 × 10¹⁵ Hz.
  • λ = c/f = 1.22 × 10⁻⁷ m (122 nm, ultraviolet).
  • Always use the difference between levels, never one level's value.
Quick check
?A hypothetical atom has only four energy levels below its ionisation energy: −8.00 eV, −5.00 eV, −3.50 eV and −1.00 eV. In a hot gas of these atoms, collisions put atoms into all four levels, and none of the atoms is ionised. What is the longest wavelength of the photons that the gas emits in transitions among these levels? Take h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹ and 1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.
Mission 4

How many lines?

  • An excited electron can drop straight down or in steps.
  • Every pair of levels gives one possible line.
  • 3 levels → 3 lines: 3→2, 3→1, 2→1.
  • Different gaps → different wavelengths. The largest gap gives the shortest wavelength.
Quick check
?An atom has only four energy levels: −1.50 eV, −2.50 eV, −4.00 eV and −6.00 eV. What is the energy of the emitted photon that has the SHORTEST wavelength?
Mission 4

Absorbing a photon; ionisation

  • A photon is absorbed only if its energy exactly equals a gap from the electron's level. All or nothing.
  • Any other photon passes straight through.
  • Ionisation energy: energy to free the electron from the ground state = 0 − E1. Hydrogen: 13.6 eV (2.18 × 10⁻¹⁸ J).
  • Any photon with at least this energy can ionise; the extra becomes kinetic energy of the freed electron.
  • From an excited level, less is needed: the ground state needs the most.
Mission 4

Three kinds of spectrum

  • Continuous: all wavelengths. Hot, dense source: a lamp filament, a star's surface.
  • Emission line: bright lines on a dark background. Hot gas at low pressure.
  • Absorption line: a continuous spectrum crossed by dark lines. White light through a cooler gas.
  • The dark lines sit at the same wavelengths as that gas's emission lines.
  • They look dark because the absorbed energy is re-emitted in all directions, so little continues forward.
Quick check
?Predict first. Two discharge tubes, P and Q, each contain a single gas. The emission spectrum of each gas shows exactly four bright lines in the visible region, but none of the wavelengths of the lines from P matches any of the wavelengths of the lines from Q. A student claims that P and Q contain the same element. Which statement is correct?
Mission 4

Evidence and fingerprints

  • Sharp lines, not a smear: atoms change energy only by fixed amounts. Evidence for discrete levels.
  • Each element has its own set of levels, so its own set of lines.
  • Match the lines with laboratory spectra → identify the element.
  • Works in a lamp, a gas cloud or a star's outer layers.
  • Helium was discovered in 1868 from a yellow line in the Sun's spectrum that matched no known element.
Mission check

?An atom has energy levels at −10.0 eV (the ground state), −5.0 eV and −3.0 eV. An atom in its ground state is struck by a photon of energy 3.0 eV. What happens?

An atom can only take in or give out energy equal to a gap between its levels. Neon's gaps are fixed, so its photons, and its colours, are too.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Alpha scattering: most pass straight through, a very few bounce back → a tiny, dense, positive nucleus.
  • The nucleus (protons and neutrons) holds over 99.9% of the mass and is 10⁴–10⁵ times smaller than the atom.
  • Closest approach: Eₖ = electric PE at d; d is an upper limit for the nuclear radius.

Mission 2

  • Nucleus: protons + neutrons (nucleons). ᴬZ X: Z protons, A nucleons, A − Z neutrons.
  • Isotopes: same Z, different numbers of neutrons; same chemistry, different mass.
  • The strong nuclear force: attractive between all nucleons, very short-range.
  • Evidence: stable nuclei despite proton repulsion; the bound deuteron; high-energy scattering.

Mission 3

  • Photon energy E = hf = hc/λ; 1 eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ J. Intensity sets the number of photons, frequency the energy of each.
  • One photon frees at most one electron; threshold hf0 = Φ; Emax = hf − Φ, independent of intensity. Evidence for photons.

Mission 4

  • Levels are negative (0 = ionised). A transition emits or absorbs one photon: hf = ΔE, λ = hc/ΔE.
  • Line spectra are evidence of discrete levels, and each element's lines identify it, in a lamp or a star.
