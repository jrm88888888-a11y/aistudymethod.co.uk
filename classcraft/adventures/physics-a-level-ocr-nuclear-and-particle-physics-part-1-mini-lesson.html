Mission 1

Mission 1: Blow an atom up to the size of a stadium. How big is the nucleus at its heart?

Make a guess: Blow an atom up to the size of a stadium. How big is the nucleus at its heart?

Mission 1

The plum-pudding model

  • 1897: Thomson discovers the electron. Atoms must contain positive charge too.
  • Plum-pudding model: positive charge spread evenly through the whole atom, electrons dotted inside.
  • Charge spread out → only weak electric fields anywhere in the atom.
  • Prediction: fast alpha particles pass through with only tiny deflections.
Mission 1

The experiment

  • Geiger and Marsden, in Rutherford's Manchester laboratory (1909–1913).
  • A narrow beam of alpha particles aimed at a very thin gold foil.
  • In a vacuum, so air does not stop the alpha particles.
  • A zinc sulfide screen flashes where an alpha particle hits. Counted through a microscope at many angles.
Quick check
?Predict first. Which statement about the results of the Geiger–Marsden–Rutherford alpha-particle scattering experiment is correct?
Mission 1

Results and conclusions

  • Almost all went straight through → the atom is mostly empty space.
  • Some were deflected through small angles → they are repelled by positive charge (plum pudding allows this too).
  • A very few were deflected by more than 90° → the positive charge and nearly all the mass are concentrated in a tiny core. Plum pudding cannot do this.
  • Rutherford (1911): the nuclear model. A small, dense, positive nucleus.
  • Bouncing back needs a strong electric repulsion from something much more massive than an alpha. Electrons are far too light.
Mission 1

The nuclear atom

  • Nucleus: protons and neutrons. Positive. Tiny.
  • Electrons around it, held by the electric attraction of the nucleus.
  • A proton or neutron has about 1840 times the mass of an electron.
  • So over 99.9% of an atom's mass is in the nucleus.
  • (The neutron was discovered in 1932.)
Mission 1

How small is the nucleus?

  • Atom: about 10⁻¹⁰ m across.
  • Nucleus: about 10⁻¹⁵ to 10⁻¹⁴ m across.
  • Atom ÷ nucleus: roughly 10⁴ to 10⁵ times.
  • Stadium-sized atom (about 200 m) → nucleus a few millimetres: about a pea on the centre spot.
  • Almost all the volume is empty; almost all the mass is in the pea.
Quick check
?A gold nucleus has radius 7.0 × 10⁻¹⁵ m and a gold atom has radius 1.4 × 10⁻¹⁰ m. Roughly what fraction of the atom's volume does the nucleus occupy?
Mission check

?A gold nucleus is about 1.4 × 10⁻¹⁴ m across and a gold atom is about 2.9 × 10⁻¹⁰ m across. In a scale drawing the nucleus is a dot 1 mm across. How wide must the atom be drawn?

The nucleus is about 10⁴ to 10⁵ times smaller than the atom: a pea in a stadium, holding nearly all the mass.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Two atoms of the same element can have different masses. What is different inside them?

Make a guess: Two atoms of the same element can have different masses. What is different inside them?

Mission 2

Nuclear notation

  • Nucleus: protons (charge +e) and neutrons (no charge). Together: nucleons.
  • ᴬZ X. Example: ⁵⁶26Fe.
  • Z = proton number (atomic number): 26 protons.
  • A = nucleon number (mass number): 56 nucleons.
  • Neutrons: N = A − Z = 56 − 26 = 30.
  • Z fixes the element. Every iron nucleus has 26 protons.
Quick check
?A nucleus of potassium-40 is written in nuclear notation as ⁴⁰19K. Which statement about this nucleus is correct?
Quick check
?Predict first. Carbon-12 (¹²6C) and carbon-14 (¹⁴6C) are isotopes of carbon. Which statement about these two nuclides is correct?
Mission 2

Isotopes

  • Isotopes: same proton number, different neutron number.
  • Same element. Same number of electrons, so the same chemistry.
  • Different mass: extra neutrons add mass.
  • Carbon: ¹²6C (6 neutrons), ¹³6C (7), ¹⁴6C (8).
  • Some isotopes are unstable: carbon-14 is radioactive; carbon-12 is not.
Mission 2

What holds the nucleus together?

  • Protons repel electrically. Two protons 1 × 10⁻¹⁵ m apart: about 230 N.
  • Gravity between nucleons is negligible.
  • The strong nuclear force acts between all nucleons: p–p, n–n, p–n.
  • It is attractive at nuclear separations and stronger than the electric repulsion there.
Quick check
?Predict first. Nucleons in a nucleus are about 10⁻¹⁵ m apart, while the nuclei of neighbouring atoms in a solid are about 10⁻¹⁰ m apart. Why does the strong nuclear force bind the nucleons within each nucleus but have no effect between neighbouring nuclei?
Mission 2

A very short range

  • The strong force is very short-range: negligible beyond a few × 10⁻¹⁵ m.
  • Nuclei of neighbouring atoms, about 10⁻¹⁰ m apart, do not feel it.
  • So it holds each nucleus together but does not bind one nucleus to the next.
  • Its full shape, including repulsion at very small separations, comes later.
Mission check

?Which pair are isotopes of the same element?

Same element means the same number of protons. Atoms of one element differ in mass because their nuclei hold different numbers of neutrons.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Protons in a nucleus repel each other. Why doesn't it fly apart, or collapse in on itself?

Make a guess: Protons in a nucleus repel each other. Why doesn't it fly apart, or collapse in on itself?

Quick check
?Predict first. In a helium-4 nucleus the two protons are about 2 × 10⁻¹⁵ m apart. At this separation the electric repulsion between them is about 58 N, an enormous force on particles of mass 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg. Yet helium-4 nuclei are stable. Also, all nuclei have nearly the same density: adding nucleons makes a nucleus bigger, not denser. What must be true of the strong force between nucleons?
Mission 3

The strong force against separation

  • Beyond about 3 fm: negligible. (1 fm = 10⁻¹⁵ m.)
  • From about 3 fm to 0.5 fm: attractive, strongest at about 1 fm.
  • Below about 0.5 fm: repulsive, and rises very steeply.
  • The force is zero at about 0.5 fm.
  • Same for p–p, n–n and p–n. Electrons do not feel it.
Mission 3

Why a nucleus is stable

  • Electric repulsion between protons is long-range: about 230 N at 1 fm, still 26 N at 3 fm.
  • At about 1 fm the strong attraction is larger: nucleons hold together. Not flying apart.
  • Squeezed below about 0.5 fm, the strong force repels. Not collapsing.
  • So nucleons are packed at a nearly fixed spacing (about 2 fm centre to centre), and nuclear density is roughly the same for all nuclei.
Mission check

?A uranium nucleus is about 15 fm across. Two protons on opposite sides of it are about 14 fm apart. Which forces do these two protons exert on each other?

Attraction between near neighbours stops the nucleus flying apart; repulsion below about 0.5 fm stops it collapsing. Far apart, only the electric repulsion remains.

Mission 4

Mission 4: How heavy would a teaspoon of pure nuclear matter be?

Make a guess: How heavy would a teaspoon of pure nuclear matter be?

Mission 4

Radius and nucleon number

  • Measured radii fit one rule:
  • R = r0A1/3, A = nucleon number, r0 ≈ 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ m.
  • Use the value of the constant given in the question.
  • From data: R against A1/3 is a straight line through the origin.
  • 8 times the nucleons → 2 times the radius. 27 times → 3 times.
  • Typical radii: about 2.7 fm (carbon-12) to about 7.4 fm (uranium-238). 1 fm = 10⁻¹⁵ m.
Mission 4

Worked example

  • Gold-197: A = 197.
  • A1/3 = 1971/3 = 5.82.
  • R = 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ × 5.82 = 7.0 × 10⁻¹⁵ m.
  • About 7 fm: roughly 20 000 times smaller than the gold atom.
Quick check
?A nucleus of lead-208 has proton number 82 and nucleon number 208. Using R = r0A1/3 with r0 = 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ m, what is the radius of this nucleus?
Quick check
?A carbon-12 nucleus contains 12 nucleons, each of mass 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg. r0 = 1.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ m. What is the density of the nucleus?
Mission 4

Every nucleus has the same density

  • Volume: V = ⁴⁄3πR³ = ⁴⁄3πr0³A. So V ∝ A.
  • Mass: m ≈ A × 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ kg. So m ∝ A.
  • Density = m/V: the A cancels. The same for every nucleus.
  • ρ = 3 × 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ ÷ (4π × (1.2 × 10⁻¹⁵)³) ≈ 2.3 × 10¹⁷ kg m⁻³.
  • Nucleons are packed at a fixed spacing, like molecules in a drop of liquid.
Mission 4

Nucleus versus atom

  • Solids, made of atoms: about 10³ to 2 × 10⁴ kg m⁻³ (gold: 1.9 × 10⁴).
  • Nuclear matter: 2.3 × 10¹⁷ kg m⁻³, about 10¹³ times denser.
  • Why: the nucleus is 10⁴–10⁵ times narrower than the atom, so its volume is about 10¹² to 10¹⁵ times smaller, yet it holds nearly all the mass.
  • A teaspoon (5 cm³) of nuclear matter: 5 × 10⁻⁶ × 2.3 × 10¹⁷ ≈ 1 × 10¹² kg, about a billion tonnes.
Mission check

?A neutron star has a mass of 2.8 × 10³⁰ kg. Model it as a sphere of nuclear matter, with the same density as any nucleus. Estimate its radius.

Nuclear matter has the same density, about 2.3 × 10¹⁷ kg m⁻³, in any amount. A whole star of it is only about 14 km in radius, and a teaspoon of it has a mass of about 10¹² kg: a billion tonnes.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Can a nucleus decay three times and end up as the same element it started as?

Make a guess: Can a nucleus decay three times and end up as the same element it started as?

Mission 5

Unstable nuclei

  • Some nuclei are unstable. They decay: they emit radiation and become more stable.
  • Decay is spontaneous: nothing triggers it. Heat, pressure and chemistry do not change it.
  • The emitted particle or photon is the radiation: α, β or γ.
  • The type of decay is set by the isotope: carbon-14 decays by β⁻; carbon-11 by β⁺.
Mission 5

Meet the radiations

  • α particle: a helium-4 nucleus. 2 protons + 2 neutrons. Charge +2e. Symbol ⁴2He or ⁴2α.
  • β⁻ particle: a fast electron from the nucleus. Charge −e. Symbol ⁰₋1e or ⁰₋1β.
  • β⁺ particle: a fast positron, the electron's antiparticle. Charge +e. Symbol ⁰₊1e or ⁰₊1β.
  • γ ray: a high-energy photon. No charge, no mass.
Mission 5

Balancing decay equations

  • Top numbers (nucleon number, A) add up the same on both sides.
  • Bottom numbers (proton number or charge, Z) add up the same on both sides.
  • An electron counts as ⁰₋1e: no nucleons, charge −1.
Mission 5

Worked example: α and β⁻

  • α decay: ²³⁸92U → ²³⁴90Th + ⁴2He
  • A: 238 = 234 + 4 ✓. Z: 92 = 90 + 2 ✓.
  • New element: Z falls by 2 (uranium → thorium). A falls by 4.
  • β⁻ decay: ¹⁴6C → ¹⁴7N + ⁰₋1e + ν̄
  • ν̄ is an antineutrino: uncharged, almost no mass, emitted with the β⁻ particle.
  • A: 14 = 14 + 0 ✓. Z: 6 = 7 + (−1) ✓.
  • Inside: a neutron → proton. Z rises by 1; A unchanged.
Quick check
?A nucleus has nucleon number A and proton number Z. It undergoes an alpha decay, and the nucleus formed then undergoes a beta-minus decay. Which expression gives the number of neutrons in the final nucleus?
Mission 5

β⁺ and γ decay

  • β⁺ decay: ¹⁸9F → ¹⁸8O + ⁰₊1e + ν
  • Inside: a proton → neutron. Z falls by 1; A unchanged.
  • γ decay: after α or β decay the new nucleus is often left excited. It drops to a lower energy state by emitting a γ photon.
  • ⁶⁰28Ni* → ⁶⁰28Ni + γ. A and Z do not change. Same nuclide, less energy.
Mission 5

Neutrinos

  • β⁻ decay emits an antineutrino ν̄; β⁺ decay emits a neutrino ν.
  • Both are uncharged, have tiny mass, and very rarely interact with matter.
Quick check
?Sodium-22 (²²11Na) decays by β⁺ emission. The daughter nucleus is left in an excited state and then emits a γ photon. What is the final nucleus?

Mission 5

Background radiation

  • A detector counts even with no source present: the background count.
  • Natural: radon gas from rocks and soil, rocks and building materials, cosmic rays, food and drink (e.g. potassium-40).
  • Artificial (smaller): medical uses, past weapons tests, the nuclear industry.
  • Correction: measure the background (long count, no source). Subtract it from every reading.
  • Corrected count rate = measured count rate − background count rate.
Mission check

?Thorium-232 (²³²90Th) decays through a chain of α and β⁻ decays to stable lead-208 (²⁰⁸82Pb). How many α decays and how many β⁻ decays are there in the chain?

An α decay lowers Z by 2; two β⁻ decays raise it by 2. So α, β⁻, β⁻ returns a nucleus to the same element: an isotope with 4 fewer nucleons.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Antimatter is real. How could you tell an antiparticle from its ordinary twin?

Make a guess: Antimatter is real. How could you tell an antiparticle from its ordinary twin?

Mission 6

Antimatter

  • Every particle has an antiparticle: the same mass, the opposite charge.
  • electron e⁻ ↔ positron e⁺ · proton p ↔ antiproton p̄ · neutron n ↔ antineutron n̄ · neutrino ν ↔ antineutrino ν̄.
  • Antiparticles are real: positrons come out of β⁺ decay, and hospitals use them in PET scans.
  • A particle and its antiparticle can annihilate: their mass becomes photons.
Quick check
?Predict first. The table shows the charge and mass of four particles that can be emitted when nuclei decay. One entry in the table is incorrect. Which row contains the incorrect entry?
Mission 6

Particles from nuclear decay

  • α particle (α, ⁴2He): 2 protons + 2 neutrons, a helium-4 nucleus. Charge +2e, mass ≈ 4 u.
  • Electron (e⁻, β⁻): charge −e, mass ≈ 0.00055 u.
  • Positron (e⁺, β⁺): charge +e, the same mass as the electron.
  • Neutrino (ν) and antineutrino (ν̄): no charge, negligible mass.
Mission 6

Reading off an antiparticle

  • Same: mass.
  • Opposite: charge (and other conserved quantities you will meet later).
  • Symbol: a bar over the particle's symbol: p̄, n̄, ν̄. Exception: e⁺ for the positron.
  • The antineutron is neutral, like the neutron, but it is not the same particle.
Quick check
?A proton has charge +e and mass 1.0073 u. Which describes the antiproton?
Mission check

?A beam of electrons enters the region between two oppositely charged parallel plates with velocity v, as shown, and follows track 3. A beam of positrons then enters with the same velocity. The figure shows four possible tracks, numbered 1 to 4 (not drawn to scale). Which track do the positrons follow?

An antiparticle has the same mass as its particle but the opposite charge. In a field it bends by the same amount, the opposite way: a mirror-image track.

Mission 7

Mission 7: Protons, electrons and neutrinos: what decides which family a particle belongs to?

Make a guess: Protons, electrons and neutrinos: what decides which family a particle belongs to?

Mission 7

Fundamental or composite?

  • Fundamental particle: no internal structure, as far as any experiment shows.
  • Fundamental: quarks and leptons.
  • Composite: made of smaller particles. Protons and neutrons are made of quarks.
  • Quarks are never found alone: they are always bound inside composite particles (quark confinement).
Mission 7

The particle families

  • Hadrons: made of quarks; feel the strong interaction.
  • All hadrons feel the strong and the weak force.
  • Baryons: three quarks (proton, neutron). Antibaryons: three antiquarks (antiproton, antineutron).
  • Mesons: a quark and an antiquark, e.g. pions (π).
  • Kaons (K) are mesons too.
  • Leptons: fundamental; do not feel the strong interaction. Electron, neutrino.
  • All leptons feel the weak force.
Quick check
?A particle is made of an up quark and a down antiquark. How is it classified?
Mission check

?A beam from an accelerator contains antineutrons, negative pions (π⁻), positrons and protons. Which of these particles never feel the strong interaction?

Family is decided by what a particle is made of, not by mass, charge or lifetime, and an antiparticle stays in its partner's family. Quarks or antiquarks inside: a hadron, which feels the strong interaction. Fundamental: a lepton, which does not.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Alpha scattering: most pass straight through, a very few bounce back → a tiny, dense, positive nucleus.
  • The nucleus (protons and neutrons) holds over 99.9% of the mass and is 10⁴–10⁵ times smaller than the atom.

Mission 2

  • Nucleus: protons + neutrons (nucleons). ᴬZ X: Z protons, A nucleons, A − Z neutrons.
  • Isotopes: same Z, different numbers of neutrons; same chemistry, different mass.
  • The strong nuclear force: attractive between all nucleons, very short-range.

Mission 3

  • Strong force: repulsive below ~0.5 fm, attractive to ~3 fm, negligible beyond.

Mission 4

  • R = r0A1/3, r0 ≈ 1.2 fm; nuclear radii are a few fm.
  • V ∝ A and m ∝ A, so all nuclei have the same density, about 2.3 × 10¹⁷ kg m⁻³: about 10¹³ times that of ordinary matter.

Mission 5

  • α: ⁴2He, A − 4, Z − 2. β⁻: neutron → proton, Z + 1. γ: no change to A or Z.
  • β⁺: proton → neutron, Z − 1. β⁻ emits an antineutrino, β⁺ a neutrino.
  • α: most ionising, least penetrating; γ: least ionising, most penetrating.
  • Subtract the background from every count.

Mission 6

  • Every particle has an antiparticle with the same mass and the opposite charge.
  • Pairs to know: e⁻/e⁺, p/p̄, n/n̄, ν/ν̄; a bar marks the antiparticle.

Mission 7

  • Hadrons (made of quarks; feel the strong interaction): baryons (qqq), antibaryons (q̄q̄q̄), mesons (qq̄). Leptons: fundamental, no strong interaction.
