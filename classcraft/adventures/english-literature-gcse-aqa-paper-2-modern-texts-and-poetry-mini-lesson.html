Paper 2: Modern Texts & Poetry

This mini-lesson teaches the skills and structure of AQA GCSE English Literature Paper 2 (8702/2): how to write on your modern text from memory, how to compare two anthology poems, how to tackle unseen poetry, and how to hit the Assessment Objectives AO1, AO2 and AO3. It drills poetic terminology, comparison and technique — not the finished essay.

Work through each screen, answer the questions as you go (some ask you to pick the strongest point, some to name a method) and collect ⭐ stars. Press Start when you're ready.

The shape of the paper

Three sections, one exam

Paper 2 is a 2 hour 15 minute exam worth 96 marks — that's 60% of your GCSE. It is closed book. You answer three sections:

  • Section A — Modern Texts (30 marks + 4 for spelling, punctuation & grammar): ONE essay (from a choice of two) on your studied modern prose or drama text. No extract is given — you write from memory. Rewards AO1, AO2, AO3.
  • Section B — Poetry (Anthology) (30 marks): compare two poems. One named poem is printed on the paper; you choose a second from the same cluster. Rewards AO1, AO2, AO3.
  • Section C — Unseen Poetry (32 marks): analyse one printed unseen poem (24 marks), then compare methods with a second unseen poem (8 marks). Rewards AO1 and AO2 only — no AO3.

Set texts differ by school: your modern text might be An Inspector Calls, Blood Brothers, Lord of the Flies, Animal Farm, A Taste of Honey, The History Boys, DNA or Anita and Me; your anthology cluster is Power and Conflict or Love and Relationships. The skills here work for any of them.

The Assessment Objectives

What the examiner rewards

Every mark is tied to an Assessment Objective (AO). Paper 2 rewards AO1, AO2 and AO3 — but unseen poetry (Section C) rewards only AO1 and AO2, never context:

Key rule: AO3 (context) counts in Section A and Section B, but NOT in Section C (unseen poetry). On the unseen, don't guess at history — analyse method and effect.

The heart of AO2

Method → effect, every time

Naming a technique earns almost nothing. The marks come from linking the method to the effect it creates on the reader:

method + evidence → effect on readere.g. "the harsh sibilance of 'slither, hiss' (method) makes the threat feel snake-like and sinister (effect)"

Useful poetic methods to spot: metaphor, simile, personification, imagery, alliteration, sibilance, enjambment, caesura, volta, rhyme, rhythm, repetition, tone, structure and semantic field.

Golden rule: never stop at "this is a metaphor." Always finish the sentence: "…which suggests / creates / emphasises / makes the reader feel …".

Worked model · annotation

Annotating a stanza

Here is a short, made-up stanza (not an anthology poem) annotated the way you'd mark up a printed poem in the exam:

The guns still whisper in the dark,
and shadows swallow every name;
we march, we march, we do not stop —
the cold earth learns us all the same.
  • "guns … whisper" — personification: makes the weapons feel alive and menacing.
  • "shadows swallow" — sibilance + metaphor: the soft hiss makes death feel silent and total.
  • "we march, we march" — repetition: mirrors a relentless, exhausting forward drive.
  • "the cold earth learns us" — imagery: reduces the soldiers to anonymous ground, stressing loss.

Notice the pattern: each note is quote → method (with terminology) → effect. That is exactly the sentence shape your poetry paragraphs need.

Section B skill · comparison

Comparing two poems well

In Section B you compare the printed poem with one you choose from the same cluster. Don't write about one poem, then the other (block writing) — integrate the comparison across shared dimensions:

  • Theme / ideas — what each poet says about power, conflict, love or loss.
  • Language — the methods each uses and their different effects.
  • Form & structure — stanza shape, rhyme, volta, enjambment.
  • Tone — angry, mournful, tender, ironic.

Comparative connectives stitch the two poems together: similarly, likewise, whereas, however, by contrast, while, unlike, both. Use them in almost every paragraph.

Section C skill · the unseen

Tackling unseen poetry

Section C gives you poems you've never met. There are two parts:

  • Q1 (24 marks): analyse ONE unseen poem on its own — argue what it's about and how the poet's methods create effects (AO1 + AO2).
  • Q2 (8 marks): compare the methods of that poem with a second unseen poem — this part is AO2 only, so focus on technique and effect, not ideas.

Remember: unseen poetry does NOT reward context (no AO3). Don't invent a poet's biography — read closely, spot methods, and explain their effects.

Evidence done well

Embed quotes — keep them short

Anthology poems are printed, but your modern text is not — so learn a bank of short quotations for it. In every section, weave a quote into your own sentence rather than dropping in a whole line:

Clumsy vs embedded

❌ "The poet is angry. The quote is: 'the guns still whisper in the dark, and shadows swallow every name.'"

✔ "The sibilant 'shadows swallow' makes death feel silent and total, deepening the poem's bleak, elegiac tone."

Misconception: long quotations don't earn more marks — they waste time and bury your analysis. Quote the few words you'll actually analyse.

Context & AO3

Context serves the analysis

In Section A (modern text) and Section B (anthology), context can enrich your points — but only if it explains a choice in the text. It must be woven in, not bolted on. (In Section C unseen poetry there is no AO3, so don't add context there.)

Bolted-on vs woven-in

❌ "Priestley wrote after two world wars. Rationing happened. Now back to the play."

✔ "Writing in 1945, Priestley uses the Inspector to voice a post-war hope for social responsibility — sharpening the play's warning to a complacent 1912 elite."

Misconception: context is not a paragraph you paste in. One relevant clause that illuminates a method or theme beats a page of dates.

Exam technique

Planning under the clock

Split your 2h 15m across the three sections: roughly 45 min on the modern text, 45 min on the anthology comparison, and 45 min on the unseen (about 35 on Q1 and 10 on Q2). Plan the first few minutes of each:

  • Underline the key words in the question (the focus + the command word "how").
  • For poetry, read each poem twice, annotating 3–4 methods and their effects.
  • Jot a comparison grid for Section B: shared ideas down one side, both poems' methods across.
  • Write a one-line thesis, then write. Leave a minute to check SpaG (Section A only).

Closed book: the anthology and unseen poems are printed, but your modern text is not. Learn a bank of short, flexible quotations for it in advance.

Worked model · Section B

A comparison paragraph in action

Watch how one paragraph compares two (made-up) poems on a shared idea, with method, effect, a connective and a touch of context:

Model paragraph (generic)

"Both poems present nature as a force beyond human control. In the first, the personified river that 'gnaws' at the bank gives nature a predatory hunger, making it feel relentless. By contrast, the second poem's gentle enjambment, as the tide 'creeps' unbroken across the line, presents that power as calm yet unstoppable. Where the first poet's violent verb warns of nature's threat, the second's flowing form quietly reminds us of our smallness — deepening each poet's sense of human insignificance."

Spot the moves: shared idea, both poems analysed (method + effect), comparative connective ("by contrast"), and a linking idea — AO1 and AO2 woven together.

Avoid these traps

The five marks-killers

  • Feature-spotting: naming techniques without explaining their effect.
  • Block comparison: writing all of Poem A, then all of Poem B, with no integration.
  • Context on the unseen: adding AO3 in Section C, where it earns nothing.
  • Fat quotes: long copied lines instead of short, embedded ones.
  • Plot retelling: narrating the modern text instead of arguing an answer.

Fix them all with one habit: every sentence should make a point, prove it with a short quote, or explain a method's effect — and in the poetry sections, keep the two poems talking to each other with connectives.

Recap

The skills to take into the exam

Paper 2: 2h 15m · 96 marks · 60% · closed book · A Modern text (30+4) · B Anthology (30, compare) · C Unseen (32)

AO1: argue a thesis, support with short quotes

AO2: method + terminology + evidence → effect on reader

AO3: context — Sections A & B only, NEVER the unseen (C)

Comparison: integrate both poems on shared ideas with connectives (whereas, similarly, by contrast)

Timing: ~45 min each section; modern text from memory, poems are printed
