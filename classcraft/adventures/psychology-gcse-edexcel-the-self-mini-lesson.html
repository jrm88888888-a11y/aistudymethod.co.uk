The self · self-concept, identity and free will

The self and how it starts

  • The self is your sense of who you are; your self-concept is your set of beliefs about yourself.
  • Lewis (1990): the self develops in two steps.
  • Existential self: first, in infancy. The baby realises it exists, separate from others, and that its actions affect the world.
  • Categorical self: next, the child describes itself in categories: age, gender, size, skills ("I'm 3", "I'm a boy"). Later come inner qualities like "shy".
The self · self-concept, identity and free will

Rogers: three parts of the self-concept

  • Rogers (1959) said the self-concept has three parts.
  • Self-image: how you see yourself, e.g. "I'm sporty, I'm a bit shy".
  • Self-esteem: how much you value yourself; high or low self-worth.
  • Ideal self: the person you would like to be.
  • The closer your self-image is to your ideal self, the higher your self-esteem. (More on this in the humanistic theory.)
The self · self-concept, identity and free will

Erikson: eight stages of identity

  • Erikson (1959): identity develops across the whole life in eight stages. Each has a crisis (a conflict) to resolve.
Life stageCrisis
InfancyTrust v mistrust
Early childhoodAutonomy v shame and doubt
Pre-schoolInitiative v guilt
School ageIndustry v inferiority
AdolescenceIdentity v role confusion
Young adulthoodIntimacy v isolation
Middle adulthoodGenerativity v stagnation
Late adulthoodIntegrity v despair
  • Resolving a crisis well builds a strength; failing leaves a problem that can carry into later stages.
The self · self-concept, identity and free will

Baumeister: believing in free will

  • Free will: the idea that we choose our own actions. Determinism: behaviour is caused by things outside our control (genes, upbringing).
  • Baumeister (2008) argued that believing in free will matters, whether or not it exists. It helps people take responsibility, control impulses and act morally.
  • When belief in free will is weakened, people become more likely to cheat, act aggressively and help less.
Key study The self · self-concept, identity and free will

Vohs and Schooler (2008)

Aim
To see whether weakening people's belief in free will makes them more likely to cheat.
Method
Lab experiment; 30 US university students randomly allocated to read a passage arguing free will is an illusion, or a neutral passage. Then maths problems on a computer with a "glitch" that showed the answer unless they pressed the space bar.
Results
The anti-free-will group reported weaker belief in free will and let the answers appear more often: they cheated more. The weaker someone's belief, the more they cheated.
Conclusion
Believing behaviour is determined increases cheating; belief in free will helps people behave honestly.
Strength
Random allocation and a standardised task allow cause and effect to be judged.
Weakness
Small student sample; artificial task; participants were deceived.

Mission 2

What has to be in place before a person can start to grow?

Make a guess: What has to be in place before a person can start to grow?

The self · the humanistic theory of self

Growing towards your potential

  • Humanistic psychologists see people as having free will and a drive to grow.
  • Self-actualisation: becoming the best person you can be, reaching your full potential.
  • Congruence (Rogers, 1959): your self-image and ideal self closely match. You feel worthwhile: high self-esteem.
  • Incongruence: a big gap between them. It brings anxiety and low self-esteem, and blocks self-actualisation.
The self · the humanistic theory of self

Rogers: positive regard

  • Rogers (1951): everyone needs positive regard: love, warmth and acceptance from others.
  • Unconditional positive regard: being valued whatever you do, e.g. "I love you even when you fail."
  • Conditional positive regard: being valued only when you meet others' expectations.
  • Conditions of worth: the rules you come to believe you must meet to be worthy, e.g. "I'm only lovable if I get top grades."
The self · the humanistic theory of self

Maslow's hierarchy of needs

  • Maslow (1943) arranged human needs in five levels.
LevelNeeds
5 (top)Self-actualisation: reaching your potential
4Esteem: respect, achievement, self-esteem
3Love and belonging: friends, family, acceptance
2Safety: security, shelter, stability
1 (base)Physiological: food, water, sleep, warmth
  • Lower needs must be largely met before a person is motivated by higher ones.
The self · the humanistic theory of self

Self-esteem and personality, the humanistic view

  • Self-esteem develops from the regard you receive. Unconditional positive regard leads to congruence and high self-esteem. Conditions of worth lead to incongruence and low self-esteem.
  • Personality develops as you meet your needs and move up the hierarchy. A person with unconditional positive regard becomes a fully functioning person: open, confident and growing.

Mission 3

Same grades, very different self-esteem. Where does the gap come from?

Make a guess: Same grades, very different self-esteem. Where does the gap come from?

The self · influences on self-esteem

What shapes self-esteem?

  • Self-esteem is how much you value yourself.
  • Internal influences come from within you, e.g. temperament, the emotional style you are born with.
  • External influences come from outside you: your experiences, e.g. how others treat you, successes and failures, and relationships.
  • The two work together: how you react to an experience depends partly on your temperament.
The self · influences on self-esteem

Temperament: an internal influence

  • Temperament is a person's inborn, biologically based way of reacting: how active, emotional, adaptable and sociable they are.
  • It shows early in babies and stays fairly stable.
  • Psychologists described easy, difficult and slow-to-warm-up babies.
  • Temperament affects how others respond: a calm, smiley baby may get warmer responses than a fussy one, which then feeds into self-esteem.
The self · influences on self-esteem

Experience: an external influence

  • Family: unconditional positive regard builds self-esteem; constant criticism lowers it.
  • Success and failure at school, sport or hobbies.
  • Comparison with friends and others, including on social media.
  • Feedback from teachers, coaches and peers.
  • Relationships, including with pets, which may offer unconditional acceptance.
Key study The self · influences on self-esteem

Van Houtte and Jarvis (1995)

Aim
To see whether owning a pet affects children's self-esteem, self-concept, autonomy and attachment to animals.
Method
Natural experiment; 130 preadolescent children. Pet owners (cat or dog) were matched with non-owners on parents' marital status and social class. All completed questionnaires.
Results
Pet owners showed more autonomy than non-owners. Their self-esteem and self-concept were higher mainly in the oldest children (about 11). Pet owners and non-owners did not differ in their attachment to animals.
Conclusion
Pets can boost self-esteem and autonomy, especially just before adolescence, perhaps by giving unconditional positive regard.
Strength
Matching controlled key participant variables such as family structure.
Weakness
No random allocation, so pets can't be proven to cause the difference; only cats and dogs counted.
Quick check
?In Van Houtte and Jarvis's study, why was each pet owner matched with a non-owner whose parents had the same marital status?

Mission 4

Can a questionnaire capture who you are in sixteen numbers?

Make a guess: Can a questionnaire capture who you are in sixteen numbers?

The self · measuring personality

Types or traits?

  • Personality is a person's lasting pattern of thinking, feeling and behaving.
  • Personality types put people into categories: you are one type or another, e.g. Type A (competitive, impatient) or Type B (relaxed).
  • Traits are dimensions: everyone has some amount of each, e.g. a score from very shy to very outgoing.
  • Trait theory describes personality as a person's scores on a set of traits.
The self · measuring personality

Personality scales

  • A personality scale is a questionnaire that measures where you sit on a trait.
  • You rate statements, e.g. "I enjoy meeting new people": strongly agree to strongly disagree.
  • Each answer is scored and the scores are added up for each trait.
  • Scoring is standardised, so results can be compared with other people's.
  • Most scales are self-report: you describe yourself.
The self · measuring personality

Allport's three kinds of trait

  • Allport (1936) found thousands of words in English that describe people, and grouped traits into three kinds.
  • Cardinal trait: one trait that dominates a person's whole life. Rare; most people don't have one.
  • Central traits: a handful of main traits that describe a person, e.g. honest, friendly, organised.
  • Secondary traits: shown only in some situations, e.g. impatient in queues, nervous before speeches.
The self · measuring personality

Cattell's 16PF

  • Cattell (1946) used a statistical method called factor analysis to reduce the long list of trait words to 16 personality factors (16PF).
  • Each factor is a scale between two opposites, e.g. reserved to warm, relaxed to tense.
  • The 16PF questionnaire gives a profile: one score on each of the 16 scales.
  • It is used in research, careers advice and job selection.

Mission check

?Can a questionnaire capture who you are in sixteen numbers?

Cattell's 16PF gives a reliable, standardised profile of your usual tendencies on 16 traits. But it relies on honest self-report, it describes rather than explains, and it underplays how much the situation changes behaviour.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Lewis: existential self (I exist) then categorical self (I'm a…). Rogers: self-image, self-esteem and ideal self.
  • Erikson: identity develops through eight stages, each with a crisis, from trust v mistrust to integrity v despair.
  • Baumeister: believing in free will helps people behave responsibly; Vohs and Schooler (2008) found that weakening that belief increased cheating. Key question: what makes you who you are?
  • Rogers: unconditional positive regard leads to congruence and high self-esteem; conditional positive regard creates conditions of worth, incongruence and low self-esteem.
  • Maslow: needs form a hierarchy from physiological to self-actualisation; lower needs come first.
  • Humanistic theory is positive and useful in therapy, but its concepts are hard to measure and may reflect Western values. Key question: what makes you who you are?
  • Self-esteem is shaped by internal influences (temperament, which is inborn and fairly stable) and external influences (experiences).
  • Van Houtte and Jarvis (1995) found pet owners had more autonomy, and higher self-esteem mainly at around 11, but a natural experiment can't prove cause.
  • Key question: what makes you who you are? Both what you are born with and what happens to you.
  • Personality can be measured with scales (questionnaires scoring traits as dimensions) or by sorting people into types (categories).
  • Allport: cardinal, central and secondary traits. Cattell: factor analysis gave 16 personality factors, measured by the 16PF.
  • Trait theory is reliable and useful, but self-report can be biased and it ignores situations. Key question: what makes you who you are?
