Migration, Identity and Sovereignty

One of the two Topic 8 options. Globalisation moves capital, goods and ideas freely — but people hit borders. This topic is about that collision: who moves, why, what it does to places, and what it does to the idea of the nation state itself.

EQ1 · causes of migration

Why people move

Migration is rarely a single decision. It is the product of forces at three scales, and a good answer names all three:

  • Economic pull and wage differentials — the single largest driver of voluntary international movement. Where a comparable job pays several times more across a border, migration is the rational response. Note the nuance: the poorest people rarely migrate internationally, because migration is expensive. It is often those with some resources and some education who move.
  • Demographic imbalance — ageing populations with shrinking workforces in some countries; large, young, fast-growing labour forces in others. This is a structural pull that no border policy removes.
  • Conflict and persecution — forced displacement. This is a legally distinct category, and confusing it with economic migration is the commonest error in this topic.
  • Environmental drivers — drought, flooding, sea-level rise, land degradation. These rarely act alone: they usually operate by destroying livelihoods, so environmental and economic drivers compound. Most environmentally driven movement to date has been internal and short-distance, not international.
  • Networks and enablers — established diasporas lower the cost and risk of moving, which is why migration flows, once started, tend to persist along corridors.

Examiner's eye: never use "push and pull" alone at A-level. Use it as a scaffold, then complicate it — with cost, with networks, with policy, and with the observation that drivers interact rather than adding up.

EQ1 · the categories, and why they matter

Migrant, economic migrant, refugee, asylum seeker

These are not synonyms. The distinction is legal, and it determines what protection a person is entitled to.

  • Migrant — the neutral umbrella term for anyone who moves their place of residence, whatever the reason.
  • Economic migrant — moves voluntarily, principally for work or a better standard of living. Entry depends on the host state's rules; there is no general right of entry.
  • Asylum seeker — has applied for protection on the grounds of a well-founded fear of persecution, and is awaiting a decision. Rights during this period are limited and vary by country.
  • Refugee — a person whose claim has been recognised, granting a legal right to remain and protection against being returned to danger.
  • Internally displaced person (IDP) — forced from home but still within their own country, and therefore outside the international refugee framework — which is why IDPs are frequently the least protected of all.

Why this is political. Because the category carries the rights, the political fight is often a fight over classification — describing forced migrants as "economic" removes an obligation; describing voluntary migrants as "refugees" creates one. Being precise here is not pedantry; it is the whole substance of the debate.

EQ1 · patterns of migration

Flows, corridors and the shape of movement

  • Growth. The absolute number of international migrants has risen over recent decades, alongside falling transport costs, better information and globalised labour markets. But note the corrective that examiners love: international migrants remain a small minority of the world population, and internal migration (especially rural–urban) is far larger in scale.
  • Core–periphery flows. The dominant pattern runs from lower-wage to higher-wage economies — the labour-market expression of the core–periphery structure you met in Superpowers.
  • Corridors. Flows concentrate along established routes shaped by colonial history, shared language, geographical proximity and existing diaspora networks. Corridors are self-reinforcing: each migrant lowers the cost for the next.
  • Temporary and circular migration. Not all movement is permanent. Seasonal agricultural labour, fixed-term contracts and student migration involve repeated or planned return — and states often prefer these schemes because they supply labour without conferring settlement.
  • South–South movement is substantial and consistently under-reported in Western media coverage, which focuses on flows towards high-income countries.
EQ2 · the source region

Remittances, brain drain and brain gain

Remittances — money sent home by migrants — are one of the most significant financial flows from richer to poorer regions, and unlike aid they go directly to households.

  • The multiplier effect: remittance income is largely spent locally on food, housing, healthcare and school fees. That spending supports local businesses, whose owners spend in turn — so each unit sent home generates more than one unit of local economic activity. Remittances also tend to be counter-cyclical: they often hold up, or rise, when the home economy is in crisis.
  • The costs: dependency on a flow that can stop; inflation in local land and housing markets; and rising inequality between households that have a migrant abroad and those that do not.
  • Brain drain: the loss of the educated and skilled — doctors, nurses, engineers, teachers — often trained at public expense in the source country and then employed in the host. This is a real transfer of human capital from the poorer country to the richer one, and it hits health and education systems hardest.
  • Brain gain (the counter-argument): returning migrants bring skills, capital and networks; diasporas invest and trade with home; and the very prospect of migration can raise the incentive to acquire education, so more people train than actually leave. Whether gain outweighs drain depends on return rates and on whether the state can absorb returning skills.
  • Demographic effects on the source: selective out-migration of young working-age adults distorts the age–sex structure, raising the dependency ratio and leaving behind an older population.

Case-study honesty: resist quoting remittance totals or migrant stocks you cannot source. The mechanism — direct-to-household transfer with a local multiplier, offset by dependency and inequality — is what carries the marks.

Skills · reading the numbers

What those two figures actually mean

A dependency ratio of 100 means there is one dependant for every one person of working age — an extremely high burden on the working population, and exactly the demographic signature that heavy out-migration of young adults produces in a source region.

  • Net migration hides gross flows. A net figure near zero can conceal enormous churn — large numbers arriving and large numbers leaving, with completely different skill profiles. Always ask for the gross figures.
  • The mirror image in the host. The same movement that raises the source region's dependency ratio lowers the host's, because migrants are disproportionately of working age. This is why ageing societies with shrinking workforces have a structural economic interest in immigration — even where the politics runs the other way.
  • Migrants are not permanently young. The demographic dividend is temporary: migrants age, and they may bring or form families. Any argument that treats immigration as a permanent fix for ageing needs to say so.

Skills link: Edexcel will hand you a population pyramid or a migration table. Calculate, then interrogate: what does the figure conceal, and over what timescale does it hold?

EQ2 · consequences for the host

The host region: economic, cultural, political

  • Economic. Migrants fill labour shortages across the skill spectrum, contribute taxes, and can raise the size of the economy. The honest counter: the effects are unevenly distributed — the aggregate gain can coexist with localised pressure on wages in specific low-paid sectors, and with real pressure on housing and public services in the neighbourhoods where migrants concentrate. Aggregate benefit and local cost are both true, and the best essays say so.
  • Demographic. Migrants are disproportionately of working age, slowing population ageing and lowering the dependency ratio.
  • Cultural. New languages, foods, religions, music and businesses. This is experienced by some as enrichment and by others as loss — and both reactions are real social facts that a geographer must explain rather than dismiss.
  • Political. Migration reshapes electoral politics; it can trigger a nationalist or restrictionist reaction; and it raises questions about integration, segregation and citizenship.

Where does tension come from? Pressure on housing and services (particularly where public investment has not followed population growth); the labour-market debate; the pace of cultural change; and — crucially — the role of media and political framing. The way migration is described (a "flow", a "wave", a "crisis") shapes public perception at least as powerfully as the numbers themselves. Public estimates of the size of migrant populations are routinely far higher than the actual figures, which tells you that perception is not simply a reading of reality.

EQ3 · nation, state, sovereignty

The nation state — a much stranger idea than it looks

Get the vocabulary exactly right; a great deal follows from it.

  • A state is a political and legal entity: defined territory, permanent population, a government, and sovereignty — supreme authority within its borders, recognised by other states.
  • A nation is a cultural and psychological entity: a group who believe they share an identity, a history, and often a language.
  • A nation state assumes the two coincide. In reality they almost never do neatly — most states contain several nations, and several nations are spread across multiple states. That mismatch is the origin of a very large share of the world's territorial conflict.
  • National identity is constructed, not natural. It is made and maintained through education, national narratives, flags, anthems, sport, media and shared institutions. Saying this is not cynicism — it is the standard scholarly position, and it explains why identity can change.

Contested nationality: who counts as a national is decided by law, and the law varies. Some states grant citizenship by descent (jus sanguinis), some by place of birth (jus soli), most by some combination plus naturalisation. Because these rules are choices, nationality is a political construction — and disputes over it are disputes about who belongs.

EQ3 · how borders are made and contested

Borders: drawn, inherited, disputed

  • Colonial borders. Many international boundaries were drawn by imperial powers, frequently with little reference to the distribution of the peoples living there — cutting through some communities and enclosing others that had no shared identity. Post-independence states largely inherited these lines, because reopening them would have been more dangerous than keeping them. The consequences — divided ethnic groups, contested territory, states without a common national identity — persist.
  • Separatist and independence movements arise where a group inside a state identifies as a distinct nation and seeks autonomy or independence. Their claims typically rest on some combination of language, ethnicity, religion, historical statehood, and a sense of economic exploitation by the centre.
  • Disputed territory occurs where two or more states claim the same space — often because a border is ambiguous, because it was imposed, or because the territory acquired new value (resources, water, strategic position). Compare directly with the maritime disputes in Superpowers: the mechanism is the same.
  • Borders are also being re-asserted. Physical barriers, biometric controls, offshore processing and externalised border enforcement (paying neighbouring states to stop people before they arrive) have all expanded — even as goods and capital move more freely than ever. That asymmetry is the central paradox of this topic.

EQ4 · is sovereignty eroding?

The pressures on the nation state

  • Supranational institutions. Joining bodies such as the EU or the UN means accepting rules and, sometimes, courts above the national level. The key analytical move: this is usually a pooling of sovereignty that states chose in exchange for influence and market access — not sovereignty simply taken away.
  • TNCs and financial markets. Firms can shift production and profits between jurisdictions; markets can discipline a government's budget within hours. Both constrain what a state can actually do, whatever its formal authority.
  • The nationalist reaction. The perceived loss of control — over borders, laws and identity — is a major driver of the resurgence of nationalism and of demands to "take back control". Whether that perception matches the reality is a separate question, and a good essay separates them.
  • The counter-argument. States still control citizenship, still hold armed forces, still tax and legislate, and demonstrated during recent global crises that they can close borders and reorder economies at extraordinary speed. The nation state is not dead.

EQ4 · managing migration

How states try to manage migration

  • Border control — physical barriers, surveillance, biometric identification, and increasingly externalisation: agreements with transit countries to intercept people before they reach the border at all.
  • Points-based systems — admission by skill, qualification, language and salary. They deliver the labour the economy is judged to need, but they select strongly for the highly skilled, which intensifies brain drain in source countries — a direct trade-off between host interest and source interest.
  • Quotas and caps — simple to state politically, but blunt: they cannot flex with labour demand, and they do not distinguish need.
  • Asylum policy — legally distinct, because obligations to those fleeing persecution are not discretionary in the way economic-migration policy is. The tension between that obligation and the political pressure to reduce arrivals is the sharpest single conflict in the whole topic.
  • International frameworks — global agreements on migration exist but are largely non-binding, and responsibility for hosting displaced people falls very unevenly, with a large share borne by countries neighbouring conflicts rather than by the wealthiest states. Assessing why cooperation is so weak is a strong evaluative line: each state has an individual incentive to shift responsibility elsewhere, so collectively the system underprovides protection.

The evaluative core of this topic: globalisation has made the free movement of capital, goods and information the default, while the movement of people remains tightly rationed by states. Explaining why states resist the one flow they could most easily benefit from economically — and why that resistance is about identity and control rather than economics — is the highest-level argument available to you here.

Exam technique · the 20-marker

Writing the evaluative essay

  • Define the contested term. "Sovereignty", "national identity", "migrant" — say which meaning you are using. This topic is full of words that do political work.
  • Disaggregate by scale. National aggregate versus local neighbourhood; source versus host; short term versus long term. Most of the marks live in these distinctions.
  • Take the opposing view seriously. This is an emotive topic and it is where students most often caricature the other side. Examiners reward the answer that states the strongest version of the argument it then challenges.
  • Use the players framework. Governments, employers, migrants themselves, host communities, source communities, the media, supranational bodies — each has different power and different interests. That is also Paper 3 thinking.
  • Conclude conditionally. Say to what extent, and on what your judgement depends.

Try it: "Evaluate the view that globalisation has fatally weakened the sovereignty of the nation state." Blocks: (1) the erosion case — supranational rules, TNCs, financial markets, transnational identities; (2) the resilience case — states still control citizenship, borders and force, and pooling is chosen, not imposed; (3) the decisive move — sovereignty has been redistributed unevenly: weakened over economic policy, largely intact over territory and membership. Judgement: "fatally" is wrong, but the perception of lost control is politically real and is itself reshaping the state.

Paper 3 · synoptic links

Where this topic connects

  • → Globalisation (Topic 3): the same process that frees capital constrains labour. Migration is globalisation's most contested flow.
  • → Superpowers (Topic 7): contested borders, disputed territory and the erosion of sovereignty by IGOs and TNCs are the same debate, viewed from the state rather than from above.
  • → Carbon Cycle and Energy Security (Topic 6) and Water Insecurity (Topic 5): environmental change and resource scarcity are drivers of displacement — mostly internal, and mostly acting through livelihoods rather than directly.
  • → Regenerating / Diverse Places (Topic 4): the local, neighbourhood-scale experience of migration and identity — the same processes, seen from the street.

Recap

The big ideas to know

Causes: wage differentials · demographic imbalance · conflict and persecution · environmental drivers (mostly acting through livelihoods) · networks and corridors. The poorest rarely migrate internationally — migration costs money

Categories: migrant (umbrella) · economic migrant (voluntary, no right of entry) · asylum seeker (claim pending) · refugee (claim recognised, protected) · IDP (internally displaced, least protected). The category carries the obligation

Patterns: growth in absolute numbers but still a small share of world population; internal > international; core–periphery flows; self-reinforcing corridors; temporary and circular migration; substantial South–South movement

Source effects: remittances (direct to households, local multiplier, counter-cyclical) vs dependency and inequality; brain drain vs brain gain (depends on return rates); rising dependency ratio

Host effects: labour supply, tax, lower dependency ratio — but aggregate gain coexists with concentrated local pressure; cultural change experienced as enrichment or as loss; media and political framing shape perception more than numbers do

Calculations: net migration = immigration − emigration · dependency ratio = (under-15s + over-64s) ÷ 15–64s × 100

Nation vs state: a state is legal and territorial; a nation is cultural. They rarely coincide — and national identity is constructed, so it can change

Borders: colonial legacy · separatist movements · disputed territory (value + ambiguity) · and simultaneous re-assertion through barriers, biometrics and externalisation

Sovereignty: pooled (not simply surrendered) to supranational bodies; constrained by TNCs and financial markets; but states still hold citizenship, borders and force. Nationalism is the reaction to perceived loss of control

Management: border control · points-based systems (which intensify brain drain) · quotas · asylum policy (a legal obligation, not a discretionary one) · weak, largely non-binding international frameworks and very uneven responsibility-sharing
