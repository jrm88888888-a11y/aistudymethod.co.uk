Mission 1: Only about one person in 27 is an international migrant. Why do some people move while most stay?

Make a guess: Only about one person in 27 is an international migrant. Why do some people move while most stay?

Specification: what this mission covers

Mission 1

Who is a migrant?

  • International migrant: "any person who changes his or her country of usual residence" (UN DESA).
  • Voluntary: chooses to move (work, family, study). Forced: compelled by conflict, persecution or disaster.
  • Economic migrant: moves mainly for work or income.
  • Refugee: outside their country "owing to well-founded fear of being persecuted".
  • Asylum seeker: has claimed protection; claim not yet decided. Undocumented (irregular): no legal permission to enter or stay.
Mission 1

Global numbers

  • 304 million international migrants in 2024, up from 275 million in 2020.
  • 3.7% of the world's population.
  • 48% were women or girls.
  • One in six was forcibly displaced across borders.
  • End of 2025: 41.6 million refugees; 9 million asylum seekers awaiting decisions.

Mission 1

Globalisation and labour demand: China and Schengen

  • China (rural–urban): 301.15 million migrant workers in 2025; 180.06 million worked away from their home towns.
  • The urbanisation rate reached 67.89% at the end of 2025; 357.88 million people lived away from their place of household registration.
  • Driver: factory, construction and service jobs in coastal cities as China joined global production.
  • EU–Schengen (international): 29 countries with checks at internal borders abolished; Bulgaria and Romania joined on 1 January 2025.
  • "Every day, over 3.5 million people cross internal borders for work, study, or to visit family".
Mission 1

Why the share varies: Singapore, Japan, Australia

  • Singapore: non-residents were 1,976,999 of 6,208,523 people in June 2026, 31.8% (our calculation).
  • Australia: 32.0% of residents born overseas (June 2025).
  • Japan: total population fell for the 14th year to October 2024, while the foreign population's net migration was positive for the third year.
  • Net migration per 1,000 people (2024): Japan 1.2; Singapore 3.4; Australia 5.2.
  • Policy (work-permit systems, skilled-visa targets) and economic engagement (trade hubs, ageing workforces) explain the gaps.
Mission 1

Events change the pattern

  • Most migrants are not forced: five in six are not forcibly displaced. Most move for work, family or study.
  • Political events: refugees under UNHCR's mandate rose from 16.1 million (2015) to 31.6 million (2023); Ukraine's emigrant numbers rose 124% from 2020 to 2024.
  • Mediterranean crossings: sea arrivals to Europe 363,123 (2016), 102,431 (2019), 263,048 (2023), 144,854 (2025).
  • Environmental: floods and droughts add to economic pushes (Pakistan, 2022).

Mission 2

The economic case for free movement

  • Economic theory: efficiency is maximised when goods (free trade), capital (open financial markets) and labour (open borders) move freely.
  • Workers move to where they are most productive; employers fill shortages.
  • One economist's review: gains from removing migration barriers "amount to large fractions of world GDP".
  • That is "one or two orders of magnitude larger" than the gains from freeing the remaining flows of goods and capital.
Mission 2

The challenge: identity and sovereignty

  • Sovereignty: a state's authority to decide who may enter and stay.
  • National identity: a shared sense of culture and belonging that migration can be seen to change.
  • UK, 2016: in the EU referendum on 23 June the UK "voted to leave the European Union".
  • UK, 2020: the government set out to "take back control of our borders" and said "We are ending free movement".
  • USA, 2025: a presidential proclamation declared that "America's sovereignty is under attack" at the southern border.

Mission 2

Free movement inside states and blocs

  • Within the UK, anyone may move region with no permission. In the year before the 2021 Census, 5.6 million people in England and Wales moved within the UK; 545,096 arrived from outside it: about 10 times fewer (our calculation).
  • EU: "Freedom of movement for workers shall be secured within the Union".
  • Schengen: 29 countries without checks at internal borders.
  • 14.06 million EU citizens lived in another EU country on 1 January 2025: 3.1% of the EU's people (our calculation).
Mission 2

…but not globally

  • 304 million international migrants in 2024: just 3.7% of people.
  • No global agreement gives a right to move for work between all countries.
  • "Vast numbers of people in low-income countries want to emigrate … but cannot".
  • Free labour movement stops at the edge of the state or the bloc.

Mission 2

Migration changes composition

  • England and Wales, 2021: 10.0 million residents born outside the UK, 16.8% (our calculation).
  • 3.64 million (6.1%) were born in EU countries, mostly arriving under free movement (our calculation).
  • EU, 2025: 31.2 million residents were citizens of non-EU countries, 6.9% (our calculation).
  • New languages, faiths, foods and festivals: the cultural and ethnic mix of the state changes.
Mission 2

Integration varies between nations

  • Integration: how far migrants share in jobs, schooling, language and civic life.
  • Employment rate, ages 20–64, 2024: born in the country vs born outside the EU:
    • Netherlands 85.9 vs 69.4 · Sweden 85.0 vs 70.4 · Belgium 74.8 vs 59.4
    • Italy 67.2 vs 66.1 · Portugal 78.3 vs 78.0 · Poland 78.2 vs 84.5
  • The gap is wide in some states and near zero (or reversed) in others.
Mission 2

…and within nations

  • England and Wales, 2021: of residents whose main language is not English, 79.7% speak English well or very well (our calculation).
  • By local authority (those with at least 5,000 such residents): Cambridge 91.2%, Pendle 62.3% (our calculation).
  • Integration depends on who came, when, why, and on local jobs, schools and services.

Mission 2

Political tensions: perceptions differ

  • Mexico–US: the US proclamation (2025) framed the border in terms of security and sovereignty and sent the Armed Forces to assist border control.
  • For Mexico, emigration brings income: remittances of US$67.9 billion in 2024, 3.6% of GDP in 2025. Mexico is among the top origins, with 11.6 million emigrants.
  • Between EU states: the UK government said its system had been "distorted by European free movement rights"; the EU treats free movement of workers as a treaty right. Free movement with the UK ended on 1 January 2021.
  • Same flows; different perceptions of economic, social, cultural and demographic impacts.
Mission 2

Who is able to migrate?

  • Skill: the UK's system needs 70 points: a job offer from an approved sponsor, at a set skill level, and English.
  • Income: salary thresholds (in 2020, £25,600); "We will not introduce a general low-skilled or temporary work route".
  • Opportunity and controls: an EU citizen could move to Germany without a visa; most other people need one.
  • Income and visa rules limit who moves: many in low-income countries "want to emigrate … but cannot".
  • Exam move (Edexcel "Assess"): weigh efficiency gains against identity and sovereignty concerns, using one bloc and one border.

Mission check

Free movement of labour can raise efficiency, but it exists only within states and some blocs. It changes the composition of societies, integration varies, and differing perceptions of impact fuel political tensions over sovereignty and identity.

Mission 3

State, nation, nation state

  • State: a political unit with sovereign control of a territory. The 1933 Montevideo Convention lists four tests: "a permanent population; a defined territory; government; and capacity to enter into relations with the other states".
  • Nation: a large group of people who feel bound by shared culture, language, history or identity.
  • Nation state: where the borders of a state and a nation roughly match.
  • In reality, most states hold more than one nation or ethnic group.
Mission 3

Sovereignty and territorial integrity

  • Sovereignty: a state's supreme authority over its own territory and affairs. The UN "is based on the principle of the sovereign equality of all its Members".
  • Territorial integrity: a state's right to keep its borders intact. Members "shall refrain … from the threat or use of force against the territorial integrity or political independence of any state".
  • Montevideo adds: "The political existence of the state is independent of recognition by the other states". In practice, recognition decides membership of the UN and much of the world's trade and diplomacy.
Mission 3

The world political map changes

  • UN members: 51 in 1945 → 99 in 1960 → 193 in 2011.
  • In 1960 alone, 17 states joined, most of them newly independent African states such as Nigeria, Senegal and Mali.
  • The newest member, South Sudan, joined in 2011.
  • The map is not fixed: borders are made, split and argued over.

Mission 3

Iceland and Singapore

  • Iceland: a remote North Atlantic island, long isolated, with one main language and culture. Even so, immigrants rose from 8.4% of the population in 2014 to 18.4% in 2023 (Statistics Iceland; calculated).
  • Singapore: a trading port at the meeting of sea routes, built by migration. Residents in 2026: 73.9% Chinese, 13.5% Malay, 9.0% Indian, 3.6% other (calculated from SingStat).
  • History, isolation and migration explain the difference: isolation favours unity; trade and migration produce diversity.
  • Neither is fixed: migration is now changing Iceland too.
Mission 3

Borders drawn from outside: Iraq and Rwanda

  • Physical vs drawn borders: some borders follow physical features (rivers, lakes, mountain ridges); others are geometric or negotiated lines drawn by outside powers, often cutting across ethnic groups.
  • Iraq: after the Ottoman Empire collapsed in the First World War, Britain ran Iraq as a mandate; Iraq ended mandate status in 1932.
  • Most Kurds live "in contiguous areas of Iran, Iraq, and Turkey"; the 1920 Treaty of Sèvres "provided for an autonomous Kurdistan but was never ratified", and the 1923 Treaty of Lausanne "made no mention of Kurdistan or the Kurds".
  • Iraq's 2005 constitution makes Arabic and Kurdish "the two official languages" and recognises "the region of Kurdistan" as a federal region.
  • Rwanda: ruled by Germany, then placed under Belgian administration after the First World War. Tensions between Hutu and Tutsi rose during decolonisation; by independence in 1962, 120,000 people, mainly Tutsi, had fled to neighbouring states.
  • Legitimacy problem: when borders or rulers are imposed from outside, some groups may not accept the state's authority.

Mission 3

A contested border: Ukraine and Russia

  • 2014: after a referendum in Crimea, the UN General Assembly called on states "not to recognize any change in the status of Crimea" (100 for, 11 against, 58 abstentions).
  • 2022: the Assembly condemned the "attempted illegal annexation" of Donetsk, Kherson, Luhansk and Zaporizhzhia (143 for, 5 against, 35 abstentions).
  • Russia voted against both; it treats the areas as Russian, while most UN members treat them as Ukrainian.
  • Movement: refugees from Ukraine rose from about 28,000 in 2021 to 5.2 million in 2025, with 3.7 million displaced inside Ukraine (UNHCR).
Mission 3

An unrecognised state: Taiwan

  • Taiwan (the Republic of China) has its own elected government, but in 1971 UN Resolution 2758, "Restoration of the lawful rights of the People's Republic of China in the United Nations", gave China's UN seat to the People's Republic.
  • The People's Republic of China regards Taiwan as part of China; Taiwan's government runs its own affairs.
  • Only 12 states have formal diplomatic relations with Taiwan (its foreign ministry lists Belize, Eswatini, Guatemala, Haiti, the Holy See, the Marshall Islands, Palau, Paraguay, St Kitts and Nevis, St Lucia, St Vincent and the Grenadines, and Tuvalu).
  • So Taiwan meets three Montevideo tests easily; its recognition is what is limited.

Statehood is partly legal and partly political. Borders shaped by empires and wars can leave groups divided and sovereignty contested, and recognition by other states decides who sits at the UN.

Mission 4

Nationalism and empire in the 1800s

  • Nationalism grew in 19th-century Europe: the idea that each nation should have its own strong state.
  • Strong states competed for power, trade and prestige by building empires abroad.
  • Imperialism: one state ruling other peoples and territories, often by force.
  • Empire spread European ideas of nationhood, which colonised peoples later used to demand their own states.
Mission 4

The British Raj

  • British Raj: "direct British rule over the Indian subcontinent from 1858 until the independence of India and Pakistan in 1947".
  • 1858: Parliament's Government of India Act transferred "British power over India from the company to the crown".
  • 1885: the Indian National Congress "held its first meeting in December 1885 in Bombay city", a focus for Indian nationalism.
  • Empire created one administration over many peoples; nationalism then grew against it.

Mission 4

1947: independence and partition

  • From 15 August 1947, "two independent Dominions shall be set up in India, to be known respectively as India and Pakistan".
  • British unification of the subcontinent "ended after World War II with communal civil war, mass migration, and partition".
  • New borders split communities and caused one of the largest forced movements of the 20th century.
  • Independence did not produce one nation state but two states, each containing many groups.
Mission 4

The post-1945 wave of decolonisation

  • Decolonisation: colonies gaining independence from imperial powers.
  • UN members: 51 in 1945 → 99 in 1960; 17 states joined in 1960 alone, nearly all in Africa.
  • 1960: the UN Declaration on the Granting of Independence to Colonial Countries and Peoples "proclaimed that colonialism should be brought to a speedy and unconditional end".
  • Since 1945, 80 former colonies have gained independence; 17 territories are still on the UN's list.

Mission 4

Costly independence: Vietnam

  • France "struggled to control its colonies in Indochina - Vietnam, Cambodia, and Laos" in the late 1940s.
  • Nationalist uprisings grew; on 7 May 1954 the French garrison at Dien Bien Phu "fell after a four month siege led by Vietnamese nationalist Ho Chi Minh".
  • France then withdrew, but the US "would not pull out of Vietnam for another twenty years".
  • Decolonisation became tangled with Cold War rivalry, prolonging conflict.
Mission 4

Costly independence: Sudan

  • Sudan gained independence "from British and Egyptian rule in 1956", then "experienced recurring civil wars primarily between North and South".
  • The 2005 Comprehensive Peace Agreement set a referendum for January 2011; "Voters overwhelmingly chose independence".
  • South Sudan declared independence on 9 July 2011 and became the UN's 193rd member.
  • Colonial borders grouped different peoples in one state; decades of war followed.

Mission 4

Migration to the imperial core

  • The British Nationality Act 1948 created "citizen of the United Kingdom and Colonies" and made Commonwealth citizens, including those of India and Pakistan, British subjects.
  • Shared citizenship, language and labour demand drew migrants from former colonies to the UK after 1948.
  • 2021 census (England and Wales): 16.8% born outside the UK; the most common non-UK countries of birth were India (920,000), Poland (743,000) and Pakistan (624,000).
  • Links continue through family ties, study and work, changing the UK's ethnic composition.

Empire, nationalism and decolonisation are one chain: empires joined peoples, nationalists broke away, and the ties that remained still move people between former colonies and the old imperial core.

Mission 5

Sovereignty and the TNC

  • Sovereignty: a state's authority over its territory, laws and taxes.
  • A TNC operates in many states at once and can choose where to place assets, staff and profits. Each state taxes only what is recorded in its territory.
  • Profit repatriation: UNCTAD estimated FDI income of $1.5 trillion in 2011 on a stock of $21 trillion; nearly one-third was retained in host economies and two-thirds repatriated.
Mission 5

Avoidance or evasion?

  • Tax evasion is illegal: hiding income or wealth from the tax authority.
  • Tax avoidance uses the rules to reduce tax. The OECD defines base erosion and profit shifting (BEPS) as "tax planning strategies used by multinational enterprises that exploit gaps and mismatches in tax rules to avoid paying tax".
  • Avoidance is lawful, but rules change and arrangements can be challenged.
Mission 5

How profit shifting works

  • A TNC places intangible assets (brands, patents, software) in a subsidiary in a low-tax jurisdiction.
  • Subsidiaries in high-tax countries pay it royalties or fees, which cut their taxable profit.
  • Transfer pricing: the prices set for trade between parts of the same TNC decide where profit is recorded.
  • States with very low or zero tax rates that attract such profits are called tax havens.
Mission 5

The scale

  • Tørsløv, Wier and Zucman estimate that close to 40% of multinational profits, close to $1 trillion, were shifted to tax havens in 2019.
  • They estimate this cut corporate tax revenue by more than $200 billion, about 10% of global corporate tax receipts.
  • These are model estimates from national accounts, not audited counts.
Mission 5

Case study: Apple and Ireland, 2003–2014

  • In August 2016 the European Commission decided that Ireland had given Apple illegal tax benefits of up to €13 billion for 2003–2014.
  • The effective tax rate of Apple Sales International "declined from 1% in 2003 to 0.005% in 2014".
  • Under Irish tax rulings, most profit was allocated to "head offices" that "existed only on paper"; those profits "were not subject to tax in any country".
Mission 5

The outcome

  • Ireland and Apple appealed separately. In 2020 the EU General Court annulled the decision.
  • On 10 September 2024 the Court of Justice set that judgment aside and confirmed the Commission's 2016 decision.
  • Apple had paid €14.3 billion into an escrow account in 2018; by May 2025 it had passed to the Irish state.
  • It was Ireland's tax rulings, not a crime by Apple, that were found to be illegal state aid.

Mission 5

Havens for wealthy expatriates

  • Some states attract wealthy individuals with low or no personal taxes. The UAE government states: "The UAE does not levy income tax on individuals".
  • The EU Tax Observatory (2024): "Global billionaires have effective tax rates equivalent to 0% to 0.5% of their wealth".
  • Offshore tax evasion has fallen "by a factor of about three over the last 10 years", after countries began exchanging bank information automatically.
Mission 5

Governments and IGOs: accept, then regulate

  • Most governments and IGOs have not abolished tax havens; they set shared rules instead.
  • OECD/G20 Two-Pillar solution (8 October 2021): agreed by 139 member jurisdictions by 9 June 2023.
  • Ireland: from 31 December 2023, groups with global turnover over €750 million pay a minimum effective rate of 15%; other businesses keep 12.5%.
Mission 5

NGO objections

  • The Tax Justice Network (an NGO) estimates countries lose US$492 billion a year to tax abuse: US$347.6 billion through multinational profit shifting and US$144.8 billion through wealthy individuals hiding wealth offshore.
  • It argues OECD reform has failed and calls for a UN tax convention; it names eight countries opposed to one, including the UK and the US.
  • These are the NGO's estimates and framing ("tax abuse"); compare them with the academic estimates above (Tørsløv, Wier and Zucman).

Mission 5

Global inequality as a threat

  • World Inequality Report 2022: the richest 10% of the world's population take 52% of global income; the poorest half earn 8.5%.
  • Wealth is more unequal: the richest 10% own 76%, the poorest half 2%.
  • Lost tax revenue weakens states' ability to fund services, so profit shifting and inequality are linked arguments in the case that the global system is unsustainable.
Mission 5

An alternative model: Ecuador

  • Ecuador's 2008 Constitution commits the state "to build A new form of public coexistence, in diversity and in harmony with nature, to achieve the good way of living, the sumak kawsay".
  • Article 71: "Nature, or Pacha Mama … has the right to integral respect for its existence" — the rights of nature.
  • The model puts well-being, equality and nature ahead of growth alone, with a larger role for the state.

CITES (biosphere)

Mission 12

Globalisation's rise and stall

  • World trade (exports plus imports) was 25.8% of world GDP in 1970 and 60.3% in 2008.
  • It fell to 51.9% in 2009, then moved between about 52% and 62%; it was 56.7% in 2024.
  • The long rise stopped after 2008. This slowdown is sometimes called "slowbalisation".

Backlash: nationalism and separatism

  • Separatism: a region seeking to leave a state and become independent.
  • A correlation between globalisation and nationalism needs evidence of a mechanism before it can be called a cause.

The UK's vote to leave the EU

  • On 23 June 2016 the UK voted to leave the European Union: Leave won 51.9%.
  • Leave won 53.4% in England and 52.5% in Wales, but 44.2% in Northern Ireland and 38.0% in Scotland.
  • A national choice to leave a trading bloc, with sharp differences between the UK's nations.
Mission 12

Brexit and the "China shock"

  • Colantone and Stanig (2018) found support for Leave "was systematically higher in regions hit harder" by globalisation.
  • Exposure was measured by "the shock of surging imports from China over the past three decades".
  • They link the effect to workers displaced by import competition "in the absence of effective compensation"; their design (an instrumental variable) "supports a causal interpretation".

Smaller states inside bigger blocs

  • Scotland, 18 September 2014: "Should Scotland be an independent country?" No 55.30%, Yes 44.70%, turnout 84.59%.
  • In 2016 only 38.0% of voters in Scotland chose Leave, against 51.9% across the UK: a nation outvoted on leaving a trading bloc.

Mission 12

Tensions in an emerging economy: Indonesia

  • Indonesia reinstated a ban on exporting nickel ore from January 2020, "reportedly in order to conserve ore for the domestic processing industry" (USGS).
  • By 2025 Indonesia mined about 2.6 million of the world's 3.9 million tonnes.
  • The EU challenged the ban at the WTO; on 30 November 2022 a panel recommended Indonesia bring its measures into line with GATT rules, and on 8 December 2022 Indonesia appealed.
  • A state trying to keep more of the benefits of globalisation clashes with trading partners and global rules.
Mission 12

Fragile states: Somalia

  • Fragile state: a state with weak capacity to govern, provide services or control its territory.
  • Somalia ranked 195th of 195 countries in the KOF Globalisation Index 2025: the least globally connected.
  • In December 2023 it became the 37th country to complete the HIPC debt-relief process.
  • A 2022 study commissioned by UNICEF and WHO estimated 43,000 excess deaths in Somalia's drought that year, half among children under 5.
  • World Bank (2026): "The security situation remains fragile"; Somalia depends on "imports and external assistance", with "a narrow export base", and the aim is "a more stable, visible, and legitimate state".
  • Exam move (Edexcel 8B.12c): a weak state collects little revenue and provides few services; test the claim that a powerful elite captures the gains from global flows, and back it with a sourced example.

Synthesis: weighing globalisation

  • Advantages: wider markets, cheaper goods, export jobs, flows of investment and ideas.
  • Risks: losers concentrated in places, fragile supply chains, emissions, political backlash.
  • Scale: gains often national and thin; losses often local and deep. That geography helps explain the backlash.

?By early May 2025, average US tariffs on Chinese goods had reached 126.1%, and China's on US goods peaked at 147.6% in mid-April; by 22 August 2026 they stood at 35.2% and 31.9%. Is this evidence that globalisation creates its own backlash?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • 304 million international migrants (3.7%, 2024); 48% female; one in six forcibly displaced; most move voluntarily for work, family or study.
  • Globalisation drives rural–urban (China, 301 million migrant workers) and Schengen mobility; shares vary with policy (Singapore 31.8%, Australia 32.0%, Japan low).

Mission 2

  • Economists see large gains from freer migration, but states guard sovereignty over borders: free labour movement exists within the UK and the EU, not globally.
  • Migration changes cultural and ethnic composition; integration (e.g. employment gaps, English proficiency) varies between and within states; perceptions of impact drive tensions (Mexico–US; UK–EU), and skill, income and border rules decide who can move.

Mission 3

  • A state has territory, population, government and the capacity for relations; a nation is a people; few states are true nation states. Sovereignty and territorial integrity are protected in the UN Charter but are contested in practice.
  • Isolation (Iceland) and migration (Singapore) shape unity; colonial and post-1918 borders (Iraq, Rwanda) can create legitimacy problems; Ukraine and Taiwan show contested borders and limited recognition.

Mission 4

  • 19th-century nationalism fuelled empire building; British rule (the Raj, 1858–1947) united the subcontinent and provoked Indian nationalism, ending in independence and partition (1947).
  • Post-1945 decolonisation created dozens of new states (UN 51 → 99 by 1960); some independence struggles were long and costly (Vietnam to 1954; Sudan's wars to 2011).
  • Post-colonial links (1948 Act, family ties) keep migration flowing to the UK: India is the top non-UK country of birth (920,000 in 2021).

Mission 5

  • TNCs challenge sovereignty by choosing where profit is recorded; states respond by cooperating (OECD 15% minimum), which limits their own choices.
  • Low-tax states attract TNC profits and wealthy individuals; governments regulate rather than abolish them, while NGOs such as the Tax Justice Network object.
  • Growing inequality is seen as a threat to the system; Ecuador's sumak kawsay model cut poverty but depended on oil.

Mission 6

  • The UN grew from 50 drafting states (1945) to 193 members; the Security Council (P5 veto) holds primary responsibility for peace, alongside peacekeeping, law and development functions.
  • Record: Iran sanctions mixed (1737 → JCPOA → snapback 2025); Congo mixed (ONUC; MONUSCO yet M23 took Goma in 2025); states also act outside the UN (Afghanistan after Article 5; Iraq 2003, "not a last resort").

Mission 7

  • The IMF and World Bank (1944) and GATT (1947, WTO from 1995) were set up by the wartime allies to rebuild a free-trade, market economy.
  • Votes and conditions give the founders, especially the USA, lasting influence; SAP and HIPC outcomes are disputed, as Jamaica shows.
  • Membership is near-universal; regional blocs range from trade-only (USMCA) to political union (EU).

Mission 8

  • IGOs manage shared environments through treaties: Montreal (ozone), CITES (wildlife trade), UNCLOS (oceans), Helsinki Rules (shared rivers), with the MA monitoring ecosystem services.
  • Success varies: Montreal is the benchmark; the others depend on national enforcement, contested sharing rules or (for the MA) have no rules at all.

Mission 9

  • The 1959 Treaty (58 Parties, 29 Consultative) keeps Antarctica peaceful, freezes claims and makes science its purpose; the wider ATS adds seals (1972), marine life (CCAMLR 1980) and the Protocol (1991) with its open-ended mining ban.
  • Strengths: no military use, open science, inspection rights; limits: frozen (not settled) claims, slow consensus, state-led enforcement.

Mission 10

  • Education (British values), sport (all-island rugby) and political parties (SNP; 2014 referendum) reinforce nationalism; legal systems, devolved governance and landscape symbols (the English countryside) anchor identity.
  • The UK is multinational: people hold nested identities, and census results depend on question design (England 2011–2021).

Mission 11

  • UK-made does not mean UK-owned: JLR (Tata), MINI (BMW), EDF (British Energy, Hinkley Point C with CGN); 57.7% of the value of UK quoted shares is held overseas (2022).
  • Westernisation spreads US corporate culture and the capitalist model; foreign ownership of land and landmarks (Qatari Diar) raises identity questions; the Register of Overseas Entities adds transparency.

Mission 12

  • World trade's share of GDP more than doubled to 2008, then stalled; regions that lost from import competition backed nationalist choices more.
  • Disunity takes several forms: independence movements within trading blocs (Scotland), tensions in emerging economies (Indonesia's nickel ban), and fragile states (Somalia).
