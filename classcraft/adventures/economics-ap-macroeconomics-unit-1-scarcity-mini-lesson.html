Mission 1: Even the richest person on Earth cannot have everything. Why not?

Make a guess: Even the richest person on Earth cannot have everything. Why not?

Mission 1

The basic economic problem

  • Scarcity: resources are limited, but wants are unlimited.
  • Economic resources (land, labor, capital, entrepreneurship) are scarce: there is not enough to produce everything people want.
  • Even the richest person has only 24 hours a day, and every resource they use is lost to other uses.
  • So every person, firm and government must choose.
  • Choosing one thing means giving up another: a trade-off.
  • Say: "scarcity" (permanent, everywhere) — not: "shortage" (a temporary lack of one good at its current price).

Mission 1

Opportunity cost

  • Opportunity cost: the value of the next best alternative forgone when a choice is made.
  • Only the next best alternative counts, not every alternative added up.
  • Individual: an evening revising means giving up a paid shift.
  • Government: a budget spent on a new hospital cannot also build a new school.
  • Society: resources used for one good cannot make another.
  • Free to you does not mean no opportunity cost: a free concert ticket still costs your evening; free school meals still use food, cooks and tax revenue.
  • Say: "the next best alternative forgone" — not: "the price paid" or "all the alternatives".
Mission 1

Worked example: the real cost of a summer course

  • Illustrative data. Amounts in any one currency.
  • Maya can take a summer course (fee 1,500) or work a summer job (pay 4,000). The job is her next best alternative.
  • Choosing the course, she gives up the job's pay and whatever the 1,500 fee could have bought.
  • Money measure of the opportunity cost: 1,500 + 4,000 = 5,500.
  • The fee alone (1,500) understates the cost: it ignores the pay she gives up.

Mission 1

Three questions every economy must answer

  • What to produce, and how much of each? (hospitals or roads, food or computers)
  • How to produce? (which mix of workers, machines and land)
  • For whom to produce? (who gets the output: by income, need or another rule)
  • AP wording: what to produce, how to produce it, and who consumes it.
  • Answering them is resource allocation.
  • In markets, prices act as signals and incentives: a rising price tells firms to make more and buyers to use less.
  • Governments also allocate: by planning, taxes and laws. another lesson compares market, planned and mixed economies.

Mission 2

The four factors of production

  • Economic resources (factors of production): the inputs used to produce goods and services.
  • Land: all natural resources: soil, water, minerals, fish, forests.
  • Labor: human effort, physical and mental, used in production.
  • Capital: man-made goods used to produce other goods and services: machines, tools, buildings, roads.
  • Entrepreneurship: organizing the other factors and taking the risk of production.
  • Capital depends on use: a car is capital for a taxi driver, a consumer good for a family.
  • Say: "capital = goods used to make other goods" — not: "capital = money". Money buys capital; it does not produce anything itself.
Quick check

Money is not a factor of production. What counts is what each resource does: natural resources, human effort, man-made tools, and the organizing and risk-taking.

Mission 3

The curve of the possible

  • A production possibility curve (PPC) shows the maximum combinations of two goods an economy can make with its resources and technology.
  • Assumptions: only two goods; a fixed quantity and quality of resources; fixed technology; on the curve, all resources are fully and efficiently used.
  • Resources are limited, so the curve has an edge: scarcity.
  • Picking one point means giving up others: choice and trade-offs.
Mission 3

Worked example: opportunity cost on the curve

  • Illustrative data: an economy makes food and machines.
PointMachinesFood (metric tons)
A0150
B10140
C20120
D3090
E4050
F500
  • Opportunity cost = the next best alternative given up.
  • Step 1: B → C adds 10 machines and loses 140 − 120 = 20 metric tons of food.
  • Step 2: per machine: 20 ÷ 10 = 2 metric tons of food.
  • Step 3: repeat along the curve: 1, 2, 3, 4, then 5 metric tons per machine (E → F).
  • Opportunity cost of 1 machine = food given up ÷ machines gained.
  • Say: "the opportunity cost of 1 machine is 2 metric tons of food" — not: a money price, or "costs 20 machines".

Mission 3

Shape: why the curve bows outward

  • Increasing opportunity cost → the curve is concave to the origin (bowed outward).
  • Why: resources are not equally suited to both goods. The first workers moved to machines are those best at machines and worst at farming. Later ones are expert farmers.
  • Constant opportunity cost → a straight line. Resources are equally good at both goods.
  • Decreasing opportunity cost → the curve is convex to the origin (bowed inward). Rare; e.g. when making more of a good makes each extra unit cheaper in resources.
  • The slope of the curve at any point shows the opportunity cost there.
Mission 3Graph lab

Slide back along the curve

  • Takeaway: rising opportunity cost works in both directions. The more food is made, the more machines each extra metric ton costs. On a straight line the cost is constant.
Mission 3

Inside, on and outside the curve

  • On the curve (A–F): all resources used → productively efficient.
  • Inside (point U): some resources are unemployed or used inefficiently.
  • Inside = underutilized resources; on the curve = efficient.
  • Why an economy can be inside: a recession, unemployed workers, idle factories, strikes, resources stuck in the wrong uses.
  • Outside (point X): unattainable with today's resources and technology.
  • Allocative efficiency: the one point on the curve that best matches what society wants. The curve alone can't show which point that is.
  • From U, more of both goods is possible with no opportunity cost: the resources were idle.
Quick check
?On a PPC for food and machines, an economy is at a point inside the curve. Which statement is correct?
Mission 3

Movements along and shifts of the curve

  • Movement along the curve: resources are reallocated between the goods. The curve stays put.
  • Shift outward: more or better resources, or better technology or productivity (education, training, investment, new discoveries).
  • Shift inward: resources lost or damaged: war, natural disaster, emigration, depleted resources.
  • Skewed (non-parallel) shift: the change affects one good only. A better machine-making method moves the machines end only; the food end stays put.
  • An outward shift is economic growth; an inward shift is contraction.
  • Real case: the 2011 earthquake and tsunami in Japan destroyed buildings, ports, roads and equipment. Japan's government estimated the damage at about 16.9 trillion yen. Lost capital → an inward shift.
  • Say: "a movement along the PPC" (reallocation) — not: "a shift" for a change of mix.
Mission 3Graph lab

Better machines only

  • Takeaway: a skewed outward shift. Each machine now costs half as much food.
Mission 3

More output: two routes

  • Route 1: move from U (inside) to the curve. Output rises by putting underutilized resources to work. The curve does not move.
  • Route 2: economic growth. The curve shifts outward: the economy's capacity rises.
  • Say: "economic growth" for an outward shift of the PPC — not: for a move from inside the curve to a point on it.
  • Both together: the economy reaches a point beyond today's curve.
Mission 3

The PPC and AD/AS

  • LRAS is vertical at full-employment output (Yf). It reflects the economy's maximum sustainable capacity, which matches the PPC.
  • Outward PPC shift ↔ LRAS shifts right.
  • Point inside the PPC ↔ real GDP below Yf.
  • Output below Yf = a recessionary gap.

Mission 4

What is specialization?

  • Specialization: concentrating on a limited range of tasks or products.
  • It happens at every level:
    • Individual: a dentist, an electrician, a coder.
    • Firm: a company that only makes bicycle brakes.
    • Region: a city known for finance or for films.
    • Country: one economy exports coffee, another aircraft parts.
  • Division of labor: splitting the making of one product into separate tasks, each done by a different worker.
  • Division of labor is specialization inside a firm.
Mission 4

Adam Smith's pin factory

  • Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations (1776), described a small pin workshop.
  • Pin-making was split into about 18 distinct operations: one draws the wire, one straightens it, one cuts it, one sharpens the point…
  • 10 workers made upward of 48,000 pins a day: about 4,800 each.
  • Working alone and untrained, Smith wrote, each "certainly could not" have made twenty pins a day.
  • Smith's three reasons output soared:
    • Dexterity: repeating one task builds skill and speed.
    • Time saved: no switching between tasks and tools.
    • Machinery: a narrow task is easier to mechanize, so machines get invented.
  • Real case, 1913: Ford's moving assembly line cut the time to assemble a car from about 12.5 hours to 93 minutes by early 1914.
Mission 4

Worked example: output and cost per unit

A sandwich bar has 4 workers, each paid 12 per hour (illustrative data).

  • Before: each worker makes whole sandwiches: 30 per hour each, so 120 per hour in total.
  • After: one slices, one fills, one wraps, one serves: 200 per hour in total.
  • Step 1: output per worker after = 200 ÷ 4 = 50 per hour.
  • Step 2: labor productivity rises from 30 to 50: (50 − 30) ÷ 30 × 100 = +66.7%.
  • Step 3: labor cost per sandwich before = 12 ÷ 30 = 0.40.
  • Step 4: labor cost per sandwich after = 12 ÷ 50 = 0.24: down 40%.
  • Same workers, same wage, same hours: more output, lower cost per unit.
Quick check
?A bakery has 6 workers, each paid 90 per day. When each worker bakes whole loaves, the bakery makes 120 loaves a day. After it divides the work into separate tasks, the same 6 workers make 180 loaves a day (illustrative data). What is the labor cost per loaf after the change?
Mission 4

Specialize, then exchange

  • A specialist makes far more of one thing than they need, and none of everything else.
  • So specialization only works with exchange: selling what you make to buy what you need.
  • Individual: a nurse sells her labor for a wage, then buys food, rent and transport.
  • National: a country exports what it specializes in and imports the rest.
  • The more people specialize, the more they depend on trade, and on a cheap, trusted way to trade.
  • Countries that specialize by comparative advantage and then trade can consume combinations beyond their own PPC. You'll calculate this in the comparative advantage mission.
Mission check

?A car-repair garage keeps the same 8 mechanics on the same hours. Instead of each mechanic doing whole jobs, each now does one type of job: tires, brakes or servicing. Repairs completed per mechanic rise by a third. Which explanation fits the analysis of the division of labor?

Same workers, same hours, more output: the division of labor raises productivity.

Mission 5

Absolute advantage

  • Absolute advantage: producing more of a good than another country with the same resources.
  • Two countries have the same quantity of resources. Output if all of them go to one good (illustrative data):
Food (metric tons)Cloth (rolls)
Country A600300
Country B240240
  • A can make more food and more cloth: A has absolute advantage in both.
  • Careful with input tables (hours or workers per unit): there, fewer inputs per unit means absolute advantage.
  • So should A make everything itself? Not if it wants the most it can get.
Mission 5

Comparative advantage

  • Comparative advantage: producing a good at a lower opportunity cost than another country.
  • Output table: opportunity cost of 1 unit of X = output of Y ÷ output of X.
Opportunity cost of…1 metric ton of food1 roll of cloth
Country A300 ÷ 600 = 0.5 cloth600 ÷ 300 = 2 food
Country B240 ÷ 240 = 1 cloth240 ÷ 240 = 1 food
  • Food is cheaper (in cloth given up) in A: A has comparative advantage in food.
  • Cloth is cheaper (in food given up) in B: B has comparative advantage in cloth.
  • With different opportunity costs, each country has a comparative advantage in one good.
  • Input table: opportunity cost of 1 unit of X = input per unit of X ÷ input per unit of Y.
  • Same idea for people: a surgeon who types faster than her receptionist still leaves the typing to the receptionist. Each hour typing costs the surgeon an hour of surgery.
  • Say: "lower opportunity cost" — not: "can make more", which is absolute advantage.

Terms of trade that benefit both

  • Terms of trade here: how many units of one good exchange for one unit of the other.
  • A cloth roll costs A 2 food to make at home and costs B 1 food.
  • So any rate between 1 and 2 food per cloth benefits both:
    • A pays less than 2 food for cloth; B gets more than 1 food for each roll it gives up.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Scarcity: limited resources, unlimited wants. It forces choices and trade-offs.
  • Opportunity cost is the value of the next best alternative forgone, even when the price is zero.
  • Every economy must decide what, how and for whom to produce.

Mission 2

  • Four factors: land, labor, capital, enterprise (in your course: entrepreneurship). Capital is man-made goods used in production, not money.

Mission 3

  • A PPC shows the maximum combinations of two goods; on it = productively efficient, inside = idle resources, outside = unattainable.
  • Bowed out = increasing opportunity cost (resources not equally suited); straight line = constant.
  • Bowed in = decreasing opportunity cost.
  • Reallocation = movement along; more or better resources or technology = outward shift (skewed if one good only).

Mission 4

  • Specialization and the division of labor raise labor productivity (Smith: dexterity, time saved, machinery) and cut unit costs.
  • Specialists must exchange; countries that specialize by comparative advantage and trade can consume beyond their own PPC.

Mission 5

  • Absolute advantage: more output from the same resources. Comparative advantage: lower opportunity cost.
  • Specializing by comparative advantage and trading at terms between the two opportunity costs lets both countries consume beyond their own PPC.
