Mission 1: Even the richest person on Earth cannot have everything. Why not?

Make a guess: Even the richest person on Earth cannot have everything. Why not?

Mission 1

The basic economic problem

  • Scarcity: resources are limited, but wants are unlimited.
  • Economic resources (land, labor, capital, entrepreneurship) are scarce: there is not enough to produce everything people want.
  • Even the richest person has only 24 hours a day, and every resource they use is lost to other uses.
  • So every person, firm and government must choose.
  • Choosing one thing means giving up another: a trade-off.
  • Say: "scarcity" (permanent, everywhere) — not: "shortage" (a temporary lack of one good at its current price).
Quick check
?Which statement best describes scarcity as economists use the term?
Mission 1

Is anything not scarce?

  • Economic good: uses scarce resources, so it has an opportunity cost. Almost everything.
  • Free good: needs no scarce resources; more is available than people want, even at a zero price. Zero opportunity cost.
  • Examples: air on a remote mountain, seawater, sunlight.
  • Free of charge is not the same as a free good. A state school uses teachers, buildings and tax revenue: an economic good given at a zero price.
  • Free goods can become scarce: clean air in a polluted city.
  • Established knowledge (a recipe, a formula) is non-rival: one person using it leaves just as much for others. So it may not be scarce, unlike most factors of production.
  • Creating new knowledge still uses scarce resources.
  • Say: "a free good has zero opportunity cost" — not: "a free good is anything with a zero price".
Mission 1

Opportunity cost

  • Opportunity cost: the value of the next best alternative forgone when a choice is made.
  • Only the next best alternative counts, not every alternative added up.
  • Individual: an evening revising means giving up a paid shift.
  • Government: a budget spent on a new hospital cannot also build a new school.
  • Society: resources used for one good cannot make another.
  • Free to you does not mean no opportunity cost: a free concert ticket still costs your evening; free school meals still use food, cooks and tax revenue.
  • Say: "the next best alternative forgone" — not: "the price paid" or "all the alternatives".
Mission 1

Worked example: the real cost of a summer course

  • Illustrative data. Amounts in any one currency.
  • Maya can take a summer course (fee 1,500) or work a summer job (pay 4,000). The job is her next best alternative.
  • Choosing the course, she gives up the job's pay and whatever the 1,500 fee could have bought.
  • Money measure of the opportunity cost: 1,500 + 4,000 = 5,500.
  • Explicit cost: money actually paid out (the 1,500 fee). Implicit cost: value of the resources used that she already owns, here her time (the 4,000 pay forgone).
  • Total economic cost = explicit + implicit = 1,500 + 4,000 = 5,500.
  • Benefit to a consumer is utility; to a firm, total revenue. The course is worth it only if its benefit to Maya is above 5,500.
  • The fee alone (1,500) understates the cost: it ignores the pay she gives up.

Mission 1

Three questions every economy must answer

  • What to produce, and how much of each? (hospitals or roads, food or computers)
  • How to produce? (which mix of workers, machines and land)
  • For whom to produce? (who gets the output: by income, need or another rule)
  • AP wording: what to produce, how to produce it, and who consumes it.
  • Answering them is resource allocation.
  • In markets, prices act as signals and incentives: a rising price tells firms to make more and buyers to use less.
  • Governments also allocate: by planning, taxes and laws. Mission 4 compares market, planned and mixed economies.

Mission 2

The four factors of production

  • Economic resources (factors of production): the inputs used to produce goods and services.
  • Land: all natural resources: soil, water, minerals, fish, forests.
  • Labor: human effort, physical and mental, used in production.
  • Capital: man-made goods used to produce other goods and services: machines, tools, buildings, roads.
  • Entrepreneurship: organizing the other factors and taking the risk of production.
  • Capital depends on use: a car is capital for a taxi driver, a consumer good for a family.
  • Say: "capital = goods used to make other goods" — not: "capital = money". Money buys capital; it does not produce anything itself.

Mission 2

Is every resource scarce?

  • Most factors of production are scarce: land, labor, capital and entrepreneurship all have other uses.
  • Established knowledge (a recipe, a formula) is non-rival: one producer using it leaves just as much for others.
  • So established knowledge may not be scarce. Discovering new knowledge still uses scarce resources.
Mission 2

Worked example: workers or machines?

  • Illustrative data. Amounts in any one currency.
  • A bakery can make 500 loaves a day in two ways:
  • Method A (labor-intensive): 10 workers + 1 machine.
  • Method B (capital-intensive): 2 workers + 4 machines.
  • A machine costs 200 a day.
  • Wage 80 a day: A = 10 × 80 + 1 × 200 = 1,000; B = 2 × 80 + 4 × 200 = 960. Choose B.
  • Wage 60 a day: A = 10 × 60 + 200 = 800; B = 2 × 60 + 800 = 920. Choose A.
  • The cheaper method depends on the relative prices of labor and capital.
  • This is how factor prices guide how goods are produced.

Mission 3

The curve of the possible

  • A production possibility curve (PPC) shows the maximum combinations of two goods an economy can make with its resources and technology.
  • Assumptions: only two goods; a fixed quantity and quality of resources; fixed technology; on the curve, all resources are fully and efficiently used.
  • Resources are limited, so the curve has an edge: scarcity.
  • Picking one point means giving up others: choice and trade-offs.
Mission 3

Worked example: opportunity cost on the curve

  • Illustrative data: an economy makes food and machines.
PointMachinesFood (metric tons)
A0150

  • Opportunity cost = the next best alternative given up.
  • Step 1: B → C adds 10 machines and loses 140 − 120 = 20 metric tons of food.
  • Step 2: per machine: 20 ÷ 10 = 2 metric tons of food.
  • Step 3: repeat along the curve: 1, 2, 3, 4, then 5 metric tons per machine (E → F).
  • Opportunity cost of 1 machine = food given up ÷ machines gained.
  • Say: "the opportunity cost of 1 machine is 2 metric tons of food" — not: a money price, or "costs 20 machines".

?Use the table on the previous screen (illustrative data). The economy moves from point D (30 machines, 90 metric tons of food) to point E (40 machines, 50 metric tons of food). What is the opportunity cost of each extra machine?
Mission 3

Shape: why the curve bows outward

  • Increasing opportunity cost → the curve is concave to the origin (bowed outward).
  • Why: resources are not equally suited to both goods. The first workers moved to machines are those best at machines and worst at farming. Later ones are expert farmers.
  • Constant opportunity cost → a straight line. Resources are equally good at both goods.
  • Decreasing opportunity cost → the curve is convex to the origin (bowed inward). Rare; e.g. when making more of a good makes each extra unit cheaper in resources.
  • The slope of the curve at any point shows the opportunity cost there.
Mission 3Graph lab

Slide back along the curve

  • Takeaway: rising opportunity cost works in both directions. The more food is made, the more machines each extra metric ton costs. On a straight line the cost is constant.
Mission 3

Inside, on and outside the curve

  • On the curve (A–F): all resources used → productively efficient.
  • Inside (point U): some resources are unemployed or used inefficiently.
  • Inside = underutilized resources; on the curve = efficient.
  • Why an economy can be inside: a recession, unemployed workers, idle factories, strikes, resources stuck in the wrong uses.
  • Outside (point X): unattainable with today's resources and technology.
  • Allocative efficiency: the one point on the curve that best matches what society wants. The curve alone can't show which point that is.
  • From U, more of both goods is possible with no opportunity cost: the resources were idle.
Quick check
?On a PPC for food and machines, an economy is at a point inside the curve. Which statement is correct?
Mission 3

Movements along and shifts of the curve

  • Movement along the curve: resources are reallocated between the goods. The curve stays put.
  • Shift outward: more or better resources, or better technology or productivity (education, training, investment, new discoveries).
  • Shift inward: resources lost or damaged: war, natural disaster, emigration, depleted resources.
  • Skewed (non-parallel) shift: the change affects one good only. A better machine-making method moves the machines end only; the food end stays put.
  • An outward shift is economic growth; an inward shift is contraction.
  • Real case: the 2011 earthquake and tsunami in Japan destroyed buildings, ports, roads and equipment. Japan's government estimated the damage at about 16.9 trillion yen. Lost capital → an inward shift.
  • Say: "a movement along the PPC" (reallocation) — not: "a shift" for a change of mix.
Mission 3Graph lab

Better machines only

  • Takeaway: a skewed outward shift. Each machine now costs half as much food.
Mission 3

More output: two routes

  • Route 1: move from U (inside) to the curve. Output rises by putting underutilized resources to work. The curve does not move.
  • Route 2: economic growth. The curve shifts outward: the economy's capacity rises.
  • Say: "economic growth" for an outward shift of the PPC — not: for a move from inside the curve to a point on it.
  • Both together: the economy reaches a point beyond today's curve.

Mission 4

Three questions every economy must answer

  • Resources are scarce, so every society must decide:
  • What to produce (and how much)?
  • How to produce it (which resources, which methods)?
  • For whom to produce it (who gets the output)?
  • An economic system is the way a society answers these questions.
  • It has two parts: who owns the resources, and the coordinating mechanism that links millions of decisions.
  • Systems differ in their institutions (e.g. property rights, rule of law) and their coordinating mechanism (prices or plans).
Mission 4

Free market economy

  • Resources are privately owned.
  • The price mechanism coordinates: prices rise where goods are wanted and scarce, fall where they are not.
  • Firms seek profit; consumers choose what to buy. Spending acts like votes (consumer sovereignty).
  • The state's role is small: protect property rights, enforce contracts, keep law and order.
  • Strengths: incentives to work, invest and innovate; prices carry information quickly; wide choice.
  • Weaknesses: some goods under-provided (e.g. street lighting); pollution ignored; large inequality of income and wealth; booms and slumps.
Mission 4

Planned (command) economy

  • Most resources are owned by the state.
  • A central planning agency answers what, how and for whom: it sets output targets and fixes prices.
  • Strengths: resources can be directed to national priorities (e.g. heavy industry, defense, health); income can be kept more equal; full employment can be a target.
  • Weaknesses: planners lack the information to plan millions of goods; fixed prices bring shortages and queues or surpluses; weak incentives to cut costs or innovate; little consumer choice.
Quick check
?In a planned economy, a central agency sets each factory's output and fixes all prices. A shortage of winter coats appears. What is most likely to happen next under this system?
Mission 4

Mixed economy

  • Both the market (private sector) and the state (public sector) allocate resources.
  • Most goods are bought and sold in markets.
  • The state provides some goods (e.g. defense, roads, often schools and healthcare), taxes, regulates and redistributes income.
  • Every real economy is mixed. They differ in how much the state does.
  • Think of a spectrum: free market at one end, planned at the other. Countries sit in between, and move along it over time.
Mission 4

Real case: planning and reform

  • The Soviet Union ran its economy through central plans, with five-year plans from 1928 until the state broke up in 1991.
  • Planning built heavy industry fast, but consumer goods were often short and queues were common.
  • China began market reforms in 1978. Over the next few years farm households were allowed to keep or sell output above their state quota; private firms were later allowed.
  • The state still owns many large firms in China, alongside a large private sector: a mixed economy.
  • Both show economies moving along the spectrum, not fixed in one box.
Quick check
?In Country Y, most firms are privately owned and most prices are set in markets. The state funds schools and hospitals from taxes and sets a minimum wage. How is Country Y's economy best described?
Mission check

?A sudden frost destroys half of a country's coffee harvest. How would the response most likely differ between a free market economy and a planned economy with fixed prices?

Markets let a price change do the coordinating; plans need someone to notice and decide.

Mission 5

Comparative advantage

  • Comparative advantage: producing a good at a lower opportunity cost than another country.
  • Output table: opportunity cost of 1 unit of X = output of Y ÷ output of X.
Opportunity cost of…1 metric ton of food1 roll of cloth
Country A300 ÷ 600 = 0.5 cloth600 ÷ 300 = 2 food
Country B240 ÷ 240 = 1 cloth240 ÷ 240 = 1 food
  • Food is cheaper (in cloth given up) in A: A has comparative advantage in food.
  • Cloth is cheaper (in food given up) in B: B has comparative advantage in cloth.
  • With different opportunity costs, each country has a comparative advantage in one good.
  • Input table: opportunity cost of 1 unit of X = input per unit of X ÷ input per unit of Y.
  • Same idea for people: a surgeon who types faster than her receptionist still leaves the typing to the receptionist. Each hour typing costs the surgeon an hour of surgery.
  • Say: "lower opportunity cost" — not: "can make more", which is absolute advantage.

Mission 6

What "rational" means

  • Economics models choices as rational: agents weigh benefits against costs and pick the option that best meets their objective.
  • Consumers aim to maximize utility (satisfaction).
  • Firms aim to maximize profit (total revenue − total cost).
  • Costs include explicit costs (money paid out) and implicit costs (what you give up without paying money, such as pay you could have earned).
  • Benefits: utility for consumers; total revenue for firms.
Mission 6

Total and marginal utility

  • Total utility (TU): the whole satisfaction from all units consumed.
  • Marginal utility (MU): the change in TU from one more unit. MU = ΔTU ÷ ΔQ.
  • Slices of pizza at one meal (illustrative data, in "utils"):

  • TU still rises while MU is positive. TU is at its maximum where MU = 0.
  • Say: "marginal utility falls" — not: "total utility falls" (TU rises until MU turns negative).

Mission 6

From diminishing MU to the demand curve

  • A consumer buys one more unit only if its extra satisfaction is worth at least the price.
  • MU falls with each unit, so the most the consumer will pay for each extra unit falls too.
  • To persuade them to buy more, the price must be lower.
  • So quantity demanded rises as price falls: the demand curve slopes downward.
  • Say: "a fall in price causes an increase in quantity demanded" (a movement along D) — not: "an increase in demand".
Quick check
?Which statement best explains, using utility, why a demand curve usually slopes downward?

  • Good X costs $3, good Y costs $1. Income is $9.

Mission 6

Deriving demand from MU/P

  • Now the price of X falls from 3 to 2. MU/P of X rises: 18, 13.5, 9, 4.5.
  • MUx/Px > MUy/Py, so switching spending toward X raises total utility.
  • New optimum: 3 X and 3 Y (3 × 2 + 3 × 1 = 9; MU/P = 9 for both; TU = 117).
  • Two points on the demand curve for X: price 3 → 2 units; price 2 → 3 units.
  • Lower price, larger quantity demanded: a downward-sloping demand curve.
Mission 6

Decide at the margin: MB = MC

  • Marginal benefit (MB): extra benefit from one more unit of an activity. Marginal cost (MC): extra cost of that unit.
  • Rule: do one more unit while MB ≥ MC. Stop where MB = MC.
  • A café decides how many extra hours to open (illustrative data, money values):

Mission 6

Costs that count, and sunk costs

  • Total economic cost (opportunity cost) = explicit cost + implicit cost.
  • Example: a weekend course costs $200 (explicit). You give up $150 of shift pay (implicit). Economic cost = $350.
  • Sunk cost: already paid and cannot be recovered, whatever you choose now.
  • Sunk and fixed costs from past choices do not change MB or MC of the next unit, so they are ignored in the optimal decision.
  • Say: "only costs that change with the choice matter" — not: "I've paid already, so I must use it".
Quick check
?Jo paid $40 for a non-refundable concert ticket. On the night she feels unwell and would enjoy staying home more than going: staying home gives her a benefit worth $30 to her, going gives a benefit worth $10. What should a rational Jo do?
Mission 6

Water and diamonds

  • The paradox of value: water is vital, diamonds are not, yet diamonds cost far more.
  • Water: plentiful. People already use a lot, so the MU of one more liter is tiny.
  • Diamonds: scarce. Few are owned, so the MU of one more diamond is high.
  • Price reflects marginal utility (what the next unit is worth), not total utility.
  • TU from water is enormous. MU of the last liter is small.
Mission 6Evaluate

How useful is the rational model?

  • Utility cannot be measured directly. "Utils" are a teaching device; economists infer preferences from choices.
  • It depends on information: real consumers rarely know every option and price.
  • It depends on effort and stakes: people use habits and shortcuts for small, frequent buys.
  • The model still predicts the direction of many responses well, e.g. buying less when price rises.
Mission check

?A hiker has had no water all day on a hot trail and would pay a large amount for one bottle. At home, after drinking plenty, she would not pay even a small amount for one more bottle. Which explanation is best?

What we pay depends on how much one more unit adds, not on how much the good matters in total.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Scarcity: limited resources, unlimited wants. It forces choices and trade-offs.
  • Opportunity cost is the value of the next best alternative forgone, even when the price is zero.
  • Every economy must decide what, how and for whom to produce.

Mission 2

  • Four factors: land, labor, capital, enterprise (in your course: entrepreneurship). Capital is man-made goods used in production, not money.
  • Rewards: rent, wages, interest, profit.

Mission 3

  • A PPC shows the maximum combinations of two goods; on it = productively efficient, inside = idle resources, outside = unattainable.
  • Bowed out = increasing opportunity cost (resources not equally suited); straight line = constant.
  • Bowed in = decreasing opportunity cost.
  • Reallocation = movement along; more or better resources or technology = outward shift (skewed if one good only).

Mission 4

  • Every economy must answer what, how and for whom; systems differ in ownership and in their coordinating mechanism.
  • Free market: prices and private ownership. Planned: the state owns and plans. Mixed: both; every real economy is mixed.

Mission 5

  • Absolute advantage: more output from the same resources. Comparative advantage: lower opportunity cost.
  • Specializing by comparative advantage and trading at terms between the two opportunity costs lets both countries consume beyond their own PPC.

Mission 6

  • Marginal utility falls as consumption rises; this explains a downward-sloping demand curve.
  • Rational choice: keep going while MB ≥ MC; the best choice is where MB = MC.
  • Utility is maximized when MU/P is equal across goods and all income is spent.
  • Ignore sunk costs; count explicit and implicit costs.
