Statistical distributions · binomial

Discrete random variables

  • A random variable XX takes numerical values by chance. It is discrete if you can list its values.
  • Its probability distribution gives P(X=x)P(X = x) for every value xx, as a table or a formula. The probabilities must add to 1:
∑P(X=x)=1Σ P(X = x) = 1
  • A discrete uniform distribution gives every value the same probability, e.g. the score on a fair die: P(X=x)=61​P(X = x) = 1/6 for x=1,2,…,6x = 1, 2, …, 6.
  • Capital XX is the variable; lower-case xx is a value it takes.
Calculate
?The discrete random variable XX has probability distribution P(X=x)=k(x+2)P(X = x) = k(x + 2) for x=1,2,3,4x = 1, 2, 3, 4. Find P(X≥3)P(X ≥ 3). Give your answer to 3 decimal places.
Statistical distributions · binomial

The binomial distribution

  • X∼B(n,p)X ~ B(n, p) counts the successes in nn trials when:
  • there is a fixed number of trials nn; each trial has two outcomes (success or failure); trials are independent; the probability of success pp is constant.
  • Then, with q=1−pq = 1 − p:
P(X=x)=(xn​)px(1−p)n−x,x=0,1,…,nP(X = x) = C(n, x) p^x (1 − p)^(n − x), x = 0, 1, …, n
  • On a tree diagram every path with xx successes has probability pxqn−xp^x q^(n − x), and there are (xn​)C(n, x) such paths: these are the terms of the expansion of (q+p)n(q + p)ⁿ.
  • In context, state the conditions as assumptions, e.g. "seeds germinate independently of each other".

Statistical distributions · binomial

Cumulative probabilities

  • Your calculator gives P(X≤x)P(X ≤ x) directly. Rewrite every other inequality in that form:
  • P(X<x)=P(X≤x−1)P(X < x) = P(X ≤ x − 1)
  • P(X≥x)=1−P(X≤x−1)P(X ≥ x) = 1 − P(X ≤ x − 1) and P(X>x)=1−P(X≤x)P(X > x) = 1 − P(X ≤ x)
  • P(a≤X≤b)=P(X≤b)−P(X≤a−1)P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = P(X ≤ b) − P(X ≤ a − 1)
  • For example, if X∼B(8,0.4)X ~ B(8, 0.4), then P(X≥3)=1−P(X≤2)=1−0.3154=0.6846P(X ≥ 3) = 1 − P(X ≤ 2) = 1 − 0.3154 = 0.6846.

Statistical distributions · the normal distribution

The normal distribution as a model (A2 only)

  • A normal distribution models a continuous variable with a symmetric bell-shaped curve. Write X∼N(μ,σ2)X ~ N(μ, σ²): the second number is the variance.
  • Mean = median = mode =μ= μ. The points of inflection are at x=μ±σx = μ ± σ.
  • Probability is area under the curve; the total area is 1. P(X=a)=0P(X = a) = 0, so P(X<a)=P(X≤a)P(X < a) = P(X ≤ a).
  • About 68% (two-thirds) of values lie within μ±σμ ± σ, about 95% within μ±2σμ ± 2σ, almost all within μ±3σμ ± 3σ.
Quick check
?X∼N(50,16)X ~ N(50, 16). Where are the points of inflection on the curve of XX?
Statistical distributions · the normal distribution

Standardising and working backwards

  • Any normal variable can be turned into the standard normal Z∼N(0,1)Z ~ N(0, 1):
Z=σX−μ​Z = (X − μ)/σ
  • zz counts standard deviations from the mean. P(Z<z)P(Z < z) is written Φ(z)Phi(z).
  • Your calculator's normal cdf gives probabilities directly. Its inverse normal finds the value with a given area to its left.
  • If μμ or σσ is unknown, standardise and use an inverse normal zz-value: x=μ+zσx = μ + zσ.

Statistical distributions · the normal distribution

Binomial or normal?

  • Binomial B(n,p)B(n, p): a count of successes in a fixed number of independent trials with constant pp. Normal: a continuous measurement, roughly symmetric about its mean.
  • A binomial count is a total of nn small yes/no effects. For large nn with pp near 0.5 its bar chart is close to a bell: B(n,p)≈N(np,np(1−p))B(n, p) ≈ N(np, np(1−p)).
  • A normal model fits badly if the data are strongly skewed or impossible values get real probability.
Calculate
?A machine fills bottles with a mean volume of 500 ml. The volumes are normally distributed and 2% of bottles contain less than 490 ml. Find the standard deviation. Give your answer to 3 significant figures.

Mission check

?Adult heights, test scores and bag masses all make the same bell shape. Why?

Each of these quantities is the result of many small, independent effects adding together (genes and diet, many questions on a test, many tiny variations in a filling machine). Totals like that cluster round the mean in a bell, just as B(n,p)B(n, p) looks like N(np,np(1−p))N(np, np(1−p)) when nn is large. That is why the normal distribution is such a useful model.

Recap

Big ideas

  • A discrete probability distribution lists P(X=x)P(X = x) for each value; the probabilities add to 1.
  • X∼B(n,p)X ~ B(n, p) needs a fixed nn, two outcomes, independent trials and constant pp; P(X=x)=(xn​)px(1−p)n−xP(X = x) = C(n, x)p^x(1 − p)^(n − x).
  • Turn every inequality into P(X≤x)P(X ≤ x) form: P(X≥x)=1−P(X≤x−1)P(X ≥ x) = 1 − P(X ≤ x − 1).
  • X∼N(μ,σ2)X ~ N(μ, σ²): a symmetric bell about μμ, inflection points at μ±σμ ± σ, probability = area. About 68%, 95% and 99.7% of values lie within 1, 2 and 3 standard deviations.
  • Standardise with Z=σX−μ​Z = (X − μ)/σ; use the inverse normal and x=μ+zσx = μ + zσ to find a value, μμ or σσ.
  • Binomial for counts, normal for continuous measurements; B(n,p)B(n, p) looks like N(np,np(1−p))N(np, np(1−p)) for large nn and pp near 0.5.
