Mission 1

Mission 1: Soak a bone in acid and it bends like rubber; bake it and it crumbles. What does that reveal?

Make a guess: Soak a bone in acid and it bends like rubber; bake it and it crumbles. What does that reveal?

Mission 1

Bone is a composite

  • Compact bone = a few cells in a large matrix.
  • The matrix has two parts:
  • Organic (30%): mainly the protein collagen, in fibres.
  • Inorganic (70%): mainly hydroxyapatite, crystals of calcium phosphate (Ca²⁺ and PO4³⁻).
  • Mineral crystals sit on and between the collagen fibres.
  • Two materials, one tissue: a composite, like steel rods in concrete.
Mission 1

What each part does

  • Collagen: high tensile strength; gives flexibility. Bone bends slightly under a load instead of shattering.
  • Hydroxyapatite: hardness and rigidity; resists compression.
  • Hydroxyapatite is also the body's store of calcium and phosphate.
  • Together: stiff enough to support, tough enough not to snap.
  • Acid dissolves the mineral → collagen left → bone bends.
  • Strong heat burns off the collagen → mineral left → bone crumbles.
  • Say: "collagen resists tension and gives flexibility" — not: "collagen makes bone hard".

Mission 1

Bone is alive: three cells

  • Osteoblasts: build bone. They secrete collagen first, then lay down the inorganic calcium phosphate on it.
  • Your spec words this as osteoblasts being "embedded in the matrix". Osteoblasts line the bone surface; once enclosed in matrix they become osteocytes.
  • Osteocytes: osteoblasts that became trapped in the matrix they made. They sit in spaces called lacunae and maintain the bone.
  • Osteoclasts: break down bone matrix, releasing calcium and phosphate into the blood.
  • Bone is broken down and rebuilt all the time: remodelling.
  • Build = break down → bone mass stays the same.
  • Breakdown faster than building → bone mass falls.
Quick check
?An astronaut spends six months in microgravity, where the skeleton carries little load. Osteoclast activity rises while osteoblast activity stays the same. What happens to the astronaut's bones?
Mission 1

The Haversian system

  • Compact bone is built of many Haversian systems (osteons), running along the bone's length.
  • Centre: a Haversian canal with blood vessels and nerves.
  • Around it: rings of matrix, the lamellae.
  • Between the rings: lacunae, each with one osteocyte.
  • Canaliculi: tiny channels joining lacunae to each other and to the canal.
  • Volkmann's canals link neighbouring Haversian canals and the bone surface.
Mission 1

Why build bone like this?

  • The mineral makes bone impermeable: food and oxygen cannot diffuse far through it.
  • Every osteocyte is within reach of a blood vessel: canal → canaliculi → lacuna.
  • Nutrients and oxygen in; carbon dioxide and wastes out.
  • Cylinders of lamellae running along the bone resist compression and bending.
  • Collagen fibres change direction from one lamella to the next, adding strength.
  • Working scientifically. Prepared slides of compact bone:
  • T.S.: circles of lamellae round a dark central canal; lacunae as dark dots; canaliculi as fine lines.
  • L.S.: Haversian canals as long parallel channels; Volkmann's canals crossing them.

Mission 2

What all cartilage shares

  • Cells called chondrocytes, each in a space, a lacuna.
  • They secrete a firm, rubbery matrix: fibres in a gel that holds water.
  • No blood vessels: food and oxygen diffuse in through the matrix.
  • So cartilage repairs slowly after damage.
  • The fibres in the matrix decide what a cartilage can do.
Mission 2

Hyaline cartilage

  • Fine collagen fibres, not visible with a light microscope.
  • Matrix looks smooth and glassy ("hyaline" = glass-like).
  • Found: ends of bones in joints, rings of the trachea, joins ribs to sternum, nose.
  • Most of the foetal skeleton is hyaline cartilage, later replaced by bone.
  • Function: a smooth, low-friction surface; firm support that still flexes.

Mission 2

Five jobs of the skeleton

  • Support: a rigid frame that holds the body up and gives it shape.
  • Muscle attachment: tendons anchor muscles to bones, so contraction moves the body.
  • Protection: skull → brain; rib cage → heart and lungs; vertebrae → spinal cord.
  • Production of red blood cells: in red bone marrow.
  • Calcium store: bone mineral holds most of the body's calcium.
Mission 2

Marrow and the calcium bank

  • In adults, red marrow lies in the spongy bone of flat bones (sternum, ribs, pelvis, vertebrae, skull) and the ends of long bones.
  • It makes red blood cells, and white blood cells and platelets too.
  • Blood calcium low → osteoclasts break down matrix → calcium released into the blood.
  • Blood calcium high → osteoblasts lay calcium down in new matrix.
  • Hormones control the balance, keeping blood calcium steady for nerves and muscles.
Quick check
?For several years, a person's diet contains too little calcium. Blood tests show a normal calcium concentration, but scans show the bones have become less dense. Which function of the skeleton explains this?
Mission check

?A student examines cartilage from an unfamiliar site. The matrix contains thick, parallel bundles of collagen fibres, with few chondrocytes, arranged in rows. Which job suits this tissue best?

Same cells, same gel. The fibres decide the job: fine collagen for smooth joints, elastic fibres for the ear, collagen bundles for the spine.

Mission 3

Two divisions

  • Axial skeleton: the central axis. Skull, vertebral column, ribs, sternum.
  • Appendicular skeleton: the limbs and the girdles that attach them to the axis.
  • Pectoral girdle + forelimbs (arms). Pelvic girdle + hind limbs (legs).
Mission 3

Girdles and limbs

  • Pectoral girdle: scapula (shoulder blade) + clavicle (collarbone), each side.
  • Forelimb: humerus → radius and ulna → carpals → metacarpals → phalanges.
  • Pelvic girdle: two hip bones (each = ilium + ischium + pubis, fused), joined to the sacrum behind.
  • Hind limb: femur → tibia and fibula → tarsals → metatarsals → phalanges. Plus the patella (kneecap).
  • Both limbs follow one plan: 1 bone → 2 bones → many small bones.
Mission 3

Two girdles, two jobs

  • Pectoral girdle: joined to the axial skeleton at only one small joint (clavicle to sternum); the scapula is held by muscles. → Mobility: the arm moves freely.
  • Pelvic girdle: joined firmly to the sacrum. → Stability: it passes the weight of the upper body to the legs.
Quick check
?Which list contains only bones of the appendicular skeleton?
Mission 3

The vertebral column

  • 7 cervical (neck) → 12 thoracic (chest) → 5 lumbar (lower back).
  • Then the sacrum (5 fused vertebrae) and the coccyx (a few small fused vertebrae).
  • Intervertebral discs of cartilage between the vertebrae absorb shock and allow bending.
  • Functions: supports the head and trunk; protects the spinal cord; allows movement; anchors muscles and ribs.
Mission 3

A typical vertebra

  • Centrum (vertebral body): the solid block that bears weight.
  • Neural arch (vertebral arch): encloses the neural canal (vertebral foramen), which holds and protects the spinal cord.
  • Neural spine (spinous process) and two transverse processes: anchor muscles and ligaments.
  • Articular processes (facets): form joints with the vertebrae above and below.

Mission 4: One bone disease makes bones bend, another makes them snap. What has gone wrong in each?

Make a guess: One bone disease makes bones bend, another makes them snap. What has gone wrong in each?

Mission 4

Three ways bone can fail

  • Recall: matrix = collagen (flexibility) + calcium phosphate (hardness), built by osteoblasts, removed by osteoclasts.
  • Too little mineral on the collagen → soft bone that bends: rickets, osteomalacia.
  • Faulty collagen → brittle bone that snaps: brittle bone disease.
  • Too little bone of normal make-up → porous bone that breaks easily: osteoporosis.
Mission 4

Rickets and osteomalacia

  • Cause: lack of vitamin D or of calcium in the diet.
  • Vitamin D is needed to absorb calcium from the gut. Skin makes it in sunlight; also in oily fish, eggs, fortified foods.
  • Too little calcium → new matrix is not mineralised → bones are soft.
  • Rickets (children, growing bones): bowed legs, swollen wrists and ankles, slow growth.
  • Osteomalacia (adults): bone pain, muscle weakness, fractures.
  • Treat and prevent: vitamin D and calcium supplements; diet; safe sunlight.
Mission 4

Osteoporosis

  • Osteoclasts break bone down faster than osteoblasts rebuild it → bone mass falls.
  • Bone composition is normal; there is just less of it. Spongy bone loses its struts.
  • Causes and risk factors: ageing; fall in oestrogen after the menopause; low calcium or vitamin D; little weight-bearing exercise; smoking; long-term corticosteroid drugs.
  • Symptoms: often none until a bone breaks. Typical breaks: wrist, hip, vertebrae.
  • Collapsed vertebrae → loss of height and a stooped back.
Mission 4

Diagnosing and treating osteoporosis

  • DEXA scan: low-dose X-rays measure bone mineral density (BMD).
  • T-score = (patient's BMD − mean BMD of healthy young adults) ÷ standard deviation of young adults.
  • Treatment: bisphosphonates (e.g. alendronate) slow osteoclasts, so less bone is removed.
  • Calcium and vitamin D supplements; weight-bearing exercise.
  • Hormone replacement therapy for some women after the menopause.
  • Prevent falls: fewer falls, fewer fractures.
Mission 4Read the data

T-scores

WHO criteria (WHO Study Group, 1994):

T-scoreCategory
−1.0 or abovenormal
between −1.0 and −2.5low bone mass (osteopenia)
−2.5 or belowosteoporosis

Illustrative patient: hip BMD = 0.700 g cm⁻². Young-adult mean = 0.940 g cm⁻²; SD = 0.120 g cm⁻².

  • T = (0.700 − 0.940) ÷ 0.120 = −0.240 ÷ 0.120 = −2.0.
  • −2.0 lies between −1.0 and −2.5: low bone mass, not yet osteoporosis.
  • Each 1.0 below zero = one standard deviation below the young-adult mean.

Mission 4

Brittle bone disease

  • Medical name: osteogenesis imperfecta.
  • Cause: inherited mutations, usually in the genes for collagen (type I).
  • Too little collagen, or faulty collagen → bones fracture with very little force.
  • Other signs: blue-grey tinge to the whites of the eyes; short stature; hearing loss; loose joints.
  • No cure. Treatment: bisphosphonates to raise bone density; metal rods inserted in long bones; physiotherapy; careful handling.
  • Say: "faulty collagen makes bone brittle" — not: "too much calcium".

Mission 4

Types of fracture

  • Closed (simple): bone breaks; skin stays intact.
  • Open (compound): broken bone pierces the skin. Risk of infection.
  • Greenstick: bone bends and cracks on one side only. Children: their bones are more flexible.
  • Comminuted: bone shatters into several pieces. High-energy impacts, e.g. road accidents.
  • Stress: tiny cracks from repeated loading, e.g. long-distance running.
  • Causes: a sudden large force (falls, impacts); repeated small forces; or weakened bone, e.g. osteoporosis, where a minor fall breaks a hip.
Quick check
?A 7-year-old falls on an outstretched hand. An X-ray shows the radius bent, with a crack on one side only; the skin is unbroken. Which fracture is this, and why is it common in children?
Mission check

?Scurvy is caused by a lack of vitamin C. Vitamin C is needed by the enzymes that make strong collagen fibres. Children with long-term scurvy can develop weak bones that fracture easily. In the bone, this most closely resembles which disorder?

To diagnose a bone disorder, ask which part has failed: mineral (bends), collagen (snaps) or total bone (porous).

Mission 5

Mission 5: Two diseases both wreck joints. Why is only one treated by calming the immune system?

Make a guess: Two diseases both wreck joints. Why is only one treated by calming the immune system?

Mission 5

Osteoarthritis: worn cartilage

  • Cause: the articular cartilage on the bone ends wears away faster than it is repaired.
  • Bone rubs on bone. The joint space narrows; bone beneath thickens; bony spurs grow at the edges.
  • Risk factors: age, earlier joint injury, obesity (more load), heavy repetitive use, genes.
  • Usually weight-bearing joints: knees, hips; also hands.
  • Symptoms: pain on use, stiffness after rest, a smaller range of movement.
Mission 5

Treating osteoarthritis

  • No cure: worn cartilage does not regrow. Treatment reduces load and pain.
  • Exercise and physiotherapy: stronger muscles support the joint.
  • Weight loss: less load on knees and hips.
  • Painkillers: paracetamol; anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs, e.g. ibuprofen).
  • Steroid injections into the joint for flare-ups.
  • Severe: joint replacement. Worn surfaces replaced with metal, ceramic or plastic parts.
Mission 5

Rheumatoid arthritis: an immune attack

  • Cause: autoimmune. The immune system attacks the body's own synovial membrane.
  • The membrane becomes inflamed and thickened; fluid builds up; the joint swells.
  • Inflammation then erodes cartilage and bone.
  • Typically small joints of hands and feet, on both sides of the body.
  • Long morning stiffness; tiredness. Can start at any adult age.
  • Trigger not fully known: genes plus environment (smoking raises risk).
Mission 5

Treating rheumatoid arthritis

  • Aim: suppress the immune attack early, before joints are destroyed.
  • DMARDs (disease-modifying drugs), e.g. methotrexate: damp down the immune system.
  • Biological drugs, e.g. anti-TNF: block a signalling molecule that drives inflammation.
  • Steroids, short term, to control flare-ups.
  • NSAIDs for pain; physiotherapy; surgery or joint replacement if joints are badly damaged.
  • Say: "rheumatoid arthritis is autoimmune" — not: "wear and tear".

Mission 5

Conditions that affect posture

  • A healthy spine has gentle front-to-back curves and is straight seen from behind.
  • Scoliosis: a sideways curve, often S-shaped. Most often idiopathic (no known cause), starting in adolescence; also from birth defects of vertebrae or muscle and nerve disorders.
  • Kyphosis: an exaggerated outward curve of the upper (thoracic) back: a hunched back. Causes: poor posture; vertebrae collapsed by osteoporosis; abnormal vertebral growth in teenagers.
  • Lordosis: an exaggerated inward curve of the lower (lumbar) back. Causes: poor posture, obesity.
  • Flat feet: the arch of the foot is low or absent. Causes: arches that never develop; weakened tendons and ligaments; obesity. Treatment: supportive shoes or insoles, exercises, physiotherapy; rarely surgery.
  • Treatment: physiotherapy and exercises; a brace while a child is growing; surgery (spinal fusion with metal rods) for severe curves; treat the cause, e.g. osteoporosis.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Compact bone matrix: 30% organic (collagen: tensile strength, flexibility) and 70% inorganic (hydroxyapatite: hardness, compression, calcium store).
  • Osteoblasts build, osteoclasts break down, osteocytes maintain; Haversian canals and canaliculi supply every osteocyte through impermeable matrix.

Mission 2

  • Cartilage: chondrocytes in lacunae, no blood vessels. Hyaline (fine collagen; joint surfaces, trachea), yellow elastic (elastic fibres; pinna, epiglottis), white fibrous (collagen bundles; discs, menisci).
  • The skeleton supports, anchors muscles, protects, makes red blood cells in red marrow and stores calcium.

Mission 3

  • Axial: skull, vertebral column, ribs, sternum. Appendicular: pectoral girdle (scapula, clavicle) + forelimb; pelvic girdle (hip bones) + hind limb.
  • Vertebra: centrum (weight), neural arch (spinal cord), processes (muscles). Cervical: vertebrarterial canals; thoracic: rib facets; lumbar: largest centrum.

Mission 4

  • Rickets (children) and osteomalacia (adults): too little vitamin D or calcium → poorly mineralised, soft bone. Osteoporosis: breakdown outpaces building → less bone; T-score ≤ −2.5. Brittle bone disease: inherited collagen fault.
  • Fractures: closed, open, greenstick, comminuted, stress; caused by large forces, repeated loading or weakened bone.

Mission 5

  • Osteoarthritis: articular cartilage wears away (age, injury, obesity); treat with exercise, weight loss, painkillers, joint replacement. Rheumatoid arthritis: autoimmune attack on the synovial membrane; treat with immune-suppressing drugs (methotrexate, anti-TNF).
  • Posture: scoliosis (sideways), kyphosis (outward upper back), lordosis (inward lower back), flat feet; physiotherapy, bracing, insoles, surgery.
