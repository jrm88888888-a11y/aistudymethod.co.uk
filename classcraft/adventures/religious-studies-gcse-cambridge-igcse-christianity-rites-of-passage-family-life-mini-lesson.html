Mission 1

What baptism is

  • Baptism is the ceremony of initiation: Christians believe it makes a person a member of the Church, the Body of Christ.
  • It uses water, with the words "in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit".
  • Jesus was baptised by John in the River Jordan (Matthew 3:13–17), and he sent his followers out "baptising them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit" (Matthew 28:19).
  • Most churches baptise babies (infant baptism). Some baptise only people old enough to say they believe (believers' baptism).

Mission 1

Into the life of the Trinity

  • Baptism is "in the name of" Father, Son and Holy Spirit: Christians believe the person is joined to God's own life.
  • Father: the baptised become God's adopted children.
  • Son: they share in Jesus' death and resurrection (Romans 6:4).
  • Holy Spirit: they receive the Spirit, who makes them holy.
  • Catholic teaching: baptism washes away original sin and all sin, gives grace, and marks the person for ever, so it is given only once.
  • It is the "door" to the other sacraments: no one receives Confirmation or the Eucharist without being baptised.
Mission 1

Infant baptism: the rite

  • Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican, Methodist and Presbyterian churches baptise babies; usually at a parish service.
  • Welcome: the priest or minister traces the sign of the cross on the baby's forehead.
  • Promises: parents and godparents reject evil and profess the faith for the child; the congregation promises support.
  • Water: poured three times over the head (or the baby is dipped), with the Trinity words: a sign of washing from sin and new birth.
  • Catholic rite: anointing with chrism (perfumed oil: sharing in Christ's role as priest, prophet and king), a white garment (clothed with Christ, cleansed from sin), and a candle lit from the Easter (Paschal) candle (the light of the risen Christ).
  • Orthodox: the baby is immersed three times, then chrismated and given Communion in the same service.
  • Godparents (sponsors) promise to help the child grow in faith; Catholic godparents must be confirmed Catholics aged at least 16.

Mission 1

Why baptise babies?

  • Grace is a gift: God's love comes first; a baby cannot earn it and does not need to.
  • Original sin: Catholics believe baptism frees from original sin, so it should not be delayed (Catholic teaching also entrusts unbaptised children to God's mercy).
  • Whole households were baptised in the early Church (Acts 16:15, 16:33).
  • A covenant sign: Presbyterian and other Reformed Christians compare it to circumcision, given to Jewish baby boys (Colossians 2:11–12).
  • Jesus welcomed children and said the Kingdom belongs to such as these (Mark 10:14).
  • The child later makes the promises personally, at confirmation (see Mission 2).
Mission 1

Believers' baptism

  • Practised by Baptists, most Pentecostals and many independent churches, for people old enough to profess faith (often teenagers or adults).
  • Testimony: the candidate tells the church how they came to faith in Jesus.
  • Immersion: the minister and candidate stand in a baptistery (pool in the church floor), river or sea; the candidate is lowered backwards under the water and raised up.
  • Symbolism: dying to the old life, buried with Christ, rising to new life (Romans 6:4).
  • Reasons: Peter said, "Repent and be baptised" (Acts 2:38): faith comes first; Jesus was baptised as an adult; the New Testament never clearly describes a baby being baptised.
  • For Baptists, baptism is an act of obedience and public witness; it does not itself save.
Mission 1

Dedication

  • Churches that practise believers' baptism often hold a dedication or thanksgiving service for a new baby.
  • No water. The minister prays for the child; parents promise to bring the child up to know Jesus; the church promises support.
  • Model: Hannah dedicates Samuel to God (1 Samuel 1:27–28); Jesus blesses children (Mark 10:16).
  • The child can later choose believers' baptism.
  • The Salvation Army also dedicates children, as it has no sacraments.
Mission 1Diversity

The debate over infant baptism

  • Catholic: baptism is necessary for salvation for those who know the Gospel, and frees from original sin; babies should be baptised soon after birth; unbaptised adults are baptised after preparation, usually at the Easter Vigil.
  • Orthodox: infants receive baptism, chrismation and Communion together: full members from the start.
  • Anglican, Methodist, Presbyterian: infant baptism is a sign of God's grace and covenant; adults who were not baptised as babies are baptised on profession of faith.
  • Baptist, most Pentecostal: only believers should be baptised; many Baptist churches baptise people who were baptised as babies, seeing infant baptism as not yet a baptism of faith.
  • Common ground: almost all churches accept each other's baptism if water and the Trinity words were used (Baptists are the main exception for infant baptism; some Orthodox churches also baptise converts afresh).

Mission 1Evaluate

"Babies should not be baptised."

  • Reasons for: faith should be a free personal choice. The New Testament pattern is "repent and be baptised" (Acts 2:38). Baptism by immersion pictures death and resurrection (Romans 6:4), which a baby cannot understand.
  • Reasons against: grace is God's gift, not a reward for understanding. Households were baptised (Acts 16:33). Original sin (Catholic view) means baptism should not wait. The child can confirm the promises later.
  • Judge it by: Is baptism mainly God's action or the believer's response? What does the New Testament show? Does the community's promise make up for the child's?
Mission 1Worked answer

Two different beliefs

Question: Explain two different beliefs about infant baptism.

  • Belief 1: Catholics believe babies should be baptised. Develop: baptism frees a person from original sin and gives God's grace, which a baby can receive without earning it.
  • Belief 2: Baptists believe only believers should be baptised. Develop: baptism is a public sign of a person's own faith, so it must follow repentance, as in Acts 2:38.

Mission 2

Why a second step?

  • In churches that baptise babies, parents and godparents make the promises.
  • Later, the person makes those promises for themselves: a rite of passage from childhood faith to adult faith.
  • In Catholic, Anglican, Lutheran and Methodist churches this is confirmation.
  • "Confirm" has two senses: the person affirms their faith, and, Christians believe, God strengthens them with the Holy Spirit.
  • Afterwards the person is a full member of the church.
Mission 2Read the source

Laying on of hands

  • In Acts 8:14–17, Philip has baptised people in Samaria. The apostles Peter and John come, pray for them, and lay their hands on them, and they receive the Holy Spirit (paraphrase).
  • In Acts 19:5–6, Paul lays hands on newly baptised believers in Ephesus and the Spirit comes on them (paraphrase).
  • Confirmation follows this pattern: baptism first, then laying on of hands and the gift of the Spirit.

Mission 2

Catholic confirmation

  • A sacrament of initiation (with baptism and the Eucharist). Usually given by the bishop, often to young people aged about 11–16.
  • Renewal of baptismal promises: candidates reject sin and profess the faith themselves.
  • Laying on of hands: the bishop extends his hands over the candidates and prays for the Holy Spirit.
  • Anointing with chrism: the bishop traces a cross on each forehead with chrism (perfumed oil) and says, "Be sealed with the Gift of the Holy Spirit."
  • Sponsor: stands behind with a hand on the candidate's shoulder; often a godparent; must be a confirmed Catholic.
  • Many candidates take a confirmation name, a saint as a model.
  • Catholics believe it gives the gifts of the Holy Spirit (wisdom, understanding, counsel, fortitude, knowledge, piety, fear of the Lord) and is given only once.
Mission 2

First Holy Communion

  • Catholic children usually make their First Holy Communion at about 7 or 8, the "age of reason".
  • Before it they make their first confession (sacrament of Reconciliation).
  • Months of preparation in parish and school; the families take part.
  • On the day, often in white clothes, children receive the host for the first time at Mass, then celebrate.
  • Meaning: they now share fully in the Eucharist, "the source and summit" of Catholic life.
  • In the Orthodox Church there is no separate First Communion: babies receive from their baptism.

Mission 2

Anglican and Methodist confirmation

  • Church of England / Church of Ireland: the bishop confirms. Candidates answer questions about their faith in front of the congregation, then the bishop lays hands on each one.
  • The Book of Common Prayer (1662) prays: "Defend, O Lord, this thy Child with thy heavenly grace, that he may continue thine for ever".
  • In some Anglican parishes children now receive Communion before confirmation; confirmation is still the adult commitment.
  • Methodist Church: the minister lays hands on candidates, who are then received into membership of the Methodist Church.
  • Officially these churches do not count confirmation among the two sacraments of the Gospel (Article XXV); it is a rite of commitment and blessing (many Anglo-Catholics still see it as sacramental).
Mission 2Diversity

Different routes into full membership

  • Catholic: baptism → First Communion → confirmation (in some dioceses confirmation now comes before First Communion).
  • Orthodox: baptism, chrismation (anointing with holy oil called myron) and Communion together, in infancy.
  • Anglican, Lutheran, Methodist: infant baptism → confirmation by bishop or minister.
  • Presbyterian: infant baptism → profession of faith before the elders (Kirk Session) → communicant member.
  • Baptist, Pentecostal: dedication → believers' baptism → received into membership, often welcomed with the "right hand of fellowship".
  • Quakers: no ceremony; people apply for membership and the meeting discerns together.

Mission 2

Why full membership matters

  • Belonging: the person is a full part of the Body of Christ in that local church.
  • Rights: e.g. receiving Communion (Presbyterian), voting in church meetings (Baptist), being a godparent or sponsor (Catholic).
  • Responsibilities: regular worship, prayer, giving, serving others, sharing the faith.
  • Support: the community promises to support members; members support each other.
  • Sponsors model the faith, pray for the candidate and help them keep their promises after the ceremony.
Mission 2Evaluate

"Confirmation should only happen when people are adults."

  • Reasons for: a promise for life needs adult maturity. Teenagers may be confirmed because of family pressure or the party. Adults who choose freely may keep their promises better.
  • Reasons against: Catholics and Orthodox see confirmation as God's gift of the Spirit, not a reward for maturity. Young people need the Spirit's strength while they grow. Orthodox churches give chrismation to babies.
  • Judge it by: Is confirmation mainly God's action or the person's promise? What does Acts 8 show? Can a teenager make a genuine commitment?
Mission 2Worked answer

Why confirmation matters

Question: Explain why confirmation is important for many Christians.

  • Point: it is the moment people make the baptismal promises for themselves. Develop: their faith becomes their own choice, not only their parents'.
  • Point: Catholics believe it gives the Holy Spirit's strength. Source: Acts 8:14–17, where the apostles lay hands on the baptised and they receive the Spirit. Develop: so the bishop lays hands on candidates and anoints them with chrism.
  • Point: it brings full membership. Develop: the person can play a full part in church life.

Mission 3

Marking life's turning points

  • A rite of passage marks a move from one stage of life to another.
  • Christians mark marriage and death in church, in front of God and the community.
  • Each rite's words and actions show what Christians believe: about love, promises, death and resurrection.
  • Churches share a core, but differ in details: Catholic, Orthodox and Protestant rites look different.
Mission 3Read the source

Two become one

  • Ephesians 5:31–33 (paraphrase): a man will leave his father and mother and be joined to his wife, and the two will become one flesh (quoting Genesis 2:24). Paul calls this a great mystery that points to Christ and the Church. Each husband must love his wife as himself, and the wife must respect her husband.
  • Christians read this as showing that marriage is a lifelong union, modelled on Christ's self-giving love.
  • Christians differ on the roles in the wider passage (5:21–33): some see distinct roles for husband and wife; many stress verse 21, "submit to one another" (paraphrase), as equal mutual love.

Mission 3

The wedding service

  • Welcome and purpose: the minister explains why marriage matters: a gift of God for love, companionship, and the family.
  • Declarations: each says they come freely to marry ("I will").
  • Vows: promises for life, made before God. Book of Common Prayer (1662): "to have and to hold from this day forward, for better for worse, for richer for poorer, in sickness and in health, to love and to cherish, till death us do part".
  • Rings: an unending circle: love without end and a sign of the vows.
  • Declaration and blessing: the minister declares them married and blesses them; the couple and witnesses sign the marriage document (in England and Wales).
  • A Catholic wedding may take place within a Nuptial Mass (with the Eucharist) or in a service of the Word without it, often when one partner is not Catholic.
Mission 3

Sacrament or holy covenant?

  • Catholic: marriage is a sacrament. The couple themselves give the sacrament to each other; the priest or deacon is the Church's witness. A valid sacramental marriage cannot be dissolved.
  • Orthodox: a sacrament (Holy Mystery); the priest joins the couple by crowning them.
  • Most Protestants (Anglican, Methodist, Baptist and others): marriage is holy, a covenant blessed by God, but not a sacrament (Article XXV lists it among rites "commonly called" sacraments).
  • All agree: marriage is meant to be a lifelong, faithful commitment before God (Mark 10:9); they differ on divorce and remarriage.
Mission 3

The Orthodox crowning

  • Betrothal: the priest blesses the rings, and they are exchanged three times between bride and groom.
  • Crowning: crowns (Greek stefana) are held or placed on their heads: they become king and queen of a new home, and are called to the self-giving of the martyrs, whose crowns are also a sign of victory.
  • Common cup: they drink from one cup of wine: a shared life, joys and sorrows.
  • Procession: the priest leads them three times around the table, the first steps of their married life together.
  • Traditionally no spoken vows: the couple's consent is asked, and the priest's prayers and the crowning make the marriage.

Mission 3

Preparing for death

  • Catholic: three sacraments may be given near death, often called the "Last Rites".
  • Reconciliation (confession): forgiveness of sins.
  • Anointing of the Sick: the priest anoints forehead and hands with blessed oil, following James 5:14–15 (the elders anoint the sick and pray for them).
  • Viaticum: a last Communion, "food for the journey" to God.
  • Orthodox: Holy Unction (anointing) and Communion.
  • Anglicans and other Protestants: the minister visits, prays, reads Scripture (often Psalm 23) and may give Communion; anointing is used in some churches.
  • Purpose: peace, forgiveness, strength and hope for the dying person, and support for the family.
Mission 3

The funeral

  • Catholic (Requiem Mass): the coffin is sprinkled with holy water, covered with a white pall, and placed beside the Paschal (Easter) candle; readings, homily, incense, and the Final Commendation of the person to God.
  • Anglican and Protestant: opening words of hope from John 11:25; hymns, Bible readings, a tribute or eulogy, prayers, thanksgiving for the person's life.
  • Orthodox: open coffin, candles, the hymn "Memory Eternal"; mourners give a last kiss of farewell.
  • Committal at the grave or crematorium. BCP 1662: "earth to earth, ashes to ashes, dust to dust; in sure and certain hope of the Resurrection to eternal life". Mourners may throw a handful of earth on the coffin.
  • Grief is real (Jesus wept, John 11:35), but Christians mourn with hope.
Mission 3Read the source

Hope at the graveside

> "I am the resurrection and the life. He who believes in me will still live, even if he dies." (John 11:25)

  • Jesus says this to Martha before raising her brother Lazarus.
  • 1 Corinthians 15:51–52 (paraphrase): Paul says not all will die, but all will be changed, "in the twinkling of an eye", when the dead are raised to a life that cannot decay.

Mission 3

Burial or cremation?

  • Christians traditionally buried their dead, as Jesus was buried (Matthew 27:59–60), showing respect for the body and hope of resurrection.
  • Catholic: cremation allowed since 1963, provided it is not chosen to deny the resurrection; since 2016 the ashes should be kept in a cemetery or other sacred place, not scattered or kept at home.
  • Orthodox: most Orthodox Churches do not allow cremation; burial is the norm.
  • Most Protestants: both burial and cremation are acceptable; God can raise the dead whatever happens to the body.
  • At an interment the coffin is lowered and earth thrown in; at a cremation the curtain closes or the coffin is withdrawn; ashes may later be buried.
Mission 3

Supporting the bereaved

  • During the funeral: the community gathers, grieves together, gives thanks, and hears the hope of the resurrection.
  • Afterwards: visits from the priest or minister, meals, bereavement groups, remembering the person on anniversaries.
  • Catholic: Masses offered for the dead; All Souls' Day (2 November); prayers for those in purgatory.
  • Orthodox: memorial services on the 3rd, 9th and 40th days and on anniversaries; kolyva (boiled wheat, a sign of resurrection, John 12:24) is shared.
  • Most Protestants give thanks for the person's life but do not pray for the dead (some Anglicans do).
Quick check
?Why do Catholic funerals include prayers for the person who has died, while most Protestant funerals do not?
Mission 3Evaluate

"Funerals are more for the living than for the dead."

  • Reasons for: tributes, hymns and meals comfort the grieving. Protestants who do not pray for the dead see the funeral mainly as thanksgiving and support. The hope of resurrection is preached to those still alive.
  • Reasons against: Catholics and Orthodox pray for the dead person and commend them to God. The rites honour the body as created by God and awaiting resurrection. The Last Rites are entirely for the dying person.
  • Judge it by: Can prayer help the dead? Does a funeral express belief about the person, or support for the mourners, or both?
Mission check

The rites at the end of life echo the rites at its start: love promised for life, and death faced in the hope of resurrection.

Mission 4

Why family matters in all three faiths

  • All three see the family as God-given: the first place where faith is lived and taught.
  • Christians and Jews share the command "Honour your father and your mother" (Exodus 20:12).
  • Muslims: the Qur'an commands kindness to parents (Qur'an 17:23).
  • Christianity: Catholics call the family the domestic church ("church of the home").
  • Islam: the family is the building block of the ummah (worldwide Muslim community); marriage is strongly encouraged.
  • Judaism: the home is often called a mikdash me'at ("small sanctuary"); Shabbat begins at the family table.
  • Each faith teaches about four areas: roles of husband and wife, nurture (bringing up children), care of the elderly, and keeping marriage strong.
Mission 4

Christian roles of men and women

  • Equal dignity: "God created man in his own image… male and female he created them." (Genesis 1:27)
  • Ephesians 5: "Wives, be subject to your own husbands, as to the Lord." (5:22) · "Husbands, love your wives, even as Christ also loved the assembly and gave himself up for her" (5:25).
  • The passage opens with "subjecting yourselves to one another" (5:21).
  • Christians read these verses in two main ways:
  • Complementarian: equal in worth, different in role; the husband leads the family in loving, self-giving service.
  • Egalitarian: 5:21 is the key; husband and wife share leadership. "There is neither male nor female; for you are all one in Christ Jesus." (Galatians 3:28)
  • Catholic teaching: man and woman are equal in dignity and complementary; both share responsibility for the family.
Mission 4

Christian nurture and care of the elderly

  • Nurture: at infant baptism, parents and godparents promise to bring the child up in the faith.
  • "Train up a child in the way he should go, and when he is old he will not depart from it." (Proverbs 22:6)
  • Fathers: "don't provoke your children to wrath, but nurture them in the discipline and instruction of the Lord" (Ephesians 6:4).
  • In practice: prayer at home, church, Sunday school, Christian schools, preparing for First Communion and Confirmation.
  • Care of the elderly: "if anyone doesn't provide for his own, and especially his own household, he has denied the faith" (1 Timothy 5:8).
  • Catholic teaching: grown children owe their parents material and moral support in old age, illness and loneliness.
  • Many churches also run lunch clubs, visiting and care homes for older people.
Mission 4

Christian marriage, divorce and remarriage

  • Marriage is a lifelong, faithful union; Jesus: "What therefore God has joined together, let no man separate." (Mark 10:9)
  • Matthew adds an exception: divorce "except for sexual immorality" (Matthew 19:9).
  • Catholic: marriage is a sacrament and cannot be dissolved. A civil divorce does not end it in Church law. A church tribunal may declare a marriage null (annulment: it was never valid), and then a church wedding is possible.
  • Orthodox: marriage is a sacrament, but the Church may allow a second (rarely a third) marriage, with a quieter, penitential service.
  • Church of England: since 2002, a divorced person may remarry in church, at the priest's discretion.
  • Methodist and many other Protestants: divorce is a sad last resort; remarriage is allowed, stressing forgiveness and a fresh start.

Mission 4

Muslim family life in brief

  • Marriage: "He ordained between you love and mercy." (Qur'an 30:21, Pickthall)
  • Roles: Qur'an 4:34 calls men qawwamun over women: "in charge of" (Pickthall) or "protectors and maintainers" (Yusuf Ali), "because they spend of their property" (Pickthall) for the family. Many Muslims read this as the husband's duty to provide.
  • Men and women have equal spiritual worth and equal reward (Qur'an 33:35).
  • Nurture: a good name, love, teaching the Qur'an and salah; the Prophet said every person is a shepherd responsible for those in their care (Sahih al-Bukhari, paraphrase).
  • Elderly: "My Lord! Have mercy on them both as they did care for me when I was little." (Qur'an 17:24, Pickthall)
  • Divorce is allowed but disliked: talaq (by the husband), khul' (a woman's right to start a divorce, usually returning the mahr), then remarriage after the waiting period (iddah). (Detail: the Muslim rites mission.)
Mission 4

Jewish roles, nurture and the elderly

  • Your spec writes G-d, a form many Jews use out of respect.
  • Orthodox: roles are different but equal in value. Women are exempt from many time-bound commandments, such as wearing tallit and tefillin; a reason often given is to free them for the home and children.
  • Women lead many home mitzvot: lighting Shabbat candles, keeping a kosher kitchen.
  • On Friday night many husbands sing Eshet Chayil: "A woman of valour who can find? for her price is far above rubies." (Proverbs 31:10, JPS 1917)
  • Nurture: "thou shalt teach them diligently unto thy children" (Deuteronomy 6:7, JPS 1917): Shabbat, festivals, Hebrew school, Bar/Bat Mitzvah.
  • Elderly: "Thou shalt rise up before the hoary head, and honour the face of the old man" (Leviticus 19:32, JPS 1917). The Talmud teaches that honouring parents includes feeding, clothing and helping them (paraphrase).
Mission 4

Jewish marriage, divorce and remarriage

  • Marriage is a mitzvah and a holy bond (kiddushin, "set apart").
  • Divorce is permitted, but sad: "For I hate putting away, saith the LORD" (Malachi 2:16, JPS 1917).
  • The Torah describes a husband writing "a bill of divorcement" (Deuteronomy 24:1, JPS 1917). This is the get, given before a beth din (rabbinic court).
  • Orthodox: without a get the couple are still married in Jewish law, whatever a civil court says. A wife whose husband refuses is an agunah ("chained woman") and cannot remarry in an Orthodox synagogue.
  • Reform and Liberal: most accept that a wife can be freed without the husband's cooperation; their courts can release her, or a civil divorce is enough.
  • Remarriage is allowed for both after a get. In Orthodox law, a kohen (priestly family) may not marry a divorced woman (Leviticus 21:7).
  • In England and Wales, a 2002 law lets a court delay a civil divorce until the get has been given.

Mission 4Diversity

Tradition and change

  • Reasons some communities keep traditional roles:
  • Scripture seems to set them out (Ephesians 5:22; Qur'an 4:34; Proverbs 31).
  • Roles are seen as complementary, not unequal: each partner has a God-given strength.
  • Clear roles give children stability and protect family time (e.g. Shabbat, family prayer).
  • Tradition links each generation to the faith of its parents.
  • Reasons some communities support change:
  • Equality in God's image (Genesis 1:27; Galatians 3:28; Qur'an 33:35).
  • Old roles fitted old economies; today many families rely on two incomes, and women are educated alongside men.
  • Role models in the tradition: Khadijah, the Prophet's first wife, ran a trading business; Deborah judged Israel (Judges 4).
  • Justice: Reform Judaism's equal ketubah; Christian egalitarians; Muslim scholars reading the texts in their historical setting.
  • Christian examples: many evangelical churches and the Catholic Church stress complementary roles; Quakers, Methodists and many Anglicans share leadership.
  • Jewish examples: Orthodox communities keep distinct roles; Reform and Liberal Judaism count women in a minyan and ordain women rabbis.
  • Muslim examples: many Muslims keep the husband as main provider; in many other Muslim families, in Muslim-majority countries and elsewhere, both partners earn and share childcare.
Mission 4

The strain of social change

  • Work and care: when both parents work, believers may feel guilt about less time for children or for placing a parent in a care home.
  • Generations: children growing up in a secular culture may question faith or roles their parents and grandparents hold.
  • Faith communities abroad: families who move country can feel torn between home customs and new norms.
  • Divorce: a Catholic who divorces and remarries without an annulment may feel cut off from Communion; an agunah suffers years of waiting; a Muslim woman seeking khul' may face family pressure.
  • Identity: some feel that changing roles betrays the faith; others feel that keeping them betrays its justice.
  • Communities respond with prayer, pastoral care from priests, imams and rabbis, family counselling and support groups.

Mission 4Evaluate

"Religious families should keep traditional roles for men and women."

  • Reasons for: scripture appears to set out roles (Ephesians 5:22–25; Qur'an 4:34). Roles are complementary, not unequal. Stability for children and care for the elderly at home. Keeps continuity with the faith of earlier generations.
  • Reasons against: equality before God (Genesis 1:27; Galatians 3:28; Qur'an 33:35). Role models such as Khadijah and Deborah. Texts were written for different societies. Fixed roles can trap women in unhappy or unjust situations (e.g. the agunah).
  • Another view: keep the values (love, faithfulness, care for young and old) while letting the roles flex.
  • Judge it by: What does each tradition count as essential? How should ancient texts be applied today? Do the roles serve the family's wellbeing?
  • Part (c), 8 marks: a paragraph for each view, each backed by evidence, then a clear, reasoned judgement of your own.

Mission 5

Agape: love that gives

  • Agape (Greek): selfless, unconditional love that seeks the good of others, expecting nothing back.
  • Christians see it in Jesus: healing, feeding, forgiving, and giving his life.
  • The Two Greatest Commandments: love God "with all your heart… soul… mind, and… strength", and "You shall love your neighbour as yourself." (Mark 12:30–31)
  • The Good Samaritan shows who my neighbour is: anyone in need, even an enemy. "Go and do likewise." (Luke 10:37)
  • Christian charity work begins here: love of God shown as love of neighbour.

Mission 5

Christian Aid

  • What it is: the aid and development agency of many British and Irish churches (Protestant, Anglican, Orthodox and others); founded 1945 to help refugees in post-war Europe; named Christian Aid in 1964.
  • What it does: emergency relief (floods, earthquakes, war); long-term development (farming, health, income); works through local partner organisations; campaigns on the causes of poverty (debt, unfair tax, climate change).
  • It helps people of any faith or none, and does not try to convert them.
  • Christian Aid Week (May): door-to-door collections by local church volunteers.
  • Why: love of neighbour (Mark 12:31); Christ in the needy (Matthew 25:40); every person made in God's image; justice (Micah 6:8). Its motto: "We believe in life before death."
Mission 5

Tearfund

  • What it is: a UK evangelical Christian relief and development agency, founded 1968 (originally the Evangelical Alliance Relief Fund).
  • How it works: mainly through local churches in poor communities. Churches are trained to look at their own needs and resources, then act together (church and community mobilisation).
  • What it does: disaster response; clean water, sanitation and hygiene; food and livelihoods; peace-building; campaigns on climate change and waste.
  • Integral mission: care for the whole person, body and spirit; where welcome, local churches share their faith as well as practical help.
  • Why: following Jesus, who preached good news to the poor and healed the sick (Luke 4:18); "let's not love in word only… but in deed and truth" (1 John 3:18).
Mission 5

CAFOD and Caritas

  • CAFOD (Catholic Agency for Overseas Development): official aid agency of the Catholic Church in England and Wales, founded 1962; grew out of Family Fast Days started by Catholic women in 1960.
  • Family Fast Day: eat a simple meal and give what you save (Lent and autumn).
  • Works with local Catholic and other partners: emergencies, health, water, farming, education; campaigns for justice.
  • Helps people regardless of religion.
  • Caritas Internationalis: the worldwide confederation of national Catholic charities (CAFOD is the member for England and Wales; SCIAF for Scotland, Trócaire for Ireland).
  • Why: Catholic social teaching: human dignity, the common good, solidarity, and a preferential option for the poor.

Mission 5

Giving time, work and money; local groups

  • Time: volunteering, e.g. at a night shelter or visiting the lonely.
  • Work: careers as service: nursing, teaching, aid work, missionary medicine.
  • Money: many Christians give regularly; some tithe (a tenth of income, from Malachi 3:10). "God loves a cheerful giver." (2 Corinthians 9:7)
  • Local and national groups (examples from the UK): church-run food banks; the St Vincent de Paul Society (Catholic, founded 1833); the Salvation Army (1865), with hostels and family support.
  • Motives: agape; Matthew 25; Jesus' mission "to preach good news to the poor" (Luke 4:18). Historically, missionaries founded hospitals and schools alongside churches.
Mission 5Diversity

Different responses to the teaching on charity

  • Body and soul: many evangelicals (e.g. Tearfund) stress meeting spiritual and physical needs together; Christian Aid and CAFOD give help with no religious message attached. Catholic teaching says charity must never be used to win converts: love is freely given (Benedict XVI, Deus Caritas Est 31, 2005, paraphrase).
  • Charity or justice? Some focus on direct giving; others say the causes of poverty must be changed too: unfair trade, debt, climate (campaigns by Christian Aid and CAFOD).
  • Faith and works: Catholics teach that salvation is God's gift of grace, received in a faith that must show itself in works of love; many Protestants stress salvation by grace through faith alone, with good works as its fruit (Ephesians 2:8–10). Both see charity as essential to Christian life.
  • "The least of these": most read it as anyone in need; some scholars read it as Jesus' followers in need.
  • Public or private? Jesus said give in secret (Matthew 6:3–4); others value public campaigns and events that raise awareness.

Christians differ on whether to share faith in words alongside help, but most see practical love for anyone in need as serving Christ.

Mission 6

Made in the image of God

"God created man in his own image. In God's image he created him; male and female he created them." (Genesis 1:27)

  • Latin: imago Dei, "image of God".
  • Not a physical likeness: Christians believe God has no body.
  • It means humans share, in a small way, some of God's qualities: reason, free will, conscience, creativity, and the ability to love and to know God.
  • Male and female are made in God's image equally.

Mission 6

Dominion or stewardship?

  • Dominion: rule or authority over nature (Genesis 1:26, 28).
  • Historian Lynn White (1907–1987) argued in 1967 that reading dominion as a licence to exploit helped cause the ecological crisis.
  • Stewardship: looking after something that belongs to someone else, and answering to them for it.
  • "The earth is the LORD's" (Psalm 24:1): humans are not the owners.
  • Most Christians today read dominion as stewardship: rule as God would, like a good shepherd, not a tyrant.
  • So Christians are stewards of the Earth for God, responsible for caring for what God made.

Mission 6

Caring for creation is loving your neighbour

  • Pope Francis, Laudato Si' (2015): care for "our common home".
  • Pollution and climate change hit the poorest hardest, and harm future generations.
  • So caring for the environment is a concrete way to "love your neighbour" (Mark 12:31).
  • Local: parishes and schools cut energy and waste, plant gardens, recycle.
  • National: Christians campaign and write to MPs for fair climate policies.
  • Global: agencies such as CAFOD and Caritas support sustainable farming and clean water.
  • Other examples: Christian conservation charities, such as A Rocha, protect habitats and wildlife.
Mission 6Diversity

How Christians see our place in creation

  • Stewardship (Catholic, Anglican, Methodist, many evangelicals): humans care for creation on God's behalf.
  • Human needs first: some conservative Christians stress dominion and human development; environmental policy must not make the poor poorer.
  • Orthodox: humans as "priests of creation", offering the world back to God in thanksgiving. Ecumenical Patriarch Bartholomew is called the "Green Patriarch".
  • Kinship with creation (Franciscan): St Francis of Assisi (c. 1181–1226) praised "Brother Sun" and "Sister Moon".
  • All agree that creation is good and humans are accountable to God for it.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Baptism is the sacrament (or ordinance) of initiation, with water and the name of the Trinity.
  • Infant baptism stresses God's grace, original sin (Catholic) and the covenant; believers' baptism stresses personal faith and the dying-and-rising picture of immersion.
  • Godparents promise to support a baptised child.
  • Dedication blesses a baby without water.

Mission 2

  • Confirmation lets people baptised as babies make the promises themselves and, Catholics believe, seals them with the Holy Spirit (Acts 8:14–17).
  • Catholic rite: bishop, renewal of promises, laying on of hands, chrism, sponsor; Anglicans and Methodists confirm by laying on of hands.
  • Other routes to full membership: Orthodox chrismation in infancy; Presbyterian profession of faith; Baptist believers' baptism.

Mission 3

  • Christian weddings: vows (or, in Orthodox weddings, crowning), rings and blessing show lifelong love modelled on Christ and the Church (Ephesians 5:31–33); Catholics and Orthodox call marriage a sacrament.
  • Preparation for death: Reconciliation, Anointing of the Sick and Viaticum (Catholic); prayer and Scripture (Protestant).
  • Funerals proclaim the resurrection (John 11:25; 1 Corinthians 15:51–52); Christians differ on cremation and on praying for the dead.

Mission 4

  • Christianity, Islam and Judaism all see family as God-given: roles, nurture of children, care of the elderly and faithful marriage.
  • Divorce: Catholic annulment only; Orthodox and many Protestants allow remarriage; Islam has talaq and khul'; Judaism needs a get (Orthodox: no get, an agunah).
  • Some communities keep traditional roles (scripture, complementarity, stability); others support change (equality in God's image, new economies, role models); change brings emotional and spiritual strain.

Mission 5

  • Agape and the Two Greatest Commandments (Mark 12:30–31) motivate Christian charity.
  • So does the Sheep and the Goats (Matthew 25:31–46): Christians read it as teaching that serving people in need is serving Christ.
  • 1 Corinthians 13: giving without love is empty.
  • Christian Aid (1945), Tearfund (1968), CAFOD (1962) and Caritas: relief, development and campaigning, for people of any faith.

Mission 6

  • Humans are made in God's image (imago Dei, Genesis 1:27): this gives every person equal dignity, free will and responsibility.
  • Genesis 1:28 gives dominion; Genesis 2:15 asks humans to "cultivate and keep" the garden. Most Christians read dominion as stewardship.
  • Catholics see care for creation as love of neighbour, carried out locally, nationally and globally.
