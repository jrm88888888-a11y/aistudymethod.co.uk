Mission 1

Mission 1: The Islamic calendar starts with a journey, not a birth. What made one escape so important?

Make a guess: The Islamic calendar starts with a journey, not a birth. What made one escape so important?

Mission 1

Makkah around 570 CE

  • Makkah: a trading town in a dry valley in western Arabia, ruled by the Quraysh tribe.
  • At its centre stood the Ka'bah. Muslims believe Ibrahim and Isma'il built it for the worship of the one God.
  • By the 6th century it was filled with idols of many tribal gods, and pilgrims to them brought trade.
  • Muslims call this time Jahiliyyah: the "age of ignorance".
  • Tribe and family decided who was protected; the poor, orphans and slaves had few rights.
Mission 1

An orphan called al-Amin

  • Muhammad was born in Makkah around 570 CE, into the Hashim clan of the Quraysh.
  • His father died before he was born; his mother died when he was about six.
  • He was raised by his grandfather, then by his uncle Abu Talib.
  • He worked as a shepherd, then as a trader on caravan journeys to Syria.
  • People called him al-Amin: "the trustworthy". Makkans left valuables with him for safe keeping.
  • He worked for Khadijah, a respected merchant; she proposed marriage, and they married when he was 25.
Mission 1Read the source

"Read!": the first revelation

  • Around 610 CE, aged 40, Muhammad often went to pray and reflect in the cave of Hira' on a mountain near Makkah.
  • One night in Ramadan, Muslims believe, the angel Jibril (Gabriel) came to him and commanded him to read or recite.

> "Read: In the name of thy Lord Who createth, Createth man from a clot. Read: And thy Lord is the Most Bounteous, Who teacheth by the pen, Teacheth man that which he knew not." (Surah 96:1–5, Pickthall)

Q1 Who does the passage say is the source of human knowledge?

Show answer

God, "thy Lord", who teaches by the pen.

Q2 Why might Muslims see "Read!" as a fitting first word of the Qur'an?

Show answer

The word Qur'an means "recitation"; the message is to be read and passed on.

Mission 1

After the cave: fear, comfort, a calling

  • Muhammad ran home shaking and asked to be wrapped up. Khadijah comforted him: God would not disgrace a man so kind and honest (paraphrase of a hadith in Sahih al-Bukhari).
  • Her Christian cousin Waraqah ibn Nawfal said the same angel who came to Moses had come to him.
  • After a pause, revelations continued for the rest of his life: about 23 years.
  • Significance: for Muslims, this is the start of Risalah in its final form: God speaks again to humanity, through a final messenger.
  • For Muslims, this shows the message came from God, not from Muhammad: he was afraid and did not seek it.

Mission 1

The Hijrah, 622 CE

  • People from Yathrib, a town about 450 km north, accepted Islam and promised to protect the Prophet (the pledges of Aqabah).
  • Muslims moved there in small groups. The Quraysh plotted to kill him: "they plot, but Allah (also) plotteth" (Surah 8:30, Pickthall).
  • They reached Quba near Yathrib, where the first mosque was built, then entered the town.
  • Yathrib became Madinat al-Nabi, "the city of the Prophet": Madinah.
  • Hijrah means "migration". The migrants are the Muhajirun; the Madinans who welcomed them are the Ansar ("helpers").
Mission 1

Why the Hijrah still matters

  • Calendar: the Islamic calendar counts from 622: year 1 AH (anno Hegirae, "in the year of the Hijrah"). The caliph Umar chose it.
  • Why not his birth or the first revelation? The Hijrah began the first Muslim community (ummah) living by God's guidance.
  • Turning point: from a persecuted minority to a community with its own mosque, leaders and laws.
  • Example: Muslims see it as a model of sacrifice: leaving home, wealth and family for God.
  • Brotherhood: the Ansar shared homes and wealth with the Muhajirun; many Muslims point to this when they welcome refugees today.
Mission check

?A Muslim charity in Britain names its project for welcoming refugees "Ansar". What does this name show about how Muslims understand the Hijrah?

The Hijrah turned a persecuted group into a community. That is why the Islamic calendar begins there. A later mission: Madinah, and the Seal of the Prophets.

Mission 2: Muslims call Muhammad the "Seal of the Prophets". Why does being the last prophet matter so much?

Make a guess: Muslims call Muhammad the "Seal of the Prophets". Why does being the last prophet matter so much?

Mission 2

The Seal of the Prophets

> "Muhammad is not the father of any man among you, but he is the messenger of Allah and the Seal of the Prophets; and Allah is ever Aware of all things." (Surah 33:40, Pickthall)

  • A seal closes and completes a document. Muslims understand the Seal of the Prophets (Khatam an-Nabiyyin) as:
  • Final: no prophet will come after him.
  • Complete: the Qur'an completes God's guidance; it does not need adding to.
  • Confirming: he brings the same message as all earlier prophets, especially Tawhid (one God), and so fulfils their teaching.
  • Universal: earlier prophets came to their own peoples; he was sent "unto all mankind" (Surah 34:28, Pickthall).
  • Implications: Sunni and Shi'a Muslims agree that any later claim to be a prophet is false. The Shi'a Imams are guides, not prophets: they bring no new revelation.
Mission 2Read the source

A messenger, not more

> "O Prophet! Lo! We have sent thee as a witness and a bringer of good tidings and a warner. And as a summoner unto Allah by His permission, and as a lamp that giveth light." (Surah 33:45–46, Pickthall)

  • He is fully human. Muslims honour him, but never worship him; they add "peace be upon him" after his name.

Q1 Name two roles 33:45–46 gives the Prophet.

Show answer

Any two: witness, bringer of good news, warner, one who calls to God, a lamp giving light.

Q2 Why does a "lamp" suit a messenger rather than God?

Show answer

A lamp gives light that comes from elsewhere: he passes on God's guidance.

Mission 2

A role model: the Sunnah

> "Verily in the messenger of Allah ye have a good example for him who looketh unto Allah and the Last Day, and remembereth Allah much." (Surah 33:21, Pickthall)

  • His way of life is the Sunnah; reports of it are the Hadith.
  • Examples Muslims learn: he mended his own clothes and shoes, helped at home, ate simply, was gentle with children and animals, and forgave enemies at Makkah.
  • Today: Muslims copy his prayer and fasting, his greetings and manners, and his care for the poor.
  • Significance: he shows how to live the Qur'an in every part of life.
Mission 2Evaluate

"Muhammad is the most important prophet in Islam."

  • For: he is the Seal: the final, complete message, sent to all people. The Qur'an is the only revealed book Muslims believe is unchanged. His Sunnah guides daily life and is named in the Shahadah.
  • Against: "We make no distinction between any of them" (2:136). All prophets taught the same Tawhid. Ibrahim is father of the prophets and is blessed in every salah; Adam was the first.
  • Another view: no distinction in faith (all are true messengers), but a difference in role: "some of whom We have caused to excel others" (2:253).
  • Judge it by: Important in what way: truth, role or example? Does "final" mean "greatest"? What does the Shahadah suggest?
Mission check

?A new group claims its founder received a fresh revelation from God in 2020, adding to the Qur'an. How would most Sunni and Shi'a Muslims respond?

For Muslims, the Seal means the message is complete. That is why Muslims look to the Qur'an and the Prophet's example, not to new prophets.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Muslims believe the Qur'an is God's own words. How does that change the way it is read and kept?

Make a guess: Muslims believe the Qur'an is God's own words. How does that change the way it is read and kept?

Mission 3

What the Qur'an is

  • Qur'an means "recitation": it is meant to be heard and recited aloud.
  • Muslims believe it is the word of God (Allah), in Arabic, revealed through the angel Jibril to the Prophet Muhammad from 610 to 632 CE.
  • It is the last of the revealed books (Kutub), and Muslims believe it is the only one kept exactly as revealed.
  • 114 surahs (chapters) made of ayat (verses; the word also means "signs").
  • After the short opening surah, al-Fatihah, surahs run roughly from longest to shortest, not in the order they were revealed.
  • Makkan surahs stress belief: one God, prophets, judgement. Madinan surahs add guidance for the community: worship, family, law.
Mission 3Read the source

Not his own words

> "Nor doth he speak of (his own) desire. It is naught save an inspiration that is inspired" (Surah 53:3–4, Pickthall)

  • The first words revealed were "Read: In the name of thy Lord Who createth" (Surah 96:1, Pickthall), in the cave of Hira' (see Mission 1).

Q1 According to 53:3–4, where do the Prophet's words in the Qur'an come from?

Show answer

Not from his own wishes: from God's inspiration.

Q2 Why does this matter for the Qur'an's authority?

Show answer

If the words are God's, they carry God's authority, not a human teacher's.

Mission 3

The Night of Power and a gradual revelation

  • Muslims believe the revelation began on Laylat al-Qadr, the Night of Power, in Ramadan: "Lo! We revealed it on the Night of Predestination." (Surah 97:1, Pickthall)
  • "The Night of Power is better than a thousand months." (Surah 97:3, Pickthall)
  • After that night, it came in parts over about 23 years, often in answer to events, to strengthen the Prophet's heart (Surah 25:32).
  • The Prophet recited each revelation; companions memorised it and scribes wrote it down on parchment, palm leaves and bone.

?Which statement about how the Qur'an was revealed matches Muslim belief?
Mission 3

Why the Qur'an has supreme authority

  • God's own words: for Muslims, not a book about God, but God speaking. It is the highest source of guidance and of Shari'ah law.
  • Unchanged: Muslims believe it is preserved exactly (15:9): the final, undistorted message.
  • Beyond imitation: "though mankind and the jinn should assemble to produce the like of this Qur'an, they could not" (Surah 17:88, Pickthall). Muslims see its language as a miracle (i'jaz).
  • In Arabic: "a Lecture in Arabic, that ye may understand" (Surah 12:2, Pickthall). A translation is treated as an interpretation of the meaning, not the Qur'an itself.
  • Complete: it confirms the earlier books and needs no addition (see Mission 4).
  • Significance: Muslims judge every other source, including the Hadith, by the Qur'an.
Quick check

Mission 3

The Qur'an in daily life

  • Formal worship: al-Fatihah is recited in every unit (rak'ah) of salah; the Friday sermon is built on the Qur'an.
  • Informal worship: personal reading and recitation; verses used in du'a (personal prayer) and for comfort; many learn it by heart and become a hafiz.
  • Home: read daily by many families; recited at a birth, a marriage and a death; verses in calligraphy on walls.
  • Mosque: children learn to read and recite it at madrasah; in Ramadan many Sunni mosques recite the whole Qur'an over the nights of tarawih prayer.
  • Guidance: for decisions about family, money, food and conduct; with the Sunnah, the basis of Shari'ah.
Mission 3

Treated with respect

  • Many Muslims make wudu (washing) before touching the Arabic Qur'an. They point to "Which none toucheth save the purified" (Surah 56:79, Pickthall). Some scholars read this verse as about angels and the heavenly book; most still see wudu as the respectful way.
  • It is kept on a high shelf, often wrapped in cloth, with nothing placed on top.
  • It is read on a stand (rahl), not on the floor; it is not taken into a bathroom.
  • Recitation begins by seeking God's protection from Satan (Surah 16:98).
  • Worn-out copies are not thrown away: they are buried or respectfully disposed of.
  • Many scholars apply these rules to the Arabic text more strictly than to translations.

Mission 3Evaluate

"Reciting the Qur'an in Arabic matters more than understanding it."

  • For: the Arabic words are God's own; translation loses meaning and beauty. Recitation is itself worship (Surah 73:4: "chant the Qur'an in measure", Pickthall). One Arabic Qur'an unites Muslims everywhere.
  • Against: it was revealed "that ye may understand" (12:2). "Will they then not meditate on the Qur'an" (47:24). Guidance only guides if it is understood; it is meant to change how people live.
  • Another view: most Muslim scholars say both matter: recite in Arabic and learn its meaning, through study, tafsir and translation.
  • Judge it by: What is the Qur'an for: worship, guidance, or both? Can a translation carry God's words? What do Muslims in different communities actually do?
Mission check

?Yusuf reads an English translation every day to understand it, but recites only in Arabic in salah. A friend says: "Then the English book is your Qur'an too." Which reply fits most Muslims' belief?

Because Muslims believe the Arabic words are God's own, the Qur'an is recited, kept and obeyed in ways no other book is.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Muslims believe God gave books to Musa, Dawud and Isa too. Why do they follow only the Qur'an?

Make a guess: Muslims believe God gave books to Musa, Dawud and Isa too. Why do they follow only the Qur'an?

Mission 4

One religion since Adam

  • Muslims believe Islam ("submission" to God) is not new: it is the original religion, taught by every prophet from Adam onwards.
  • "Lo! religion with Allah (is) the Surrender (to His Will and Guidance)." (Surah 3:19, Pickthall)
  • Ibrahim "was not a Jew, nor yet a Christian; but he was an upright man who had surrendered (to Allah)" (Surah 3:67, Pickthall): a hanif, a pure believer in one God.
  • God commended the same religion to Nuh, Ibrahim, Musa and Isa: "Establish the religion, and be not divided therein" (Surah 42:13, Pickthall).
  • So belief in the earlier books (Kutub) is one of the six articles of faith.
Mission 4

Five books, five messengers

  • Sahifah (Scrolls) → Ibrahim (the Qur'an also links scrolls to Musa).
  • Tawrat (Torah) → Musa (Moses).
  • Zabur (Psalms) → Dawud (David).
  • Injil (Gospel) → Isa (Jesus).
  • Qur'an → Muhammad, the last.
  • In English: the Scrolls of Abraham, the Torah, the Psalms, the Gospel.
  • Muslims understand the Injil as the message God revealed to Isa, not the four Gospels later written about him.
  • Muslims believe each was God's word for its time and people; the Qur'an is for all people and all time.
Mission 4Read the source

"Guidance and a light"

> "Lo! We did reveal the Torah, wherein is guidance and a light" (Surah 5:44, Pickthall)

> "We bestowed on him the Gospel wherein is guidance and a light, confirming that which was (revealed) before it in the Torah" (Surah 5:46, Pickthall)

Q1 What two words does the Qur'an use for both the Torah and the Gospel?

Show answer

"Guidance" and "a light".

Q2 How does each book relate to the one before it?

Show answer

It confirms it: the Gospel confirms the Torah, as the Qur'an later confirms both.

Mission 4

The Scrolls (Sahifah)

> "Lo! This is in the former scrolls. The Books of Abraham and Moses." (Surah 87:18–19, Pickthall)

  • The Sahifah are the earliest revealed writings the Qur'an names. Muslims believe they are lost: no copy exists.
  • The Qur'an says something of what they taught:
  • Success comes to those who grow in purity and pray; "the Hereafter is better and more lasting" (Surah 87:14–17, Pickthall).
  • "No laden one shall bear another's load", and each person has only what they strive for (Surah 53:38–39, Pickthall).
  • Significance: for Muslims, even the oldest revelation taught the same message: one God, personal responsibility, judgement.
Mission 4

Why "distorted"?

  • Muslims believe the original books were true revelation. Over time, they hold, the message was changed (tahrif: alteration).
  • The Qur'an speaks of some who "change words from their context and forget a part" of what they were taught (Surah 5:13, Pickthall), and of those who "write the Scripture with their hands and then say, 'This is from Allah'" (Surah 2:79, Pickthall).
  • Two Muslim views:
  • The text was changed: words were added, lost or altered, so today's Bible is not the original revelation. This is the most widely held view today.
  • The meaning was twisted: the words survive in large part, but were misread or misapplied. Some classical scholars held this.
  • Jews and Christians reject this claim: they hold that their scriptures were carefully and faithfully passed on.
Mission 4

The Qur'an as final and "watcher"

> "And unto thee have We revealed the Scripture with the truth, confirming whatever Scripture was before it, and a watcher over it." (Surah 5:48, Pickthall)

  • Confirming: Muslims believe the Qur'an agrees with the true core of every earlier book.
  • Watcher (muhaymin: guardian, judge): where earlier texts differ from it, Muslims follow the Qur'an.
  • Final and undistorted: Muslims believe God has guarded the Qur'an from change (Surah 15:9), so no further book is needed.
  • 5:48 also says God gave each community "a divine law and a traced-out way", and tells all to "vie one with another in good works" (Pickthall).

Mission 4Diversity

What authority do the earlier books have today?

  • Shared by Sunni and Shi'a Muslims: belief in the original books is part of faith; they are honoured; the Qur'an is the final authority.
  • Most Muslims: do not use the Bible for guidance: the Qur'an contains all they need, and the Sahifah are lost.
  • Some Muslim scholars: study the Bible to understand Jews and Christians, and look for teachings they believe survive from the original revelation.
  • In interfaith dialogue: many Muslims stress shared prophets and values with Jews and Christians, the "People of the Scripture": "our Allah and your Allah is One" (Surah 29:46, Pickthall).
  • Some Muslims are wary of reading the Bible, fearing confusion between true and changed teaching.

Mission 4Evaluate

"The earlier holy books are of no importance to Muslims today."

  • For: the Qur'an is final, complete and unchanged; most Muslims believe today's texts are altered; the Sahifah are lost. Muslims do not use them for law or worship.
  • Against: belief in the Kutub is an article of faith. The Qur'an calls them "guidance and a light" and confirms them. They show God has always guided humanity, and they build bridges with Jews and Christians.
  • Another view: they matter for belief (God sent them) and for history, but not for practice (the Qur'an guides daily life).
  • Judge it by: "Importance" for what: faith, guidance or relations with others? Does belief that they were changed remove all their value? What do Muslims actually do with them?
Mission check

?Hannah, a Christian, shows her Muslim friend Zain an old manuscript of the Psalms in a museum. She asks if he believes the Psalms come from God. What would most Muslims say?

Muslims believe in all of God's books, but follow the one they believe God has kept unchanged: the Qur'an.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Sunni and Shi'a Muslims both honour Ali. Why did one question in 632 divide them?

Make a guess: Sunni and Shi'a Muslims both honour Ali. Why did one question in 632 divide them?

Mission 5

632: who leads now?

  • The Prophet died in June 632. He had been prophet, judge and leader of the Muslim community (ummah).
  • No new prophet would come (see Mission 2). But who would lead?
  • One answer: the community chooses the best-qualified Muslim. Those who followed this became the Sunni Muslims: "people of the Sunnah and the community".
  • Another answer: God had already chosen a leader from the Prophet's family, through the Prophet: his cousin and son-in-law Ali. His supporters were the Shi'at Ali, "the party of Ali": Shi'a Muslims.
  • At first the question was about leadership. Over time it shaped belief, law and worship.
Mission 5

Abu Bakr chosen

  • While Ali and the Prophet's family prepared his burial, leaders of Madinah met at the Saqifah (a meeting hall).
  • Umar pledged loyalty (bay'ah) to Abu Bakr; most of those present followed. Abu Bakr became the first caliph (khalifah: successor).
  • Sunni view: a right choice by consultation (shura): Abu Bakr was the Prophet's closest friend, his companion in the cave, and led the prayers in the Prophet's last illness.
  • Shi'a view: the meeting ignored the Prophet's choice. Ali and the family were absent.
  • Ali later gave his pledge to Abu Bakr: Sunni sources present this as agreement; Shi'a sources, as accepting what he could not change, for the sake of unity.
Mission 5

Ghadir Khumm: two readings

  • March 632: returning from the Farewell Pilgrimage, the Prophet stopped at the pool (ghadir) of Khumm, took Ali's hand and said: for whoever I am his mawla, Ali is his mawla (paraphrase of a hadith in Sunni and Shi'a collections).
  • Mawla can mean "master, leader" or "friend, ally".
  • Shi'a reading: "master": a public appointment of Ali as leader after him. This is nass: explicit designation of the next Imam, by God's command through the Prophet.
  • Sunni reading: "friend": the Prophet answered complaints about Ali and told Muslims to love and support him; it was not about the caliphate.
  • Shi'a Muslims celebrate the day as Eid al-Ghadir.

Mission 5

Two traditions today

  • Sunni Muslims (about 87–90% of Muslims): leadership by the best-qualified, chosen by the community; authority in the Qur'an, the Sunnah and the scholars.
  • Shi'a Muslims (about 10–13%): the imamate (Imamah): leadership belongs to Imams from the Prophet's family, appointed by God through nass, beginning with Ali.
  • Role of the Imam (Shi'a): to guide the community without error, to interpret the Qur'an, to keep the Prophet's teaching pure (more in Mission 6).
  • Significance: for Shi'a Muslims, Imamah is a root of faith (Usul ad-Din); Shi'a groups include Twelvers (the largest), Isma'ilis and Zaydis (whose view of the imamate is less strict).
  • Shared: one God, the Qur'an, the Prophet; prayer, fasting, zakah and Hajj (Sunni Muslims call these acts, with the Shahadah, the Five Pillars). Sunni Muslims also love the Prophet's family: every salah sends blessings on "Muhammad and the family of Muhammad".
Quick check
?Which belief is held by Shi'a Muslims but not by Sunni Muslims?
Mission 5Evaluate

"The split between Sunni and Shi'a Muslims was only about politics."

  • For: it began with a practical question: who leads the community? Both share the Qur'an, the Prophet, prayer, fasting, zakah and Hajj. Ali himself worked with the first three caliphs.
  • Against: for Shi'a Muslims the leader is chosen by God, and Imamah is a root of faith. The two traditions developed different hadith collections, schools of law and practices (e.g. Ashura). Readings of the Qur'an (33:33) and Hadith (Ghadir Khumm) differ.
  • Another view: it began as a question of authority, which in Islam is both religious and political, so the two cannot be separated.
  • Judge it by: Is choosing a leader a religious question in Islam? How much do the differences affect worship and belief today? How do Sunni and Shi'a Muslims describe the split themselves?
Mission check

?Sunni and Shi'a students plan a joint assembly about Ali ibn Abi Talib. Which statement could both groups share without disagreement?

Both traditions honour Ali. They divided over whether the Prophet's successor was chosen by the community or appointed by God.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Shi'a Muslims follow twelve Imams, and every mosque has an imam. Is that the same word for the same job?

Make a guess: Shi'a Muslims follow twelve Imams, and every mosque has an imam. Is that the same word for the same job?

Mission 6

One word, two meanings

  • Imam (Arabic) means "one who stands in front": a leader.
  • Every mosque has an imam: the person who leads the prayer and serves the community. Sunni and Shi'a mosques both have one.
  • For Shi'a Muslims, the Imams (capital I) are something more: leaders chosen by Allah from the Prophet's family to guide the ummah after him.
  • Belief in these Imams, Imamah (the imamate), is one of the Shi'a five roots of faith.
  • So the same word can mean a local prayer leader or a divinely chosen guide.
Mission 6

The imamate: who guides after the Prophet?

  • Shi'a Muslims believe Allah would not leave the community without a guide after the Seal of the Prophets.
  • No new prophet comes, but each age needs a guide to explain the Qur'an without error.
  • So Allah appointed Imams from the Ahl al-Bayt (the Prophet's household), beginning with Ali, his cousin and son-in-law.
  • Role: to lead, to interpret the Qur'an and Sunnah, and to be a perfect example.
  • Significance: the imamate continues the guidance of prophethood. Sunni Muslims see leadership as chosen by the community, not a root of faith (see Mission 5).
Mission 6Read the source

Ghadir Khumm and "leaders who guide"

  • In 632, returning from his Farewell Pilgrimage, the Prophet stopped at the pool (Ghadir) of Khumm. He took Ali's hand and said: "Of whomever I am mawla, Ali is his mawla" (Hadith of the pond of Khumm, paraphrase).
  • Shi'a reading: mawla means master and guardian; this was Ali's public appointment as leader.
  • Sunni reading: most Sunni scholars accept the hadith but read mawla as friend and supporter: an honour for Ali, not a succession.
  • Allah told Ibrahim: "I will make thee an Imam to the Nations." (Qur'an 2:124, Yusuf Ali) Ibrahim asked for Imams from his offspring too; Allah replied that His promise does not reach wrongdoers (paraphrase).

Q1 In 2:124, who makes someone an Imam?

Show answer

Allah: Shi'a Muslims see the imamate as a divine appointment.

Q2 Why do Sunni and Shi'a Muslims read Ghadir Khumm differently?

Show answer

They understand "mawla" differently: guardian and leader, or friend.

Mission 6

What makes an Imam

  • Nass (designation): each Imam is named by the one before, by Allah's command. The community does not elect him.
  • Inerrancy ('ismah; OCR: infallible; EDXI: sinless): Allah protects the Imams from sin and from error in teaching. Shi'a scholars cite Qur'an 33:33: Allah wishes to remove all abomination from "ye members of the Family" (Yusuf Ali), read as the Ahl al-Bayt.
  • Knowledge: the Imams inherit the Prophet's knowledge and are the true interpreters of the Qur'an.

Mission 6

The Twelve Imams and the Hidden Imam

  • Ali (d. 661): the first Imam; his sermons and letters are collected in Nahj al-Balagha.
  • Husayn (3rd), killed at Karbala in 680: his stand against injustice is remembered at Ashura.
  • Ja'far al-Sadiq (6th, d. 765): a great teacher; Twelver law is called the Ja'fari school after him.
  • Ali al-Rida (8th, d. 818): his shrine at Mashhad, Iran, is a major place of pilgrimage.
  • Muhammad al-Mahdi (12th), born 869: Twelvers believe Allah hid him (the occultation), that he is alive and that he will return with Isa (Jesus) to fill the earth with justice.
Mission 6

Twelvers and Seveners today

  • Twelvers (the large majority of Shi'a): while the Imam is hidden, senior scholars (mujtahids; the most senior, marja', "source to follow") guide believers in his place.
  • Scholars disagree how far this reaches: some say into government (Iran's system of rule by a scholar); others say mainly religious guidance (many scholars in Najaf, Iraq).

Mission 6Diversity

imam and Imam side by side

  • Sunni imam: chosen by the community · leads prayer, preaches, teaches · an ordinary believer with knowledge · many in every generation.
  • Shi'a prayer leader: the same role in Shi'a mosques, usually a trained scholar.
  • Shi'a Imam: appointed by Allah through nass (designation by the Imam before) · sinless and without error · the true interpreter of the Qur'an · twelve for most Shi'a (Twelvers), the last alive but hidden; a living Imam for most Isma'ilis (a smaller Shi'a group).
  • Different: for Shi'a Muslims, leadership of the ummah is a matter of faith (a root); for Sunni Muslims, a matter of practice and community choice.
Mission 6Evaluate

"The Imams are the most important source of guidance for Shi'a Muslims today."

  • For: Imamah is a root of faith. The Imams' sayings fill Shi'a hadith collections and shape Ja'fari law. Twelvers believe the Hidden Imam is alive and watches over the community. For most Isma'ilis the living Imam guides each age.
  • Against: the Qur'an is the word of Allah and comes first for all Muslims. The Prophet's Sunnah comes before the Imams' teaching. For Twelvers, day-to-day guidance now comes from scholars, because the Imam is hidden.
  • Another view: for Shi'a Muslims the sources do not compete: the Imams are the guardians of the Qur'an's meaning (the Hadith al-Thaqalayn joins "the Book" and "the family").
  • Judge it by: Which source settles disagreements? Does "guidance" mean law, example or spiritual help? Do Twelvers and Isma'ilis answer differently?
  • 12-mark tip: argue both sides with developed reasons and a source, then reach a justified conclusion of your own.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Muhammad (born c. 570 in Makkah) was an orphan, a trader and known as al-Amin, "the trustworthy".
  • In 610, in the cave of Hira', Muslims believe Jibril brought the first revelation: "Read!" (Surah 96:1–5).

Mission 2

  • Muslims believe Muhammad is the Seal of the Prophets (33:40): the final prophet, confirming all earlier prophets and sent to all people.
  • His Sunnah makes him the model for Muslim life (33:21); traditions differ on how his teaching was passed on.

Mission 3

  • Muslims believe the Qur'an is God's word in Arabic, revealed through Jibril to Muhammad from 610 to 632, beginning on the Night of Power.
  • For Muslims it has supreme authority; it shapes worship, home life and moral choices; it is handled with special respect.

Mission 4

  • Muslims believe Islam is the original religion: God sent the same message to every prophet, with books for some: Sahifah (Ibrahim), Tawrat (Musa), Zabur (Dawud), Injil (Isa), Qur'an (Muhammad).
  • Most Muslims believe the earlier books were changed (tahrif); the Qur'an confirms what is true in them and is the final, undistorted "watcher" (5:48).
  • Muslims differ on how far the earlier books matter today: for belief and dialogue, if not for daily guidance.

Mission 5

  • In 632 the question "who leads?" had two answers: chosen by the community (Sunni: Abu Bakr, then Umar, Uthman, Ali) or appointed by God from the Prophet's family (Shi'a: Ali, by nass at Ghadir Khumm).

Mission 6

  • Every mosque has an imam: a prayer leader chosen for knowledge and character, with no priestly status.
  • Shi'a Muslims believe in the Imamate: Imams appointed by Allah (nass), free from sin and error, beginning with Ali at Ghadir Khumm; for Twelvers the twelfth is hidden and will return.
