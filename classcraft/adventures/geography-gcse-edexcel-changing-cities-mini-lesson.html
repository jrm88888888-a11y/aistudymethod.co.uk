Make a guess: In 1979 Shenzhen was what its government calls "a small backward border town". By 2024 it had 17.98 million residents. Where did they all come from?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel GCSE Geography A (1GA0)

  • 4.1 contrasting urbanisation trends over the last 50 years in developed, emerging and developing countries
  • 4.1 how and why urbanisation happened at different times and rates, and its effects
Mission 1

Urbanisation

  • Urbanisation: a rising share of a country's people living in towns and cities.
  • Urban growth: a rising number of urban people.
  • Cities grow in three ways:
    • natural increase: more births than deaths;
    • migration into the city;
    • reclassification: villages and towns grow big enough to be counted as part of a city.
Mission 1

The world since 1950

  • 1950: 20% of the world's 2.5 billion people lived in cities.
  • 2025: 45% of 8.2 billion lived in cities and 36% in towns.
  • Using each country's own definition of "urban", the world's urban share rose from 37.4% in 1974 to 57.6% in 2024.
Mission 1

Contrasting trends, 1974 to 2024

Urban share of the population, by income group:

  • High income: 69.2% → 80.1%
  • Upper-middle income: 28.8% → 67.8%
  • Lower-middle income: 22.9% → 41.6%
  • Low income: 18.6% → 34.7%
  • Developed UK: 77.3% → 83.2%. Emerging India: 21.0% → 35.4%. Developing Ethiopia: 9.2% → 23.6%.
Mission 1

Different times, different speeds

  • High-income countries urbanised first, with industrialisation. On the UN's new city definition, their city populations passed their rural populations by 1957.
  • Low-income countries started later: on the UN definition they reached urban–rural parity only in 2017. (On national definitions, they are still mostly rural.)
  • Urban population growth in 2024:
    • low income 3.99% a year · lower-middle 2.27% · upper-middle 0.90% · high income 0.84%.
  • Fast growth comes from high birth rates and migration; slow growth where most people already live in cities.
  • In some high-income countries people later moved out of cities, to suburbs (suburbanisation) or the countryside (counter-urbanisation); parts of Europe saw city populations fall.
Quick check
?From 1974 to 2024, the urban share rose by 10.9 percentage points in high-income countries but by 39.0 in upper-middle-income countries. Why the difference?
Mission 1

Why people move to cities

  • Push (rural): poverty, few jobs, mechanised farming, poor schools and clinics.
  • Pull (city): jobs, higher wages, education, health care, bright lights.
  • Natural increase: young people are the most likely to move to cities, so cities gain births as well as migrants.
Mission 1

Shenzhen

  • Established in March 1979; China's first special economic zone from August 1980.
  • 17.98 million permanent residents at the end of 2024.
  • Its government calls it "a young immigrant city": average age 32.5 at the seventh national census.
Mission 1

China: what drove city growth?

  • China's urban share (national definition): 20.6% in 1982 → 63.8% in 2020.
  • Of China's urban growth between the 2010 and 2020 censuses:
    • 56% rural-to-urban migration
    • 26% administrative changes (reclassification)
    • 18% natural increase.
  • Worldwide, natural increase and reclassification drive most city growth; migration matters most in Eastern and South-Eastern Asia and sub-Saharan Africa.
Quick check
?Use the figures earlier in this mission. What share of China's urban growth in 2010–2020 did not come from rural-to-urban migration?
Mission 1

Megacities

  • Megacity: a city of 10 million people or more.
  • 8 megacities in 1975 → 33 in 2025 → 37 projected by 2050.
  • 2025: 19 in Asia (India 5, China 4), 5 in Latin America, 4 in Africa, 3 in Europe, 2 in Northern America.
  • Largest in 2025: Jakarta (41.9 million), Dhaka (36.6 million), Tokyo (33.4 million).
  • They emerge where big countries urbanise fast: "Most megacities are located in the global South".
Mission 1

Effects of rapid urbanisation

  • Developed countries urbanised earlier and more slowly, with industry; later, many people moved out to suburbs.
  • Emerging and developing countries are urbanising faster, often faster than jobs and housing can be provided.
  • In 2022, 1.12 billion people lived in slums or informal settlements: 24.8% of the world's urban population.
  • Effects: informal housing and jobs, pressure on water, sanitation and transport; but also more jobs, services and economic growth.
Mission check

?Between 1975 and 2025, Ghana's total population grew 3.5 times, but its city population grew 13.5 times, from just under 2 million to 27 million. Where did most of the extra city dwellers come from?

When cities grow much faster than the whole country, people must be moving in from rural areas and towns. In China, 56% of urban growth in 2010–2020 came from rural-to-urban migration; Shenzhen calls itself "a young immigrant city".

Mission 2

The UK's people

  • UK population: about 69.5 million (mid-2025).
  • Latest censuses: England 56.5 million and Wales 3.1 million (2021); Scotland 5.4 million (2022); Northern Ireland 1.9 million (2021).
  • London alone had 8.8 million: about 1 in 8 of the people counted (our calculation).
Mission 2

Dense and sparse

  • Population density = people per square kilometre (km²).
  • London: 5,598 per km², the most densely populated region.
  • England 434; Wales 150; South West England 239, the least dense English region.
  • Glasgow City: 3,555. Highland 9.2; Na h-Eileanan Siar (Outer Hebrides) 8.6, the lowest in Scotland.
  • Pattern: dense in the south-east, Midlands, north-west England and Scotland's Central Belt; sparse in upland Scotland, Wales and the Pennines.

Mission 2

An urban country

  • In England, an area is rural if it is outside settlements of more than 10,000 people.
  • In mid-2024, 17% of England's people lived in rural settlements.
  • So about 83% lived in urban areas (our calculation).
  • That share is the degree of urbanisation. How fast it changes is the rate.
Mission 2

Physical factors

  • Uplands are often steep, wet and windy, with poor soils: few people live there.
  • The North Pennines has "a severe climate, with high rainfall, low temperatures and strong winds"; its population "is low, with only a few small market towns".
  • Lowlands, coasts and river estuaries are easier to farm, build on and trade from: most big cities grew there.
Mission 2

Human factors

  • Jobs and money: London had the highest GDP per head of any UK region in 2024, £72,201. Jobs draw people.
  • History: England and Wales grew fastest from 1801 to 1911, by 13.6% a decade on average, as industrial cities grew. Industry around the Tees estuary, for example, grew from "the abundance of mineral deposits" nearby.
  • Transport: ports, railways and motorways link the biggest cities.
Quick check
?Why is the North Pennines one of England's emptiest areas?
Mission 2

Different rates of growth

  • Census 2011 to 2021, population change:
  • East of England +8.3%; South West +7.8%; London +7.7%.
  • North East +1.9%, the lowest in England; Wales +1.4%.
  • Scotland 2011–22: growth mostly in the Central Belt, especially around Edinburgh, "driven by migration"; falls in the west and south-west, where deaths outnumbered births.
Mission 2

Why rates and degree differ

  • Economy: growth in jobs (London, the South East) attracts migrants. UK manufacturing jobs fell sharply from the 1960s; older industrial regions grew more slowly.
  • Migration: London gains people from abroad but loses more to the rest of the UK; in 2024–25 its population fell by 0.29%.
  • Counter-urbanisation: net movement within England is towards mostly rural council areas, so towns and villages near big cities grow fast.
  • Age: older regions have more deaths. 22.3% of the South West was aged 65+ in 2021, against 11.9% in London.
  • Physical limits: uplands and remote islands have little room or reason to grow.
Quick check
?Between 2011 and 2021 the East of England grew by 8.3% and the North East by 1.9%. Which is the best explanation?
Mission check

?In Country Z, areas P and Q are both flat, low-lying and have the same mild climate. P is a port that has been a big city since the 1800s; Q is farmland far from main roads. Why is P far more densely populated?

The UK fits the same pattern: London is over 600 times as dense as the Highlands, because people cluster where jobs, transport and history meet on easy lowland, and stay sparse on harsh uplands.

Mission 3: London produces more per person than any other UK region. So why can so many Londoners not afford a home?

Make a guess: London produces more per person than any other UK region. So why can so many Londoners not afford a home?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel GCSE Geography A (1GA0)

  • 4.3 site, situation and connectivity of the chosen UK city in national, regional and global context
  • 4.3 structure (CBD, inner city, suburbs, rural–urban fringe) by function and building age
Mission 3

London: where and why it matters

  • The UK's capital, on the River Thames in south-east England. Population 8.80 million in 2021.
  • UK: the highest GDP per person of any UK region in 2024, £72,201: 2.4 times the North East's (our calculation).
  • World: one of only two Alpha++ world cities in the GaWC ranking, with New York; Heathrow is "Britain's aviation hub".
Mission 3

Site, situation and connectivity

  • Site: where the city was built. London grew where the Thames could be bridged and sea-going ships could reach.
  • Situation: its location relative to other places: south-east England, close to mainland Europe.
  • Connectivity: how well it is linked. Heathrow, the UK's hub airport; the Elizabeth line carries about 660,000 journeys a day.
  • Exam move: use all three scales: regional (south-east), national (capital, transport hub), global (finance, aviation).
Mission 3

Growth: natural change and migration

  • London shrank to 6.61 million in 1981, then grew to 8.80 million by 2021. 2011–2021: up 7.7% (our calculation).
  • International migration: in 2021, 40.6% of Londoners were born outside the UK.
  • National migration: young adults move in for work and study; many families move out. 47.4% of Tower Hamlets residents were aged 20–39, against 26.3% across England (Census 2021).
  • In the year to mid-2025, London's population fell 0.29%.
Mission 3

How migration changed London's character

  • Ethnicity: 53.8% of Londoners were White in 2021, against 81.0% across England; 20.7% were Asian and 13.5% Black.
  • Neighbourhoods differ: in Tower Hamlets, 44.4% of residents were Asian; in Richmond upon Thames, 80.5% were White.
  • Language: London had the lowest share of people with English as their main language: 78.4%.
  • Migration brings new food, faith buildings, festivals, music and businesses to the city.
Quick check
?London's population fell 0.29% in the year to mid-2025, even though it had more births than deaths and more people arriving from abroad than leaving. What must explain the fall?
Mission 3

Urban structure: function and building age

  • CBD (the City, the West End): offices, shops, theatres; old street plan with the newest, tallest buildings.
  • Inner city: 19th-century terraced housing, old docks and factories; later council estates, e.g. the Heygate, completed in 1974.
  • Suburbs: 20th-century semi-detached and detached houses with gardens; mainly residential.
  • Rural–urban fringe: the city's edge: green belt, farms, retail parks and airports.
  • Building age generally gets younger as you move out from the old core, except where areas have been rebuilt.
Quick check
?A zone has Victorian terraces, former warehouses turned into flats, and a 1970s council estate. Which zone is it?
Mission 3

Challenges: inequality and deprivation

  • Housing cost: an average London home cost 10.6 times average earnings in 2025, the highest of any region; the North East's ratio was 5.0.
  • In 19 of London's 33 boroughs, the ratio was above 12.
  • Deprivation is concentrated in "parts of east London".
  • Gaps between boroughs: in Tower Hamlets, 35.9% of households rent from a council or housing association; in Richmond upon Thames, 62.4% own their home.
Quick check
?London has the UK's highest GDP per person, yet many Londoners struggle. Which statistic best shows why?
Mission check

?A fast-growing city has high wages and many new jobs, but homes cost more than ten times average earnings and some old estates are being demolished. Which outcome is most likely?

London produces £72,201 per person, the UK's highest, but an average home costs 10.6 times average earnings. High output does not reach everyone.

Mission 4: Detroit had 1,849,568 people in 1950 and 639,111 in 2020. What emptied the Motor City?

Make a guess: Detroit had 1,849,568 people in 1950 and 639,111 in 2020. What emptied the Motor City?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel GCSE Geography A (1GA0)

  • 4.4 sequence of urbanisation, suburbanisation, counter-urbanisation and re-urbanisation in the chosen UK city (London)
  • 4.5 causes of deindustrialisation (globalisation, decentralisation, technology, transport) and impacts on the chosen UK city
Mission 4

The Motor City empties

  • Detroit, USA, the home of the American car industry, had 1,849,568 people in 1950 and 639,111 in 2020.
  • Historian Thomas Sugrue told the Associated Press that Detroit lost 140,000 jobs between 1947 and 1963.
  • Factories closed or moved out; people who could, often the better-off, moved to the suburbs and beyond.
  • Jobs left the city, and people followed.
Mission 4

Deindustrialisation: two big causes

  • Deindustrialisation: a long-term fall in manufacturing jobs in a city or country.
  • Globalisation: countries trade more freely, so firms can make goods where wages and costs are lower (the global shift). UK factories faced cheaper imports.
  • Technology: machines and robots (automation) do work people once did, so factories need fewer workers.
Mission 4

More causes

  • Decentralisation: firms leave crowded, costly inner-city sites for cheaper, bigger sites at the edge or in other regions.
  • Transport: bigger ships, containers and lorries favour new ports and motorway sites over old inner-city docks and rail yards.
  • Government policies can speed decline (ending support for loss-making industries, opening markets to imports) or slow it (grants to firms, regeneration schemes).
Mission 4

The UK picture

  • Manufacturing's share of UK jobs fell from an average of 25% between the world wars to an average of 9.5% in 2000–2016.
  • ONS: manufacturing employment "declined drastically from the 1960s onwards".
  • By 2016, services (the tertiary sector) gave 83.6% of UK jobs.
Quick check
?A car firm closes its UK factory and builds the same model in a country where wages are lower. Which cause of deindustrialisation is this?
Mission 4

Four stages of urban change

  • Urbanisation: people move into the city; it grows.
  • Suburbanisation: people move from the inner city to the suburbs at its edge.
  • Counter-urbanisation: people move beyond the city to small towns and villages.
  • Re-urbanisation: people move back into the inner city.
Mission 4

London's population story

Census counts and estimates for London:

  • Urbanisation: 1.1 million (1801) → 8.6 million (1939).
  • Inner and outer London: inner London fell from 4.90 million (1901) to 2.43 million (1981), while outer London grew from 1.61 million (1901) to 4.52 million (1951).
  • Decline and counter-urbanisation: Greater London fell from 8.62 million (1939) to 6.61 million (1981): down 23% (our calculation).
  • Re-urbanisation: back up to 8.80 million (2021); inner London grew 40% from 1981 (our calculation).
  • In the year to mid-2025, London's population fell by 0.29%.
Quick check
?Between 1901 and 1951, inner London's population fell while outer London's nearly tripled. Which process does this show?
Mission 4

Why people moved out, and back

  • Suburbanisation: trams, railways and then cars; cheaper land; space and gardens; clearance of crowded inner-city housing.
  • Counter-urbanisation: quieter, cleaner places; retirement; better roads and remote working let people commute or work from home.
  • Re-urbanisation: regeneration of old docks and factories; service jobs in the centre; students and young professionals; international migrants.
Mission 4

Deindustrialisation's impact on London

  • Jobs: manufacturing jobs fell nationally from the 1960s, including in London's factories and docks.
  • People: as factory and dock jobs went, more people left; Greater London's population kept falling until 1981. (The fall began in 1939, so war and planned moves to new towns played a part too.)
  • Deprivation: England's 2025 deprivation index still finds concentrations in areas with a history of heavy industry and in "parts of east London".
  • Land: closed docks and factories left derelict land, later reused for offices and housing (re-urbanisation).
Quick check
?Which is an impact of deindustrialisation on a city, rather than a cause?
Mission check

?Greater London lost about 2 million residents between 1939 and 1981, then gained about 2.2 million by 2021. What best explains the loss?

In Detroit, as in London, jobs left the city (deindustrialisation) and people followed them outwards (suburbanisation and beyond). Detroit lost about 65% of its 1950 population by 2020; London lost about 23% by 1981, then grew again as it re-urbanised.

Mission 5

The Census

  • A count of every person and household, every 10 years; the latest in England was 2021.
  • Results are published for small areas. The smallest, output areas (OAs), mostly hold 40–250 households and 100–625 people.
  • Your specification uses 2011 Census output-area data; most 2011 OAs were kept unchanged in 2021.
Mission 5

London in the 2021 Census

  • 8.80 million people, up 7.7% since 2011 (our calculation).
  • Young: 33.2% aged 20–39, against 26.3% in England; only 11.9% aged 65+, against 18.4% (our calculation).
  • Born abroad: 40.6%. Ethnicity: 53.8% White, 20.7% Asian, 13.5% Black.
  • Housing: 30.0% of households rent privately, against 20.5% in England.
  • Dense: about 5,598 people per km².
Quick check
?In 2021, 11.9% of Londoners were aged 65 or over, against 18.4% across England. What does this suggest?
Mission 5

Why has London grown?

  • Natural increase: a young population means more births than deaths.
  • International migration: more people arrive from abroad than leave; 40.6% of residents were born outside the UK.
  • Re-urbanisation: after falling to 6.61 million in 1981, London grew to 8.80 million in 2021.
  • But in the year to mid-2025, net moves to the rest of the UK outweighed both, and London shrank 0.29%.
Mission 5

Causes of migration

  • International: work, study, joining family, or seeking safety. London's global firms, universities and existing communities pull people in.
  • National, inward: young graduates and workers from the rest of the UK come for jobs and study.
  • National, outward: families leave for more space and cheaper homes. ONS found that first-time buyers bought homes further from the capital than 10 years earlier.
  • Housing cost is a key push factor: an average London home costs 10.6 times average earnings.

Mission 5

Economic change: from docks to finance

  • Deindustrialisation: UK manufacturing jobs "declined drastically from the 1960s onwards"; London's docks and factories closed.
  • Growth of services: by 2016, services provided 83.6% of UK jobs. In London, finance and business services grew in the City and at Canary Wharf, on former docklands.
  • London's GDP per person is the UK's highest; the City of London and Westminster are such large outliers that ONS leaves them off its map.
Mission 5

How economic change widens inequality

  • New high-paid jobs push up house prices faster than ordinary wages.
  • Housing: London homes cost 10.6 times earnings on average; in Kensington and Chelsea, 25.2 times.
  • Deprivation: concentrated in big conurbations, former heavy-industry areas, coastal towns and "parts of east London".
  • So workers in low-paid services (cleaning, care, catering) live beside, but not like, workers in high-paid finance.
Mission 5

Quality-of-life differences

  • Housing: owning versus renting (Richmond 62.4% owners; Tower Hamlets 23.1%).
  • Environment: Tower Hamlets recycles 17.0% of household waste, Bromley 49.9%; rates vary with "how heavily populated an area is, the kind of housing present".
  • Measuring it: quality of life combines income, housing, health, education and environment; the Indices of Deprivation combine these for small areas.
  • Exam move: explain a chain: economic change → jobs and pay → house prices → who can live where → quality of life.
Mission 5

Skill: using output-area data

  • Choose one variable (e.g. % aged 65+, % private renting, % born abroad).
  • Map it for the OAs in your study area as a choropleth (darker shading = higher value).
  • Compare with a second map, such as deprivation, and describe the pattern: where is it highest, lowest, any anomalies?
  • Limits: OAs are small, so a few households change the percentage; data are 10 years apart; boundaries can change between censuses.
Quick check

Mission check

?Two output areas in the same city. OA1: 52% aged 20–39, 60% private renting, many recent arrivals. OA2: 30% aged 65+, 75% owner-occupied. A new financial district opens nearby and house prices rise sharply. Which is most likely?

In Tower Hamlets, 47.4% of residents were aged 20–39: young workers and students move in for jobs. Many leave when they want space or to buy: first-time buyers now buy further from the capital, because London homes cost 10.6 times earnings.

Edexcel GCSE Geography A (1GA0)

Mission 6

The shift online

  • Internet sales as a share of all retail sales in Great Britain:
  • 2007: 3.4% → 2019: 19.2% → 2025: 27.5%
  • The peak was 37.8% in January 2021, during the coronavirus pandemic.
  • Today about £1 in every £4 spent in shops is spent online (our rounding of 27.5%).
Mission 6

Pressure on the CBD and high street

  • Across Great Britain, retail businesses on high streets fell 2% from 2012 to 2017, while those off the high street grew 6%.
  • High-street retail jobs fell in every country and region except London (+6%).
  • Even London's main shopping street, Oxford Street in the West End CBD, "has faced significant challenges in recent years due to the impact of the pandemic".
Mission 6

Out-of-centre shopping: Westfield Stratford City

  • Opened in September 2011 in Stratford, east London, well away from the West End CBD.
  • Built for £1.8 billion on a site that was "unsightly landfill", next to the Olympic Park.
  • Over 300 shops and over 70 places to eat, a 17-screen cinema, hotels and offices; direct access to Stratford International station.
  • The minister opening it called it "Europe's biggest shopping centre".
Quick check
?Why is Westfield Stratford City described as out-of-centre shopping?
Mission 6

Out-of-town shopping: costs and benefits

  • Benefits: many shops in one place; parking and transport links; jobs and regeneration (the minister said Westfield "brings welcome investment and jobs").
  • Costs: can draw shoppers and trade away from older town centres; car-based sites add traffic; out-of-town sites can take greenfield land.
  • Westfield Stratford City avoided greenfield land and is served by trains, which reduces some costs.
Mission 6

Internet shopping: costs and benefits

  • Benefits: shop any time; compare prices; easier for people far from shops or with limited mobility.
  • Costs: fewer shop jobs and empty units on high streets; more delivery van traffic; harder for people who are not online.
  • Online's share rose to 27.5% of retail in 2025, so these effects are now large.
Quick check
?Which is a cost of the growth of internet shopping for a city?
Mission 6

A high street changing: Oxford Street, London

  • From 28 February to 2 May 2025, the Mayor of London consulted on Oxford Street's future; there were over 6,000 responses.
  • Almost 70% of Londoners who expressed a view supported a Mayoral Development Corporation; two-thirds favoured pedestrianisation.
  • The Oxford Street Development Corporation was set up on 1 January 2026 to "lead the transformation of Oxford Street".
  • Transport for London was asked to develop proposals to pedestrianise the street.
Mission check

?In a market town, two clothes shops close. In the same year a café, a gym and a parcel-collection point open in their place. What does this show?

Internet shopping grew from 3.4% to 27.5% of retail sales (2007–2025), and out-of-centre malls like Westfield Stratford City drew shoppers from older centres. High streets such as Oxford Street are responding by changing function: more leisure, food, services and public space.

Mission 7

Mission 7: Urban areas use 75% of the world's energy. Vauban, a whole eco-district in Germany, is home to about 5,300 people. Can eco-cities and smart cities make a real difference?

Make a guess: Urban areas use 75% of the world's energy. Vauban, a whole eco-district in Germany, is home to about 5,300 people. Can eco-cities and smart cities make a real difference?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel GCSE Geography A (1GA0)

  • 4.5 strategies for more sustainable urban living and better quality of life in the chosen UK city (London): recycling, jobs, transport, housing etc.; ecological footprint skill
Mission 7

Why city living matters

  • Urban areas use 75% of the world's energy and produce 75% of its greenhouse gas emissions (UN, citing the European Commission).
  • Sustainable urban living: meeting people's needs now without harming future generations.
  • Edexcel adds jobs, education, health, transport and affordable, energy-efficient housing.
Mission 7

The ecological footprint

  • Ecological footprint: the area of productive land and sea a population needs to produce what it uses and to absorb its waste, especially carbon emissions.
  • It is measured in global hectares (gha).
  • A city's footprint is far bigger than the city itself: its food, energy and goods come from elsewhere.
  • Lower footprint per person = more sustainable living.
Quick check
?In a made-up town, 20,000 people each have an ecological footprint of 4 global hectares. The town covers 5,000 hectares. How many times larger is its total footprint than its own area?
Mission 7

Vauban, Freiburg (Germany)

  • Built on a former French army barracks, left empty when the forces departed in 1992.
  • About 41 ha, with about 5,267 residents (December 2020).
  • A tram line has served the district since April 2006.
Mission 7

Energy conservation

  • Low-energy building is compulsory in Vauban (65 kWh per m²).
  • 277 homes are built to passive-house standard: at most 15 kWh per m² a year.
  • In the Solar Settlement's plus-energy houses, more energy is produced than used.
  • A woodchip-fired combined heat and power plant supplies heat, and electricity for about 700 households.
Mission 7

Water conservation

  • Vauban has no rainwater sewers: rain runs in open channels to trenches where it soaks into the ground.
  • Some builders also collect rainwater in tanks (cisterns) and reuse it.
  • Keeping rain on site refills groundwater and protects places downstream from floods.
  • Other measures in many cities: water meters, low-flush toilets, rainwater harvesting, reusing "grey" water from sinks and showers.
Mission 7

Fewer cars

  • Vauban is "not a car-free but a car-reduced district" (translated).
  • No public parking on residential streets: car owners must buy a space in one of two district garages, about 470 spaces.
  • In December 2020 there were 1,132 private cars for about 5,267 residents.
Quick check
?Use the figures earlier in this mission. How many cars were there per 1,000 Vauban residents?
Mission 7

Waste recycling and green space

  • Waste recycling: separate bins for paper, glass, plastics and food; composting; reuse; less sent to landfill.
  • Green space: parks, trees, green roofs and gardens cool the city, store floodwater, support wildlife and improve health.
  • Build on brownfield (used) land first: about 60% of land turned into urban space worldwide since 1970 was productive farmland.
Quick check
?Which Vauban feature is an example of water conservation?
Mission 7

London: strategies for sustainable living

  • Transport and health: the Ultra Low Emission Zone covered all of London from 29 August 2023; about 97% of vehicles seen were compliant, and harmful roadside NO2 in 2024 was estimated to be 27% lower than without it. The Elizabeth line carried about 660,000 journeys a day by July 2024.
  • Housing and jobs: at Elephant Park, the affordable housing share was raised from 11% to 25%, and a Construction Skills Centre aims to train 1,000 local residents a year.
  • Recycling: London's boroughs collect household recycling, but rates vary widely: in 2024/25 from 17.0% (Tower Hamlets) to 49.9% (Bromley), against an England average of 42.0%.
  • Water: the Thames Tideway "super sewer" was fully operational in February 2025; storm overflow volumes into the Thames fell by about 86% in 2025 (modelled, in an unusually dry year).
Mission 7

Evaluating London's strategies

  • Reach: these schemes affect millions of Londoners; Vauban houses about 5,300.
  • Costs: non-compliant vehicles paid a daily ULEZ charge of £12.50 in 2023, which falls on owners of older vehicles; the Tideway cost £4.7 billion, adding up to £25 a year to average household bills before inflation.
  • Evidence: some results are modelled estimates (ULEZ NO2, Tideway volumes), not direct measurements.
  • Exam move: judge each strategy by who benefits, who pays, and how strong the evidence is.
Mission 7

Eco-cities, smart cities and retrofitting

  • Eco-city or eco-district: a whole area designed for a low footprint, like Vauban.
  • Smart city: uses digital technology and data to run services such as traffic and energy.
  • Retrofitting: adding these ideas to an existing city, where most people already live.
Mission check

?A made-up city of 3 million people builds one new eco-district for 5,000 people, with low-energy homes and few cars. What difference does this make?

Eco-districts like Vauban (about 215 cars per 1,000 residents, compulsory low-energy homes) show what works, but they house few people. Real difference comes when their ideas are retrofitted to existing cities, where most people live.

Mission 8: Mumbai keeps drawing people in from across India. Who really gains from its growth?

Make a guess: Mumbai keeps drawing people in from across India. Who really gains from its growth?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel GCSE Geography A (1GA0)

  • 4.6 site, situation and connectivity in national, regional and global context
  • 4.6 city structure (CBD, inner city, suburbs, urban-rural fringe) by function and building age
  • 4.7 past and present population growth: natural increase, migration, investment and economic growth
Mission 8

Where is Mumbai?

  • Site: on India's west coast, on the Arabian Sea; built on a group of former islands, with a sheltered natural harbour.
  • Situation: capital of Maharashtra state; India's main gateway to the Arabian Sea and the Gulf.
  • India is lower-middle income.
  • India is an emerging country.
Mission 8

Why Mumbai matters

  • Global: a world city at the Alpha level, linked to firms worldwide (GaWC, 2024). In the 19th century it became "a global trading centre".
  • National: home of India's central bank; the Reserve Bank's central office "permanently moved to Mumbai in 1937".
  • Regional: the main centre for jobs, ports, trade and services in Maharashtra and the wider city-region.
  • Connectivity: international airport, container port, and a suburban rail network linking the city-region.
Mission 8

How big, and how fast?

  • UN, same boundary method each year:
    • 2000: 16.0 million · 2025: 20.2 million · 2050 (projected): 23.1 million.
    • 12th-largest city in the world in 2025; growth 0.94% a year (2000–25).
  • Census 2011, Greater Mumbai's municipal area: 12,442,373 people.
  • Different boundaries, different totals: always name the source.
  • It is the world's densest city of a million or more: 27,000 people per km².
Mission 8

Why it grows

  • Natural increase: births exceed deaths in a young population.
  • Rural–urban migration from across India:
    • Push: low farm incomes, few jobs, poor services in rural areas.
    • Pull: jobs, wages, schools, hospitals in the city.
  • Since the 1960s M/East ward "has given shelter to migrants from different parts of India".
  • Investment and economic growth: finance, trade and industry create jobs that attract migrants.
Mission 8

A clue in the census

  • Greater Mumbai, 2011: 6,715,931 males and 5,726,442 females.
  • That is about 853 females per 1,000 males (our calculation).
  • Many migrants are young men who come alone to work, sending money home.
Quick check
?A young man leaves his village in Bihar because farm work pays too little, and takes a job on a Mumbai building site. Which factors are at work?
Mission 8

City structure

  • CBD (south, Fort area): banks, offices, the stock exchange. Oldest buildings: 19th-century Victorian Gothic and 20th-century Art Deco either side of the Oval Maidan.
  • Inner city: old mill districts and tenements, now being rebuilt as offices and flats; dense informal settlements.
  • Suburbs (north): newer high-rise flats, offices and malls along the rail lines.
  • Urban–rural fringe: new estates, industry and the planned city of Navi Mumbai across the harbour.
  • Buildings get newer as you move north, away from the old port.
Mission 8

Opportunities

  • Economic: jobs in finance, trade, ports, film, manufacturing and services; urban industry stimulates growth across Maharashtra.
  • Social: more schools and hospitals than in most rural areas. In 2011, about 90% of Greater Mumbai's people aged 7+ could read and write (calculated from census).
  • Resources: piped water and electricity reach far more homes than in villages.
  • For migrants, wages sent home help rural families.
Mission check

?Who gains from Mumbai's growth? Which answer gives one economic and one social opportunity?

Migrants gain jobs and wages, their families gain from money sent home, and the city gains as a national and global hub. But growth this fast has a price. The next mission shows who pays it.

Mission 9

Mission 9: In 2011, 1 in 10 people in Mumbai's D ward lived in slums. In M/East ward, more than 3 in 4 did. Why such a gap inside one city?

Make a guess: In 2011, 1 in 10 people in Mumbai's D ward lived in slums. In M/East ward, more than 3 in 4 did. Why such a gap inside one city?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel GCSE Geography A (1GA0)

  • 4.7 causes of national and international migration and impacts on parts of the city (age, ethnicity, housing, services)
  • 4.7 how growth brings inequality and reasons for quality-of-life differences
  • 4.8 challenges of rapid urbanisation: infrastructure, services and housing; under- and informal employment; pollution
Mission 9

Mumbai: a fast-growing city

  • Mumbai is on India's west coast, on the Arabian Sea.
  • Census 2011: 12,442,373 people in Greater Mumbai.
  • The UN, counting the whole built-up city, gives 15.95 million in 2000 and 20.20 million in 2025: a rise of about 4.25 million in 25 years (our calculation).
  • In 2025 it was the most densely populated city of a million or more in the world: about 27,000 people per km².
  • India is an emerging country.
Mission 9

Why people move to Mumbai

  • Cities grow by natural increase (more births than deaths) and migration.
  • Push from rural areas: low farm incomes, few jobs, drought, poor services.
  • Pull to the city: jobs, wages, schools and hospitals.
  • Since the 1960s, M/East ward "has given shelter to migrants from different parts of India, as well as those who have been 'resettled' from slums in the inner recesses of the city".
  • National migrants come from elsewhere in India; international migrants come from other countries.
  • Young people are the most likely to move from the countryside to cities, so the areas they settle in gain young families and need schools and housing; many cluster with people from their home region.
Mission 9

Slums: unplanned housing

  • A slum is crowded housing that lacks basic services such as water, toilets and drains; much of it is built without permission.
  • Census 2011: 6,534,460 of Greater Mumbai's 12,442,373 people lived in slums: 52.5% (our calculation).
  • Praja Foundation, citing the same census, gives 41.84%.
Mission 9

Where the slums are

  • Slums grow on land others do not want or cannot use: beside railways, on marshy or flood-prone ground, next to landfill, at the city edge.
  • D ward: 34,699 of 346,866 people in slums (10.0%).
  • M/East ward: 685,994 of 807,720 (84.9%).
  • M/East contains "Asia's second largest and oldest open land-fill".
  • Formal housing dominates where land is most valuable.
Quick check
?Two sources, both using the 2011 census, give Mumbai's slum share as 52.5% and 41.84%. What is the best explanation?
Mission 9

Life in M/East

Praja Foundation, citing a 2017 study by the charity Apnalaya:

  • The landfill still receives about 4,500 tonnes of garbage a day.
  • Infant mortality: 66 per 1,000 live births, against a national average of 41.
  • Average age at death: 39.
  • Air and groundwater are contaminated; basic amenities "are conspicuous by their absence".
  • Average family income in its slum clusters: Rs 7,802 a month.
Quick check
?Which M/East figure is the clearest sign of poor health and living conditions?
Mission 9

Informal work

  • Formal jobs: contract, regular pay, taxes paid, legal protection.
  • Informal jobs: no contract, sick pay or pension. Examples: street vending, waste picking, domestic work, small workshops, building-site day labour.
  • In 2024, informal jobs were 81.5% of all jobs in lower-middle-income countries such as India, against 12.9% in high-income countries.
  • Causes: migrants arrive faster than formal jobs are created; many lack qualifications; informal work is easy to start.
  • In M/East, "Every second male and six out of seven females are unemployed".
  • Under-employment: people work fewer hours, or in less skilled jobs, than they want.
Mission 9

Informal work: for and against

  • For: income for people with no formal job; cheap goods and services; recycling of waste; skills learned on the job.
  • Against: low, irregular pay; no sick pay or pension; unsafe conditions; child labour risk.
  • For the city: few taxes are paid, so less money for services.
Quick check
?Which worker is in the informal sector?
Mission 9

Challenges of rapid growth

  • Housing: about half the city lived in slums at the 2011 census.
  • Water, sanitation and energy: in M/East, families won legal water and electricity connections only after organising; many homes share or lack toilets.
  • Waste and pollution: about 4,500 tonnes a day to M/East's open landfill; air and groundwater contaminated.
  • Services and transport: schools, clinics, roads and trains struggle to keep up with demand.
  • Jobs: high unemployment and informal work (jobs with no contract, sick pay or pension).
Mission 9

One city, two worlds

  • Wealth: an Alpha world city and India's central bank.
  • Poverty: M/East ranks bottom of Mumbai's 24 wards for human development (Mumbai Human Development Report 2009, cited by Praja).
  • Reasons for the gap in quality of life:
    • land values: the rich live where land is valuable; the poor where it is cheap;
    • history: M/East absorbed migrants and people "resettled" from inner-city slums;
    • access to formal jobs, water, schools and health care.
Mission check

?In a made-up city, Ward A sits by the port and business district, where land is expensive. Ward B is at the edge, beside the city's landfill; people cleared from central slums were resettled there, and new migrants keep arriving. Which ward will have the higher slum share, and why?

Land values and history explain the gap: D ward 10.0% in slums, M/East 84.9%. Cheap marginal land beside a landfill, resettlement from inner-city slums and new migrants concentrated slums in M/East.

Mission 10: Between 42% and 52% of Mumbai's people lived in slums in 2011. Who should decide how the city fixes that?

Make a guess: Between 42% and 52% of Mumbai's people lived in slums in 2011. Who should decide how the city fixes that?

Specification: what this mission covers

Edexcel GCSE Geography A (1GA0)

  • 4.8 pros and cons of bottom-up and top-down approaches to managing the chosen city
  • 4.8 role of government policies in improving social, economic and environmental quality of life in the chosen city
Mission 10

Mumbai: a megacity under pressure

  • About 20.2 million people in 2025.
  • One of the densest cities on Earth: about 27,000 people per km².
  • About 42% to 52% of residents lived in slums in 2011, depending on which census table is used.
  • India's financial capital: home of the Reserve Bank of India since 1937.
Mission 10

Top-down and bottom-up

  • Top-down: governments or big organisations plan and pay; large and fast, but local people may have little say.
  • Bottom-up: residents and community groups decide and act; cheap and trusted, but small.
  • Mumbai uses both.
Mission 10

Top-down 1: transport

  • Mumbai Urban Transport Project: World Bank loan of US$542 million (2002–2011) for "an efficient and sustainable urban transport system".
  • A later phase added 864 suburban rail cars, taking the fleet to about 3,124.
  • Metro Line 3: 33.5 km, "fully underground", Colaba–Bandra–SEEPZ.
  • For: faster journeys to jobs; electric trains instead of cars.
  • Against: people affected by the project had to be resettled; huge cost.
Mission 10

Top-down 2: slum rehabilitation

  • The Maharashtra state government set up the Slum Rehabilitation Authority (SRA) in December 1995.
  • The deal: developers build free flats for slum dwellers, and in return may build extra floor space (incentive FSI) to sell on the open market.
  • Residents who move out during building can receive rent.
  • For: new, permanent homes at no cost to residents or the state.
  • Against: depends on developer profit; the SRA can take "punitive action on non-participating slum-dwellers".
Mission 10

Bottom-up: community action

  • SPARC began in 1984 in Byculla, Mumbai; it works in an alliance with the National Slum Dwellers Federation (NSDF) and Mahila Milan (women's groups).
  • Women's savings groups manage loans and help families get identity documents.
  • The alliance has built "many hundreds of community toilets" with city governments.
  • In M/East, citizen-led work with the charity Apnalaya helped families get "legal water connections, electricity connections, cooking gas".
  • For: fits local needs; residents stay in place. Against: slow; still needs government land and permission.

Government policy and quality of life

  • Social: SRA flats give secure homes; the UN calls for slum upgrading that prevents displacement.
  • Economic: faster transport links homes to jobs; Mumbai Metro One, the Versova–Andheri–Ghatkopar line, says it saves 90 minutes of travel time.

Mission check

?City Z's government, without consulting residents, plans to clear an informal settlement near the city centre and rehouse everyone in free flats 25 km away, beside a new metro station. Which is the strongest criticism of this top-down plan?

Mumbai combines top-down projects (railways, metro, SRA flats) with bottom-up action (SPARC, Mahila Milan, Apnalaya). Top-down gives scale; bottom-up gives residents a say in where and how they live.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The world's urban share rose from 37% to 58% in 50 years.
  • High-income countries urbanised early and are now growing slowly; low-income and lower-middle-income countries are urbanising fastest, through migration and natural increase.
  • Megacities rose from 8 (1975) to 33 (2025), mostly in Asia.

Mission 2

  • About 69.5 million people; densest in London (5,598 per km²), the Midlands, northern English cities and Scotland's Central Belt; emptiest in the uplands (Highland 9.2 per km²).
  • About 83% of England is urban (degree); growth (rate) was fastest in the East, South West and London, slowest in the North East and Wales, because of jobs, migration, age and physical limits.

Mission 3

  • London: the UK's capital and an Alpha++ world city; 8.80 million people (2021), 40.6% born abroad, with neighbourhoods that differ sharply.
  • Opportunities (jobs, culture, transport, parks) sit beside challenges (housing costs, deprivation in east London).

Mission 4

  • Deindustrialisation came from globalisation, technology, decentralisation, transport change and government policy. UK manufacturing fell from about a quarter of jobs to under a tenth.
  • London shows the whole sequence: urbanisation to 1939, suburbanisation, decline to 1981, then re-urbanisation.

Mission 5

  • Census 2021: London is young (33.2% aged 20–39), diverse (40.6% born abroad) and renting (30.0% private); it grew 7.7% in 2011–2021 through natural increase and international migration.
  • Young adults move in for work and study; families move out for space and cheaper homes; inner boroughs like Tower Hamlets differ sharply from outer ones like Richmond.
  • Economic change (from docks to finance) raised house prices faster than ordinary pay, widening inequality.

Mission 6

  • Retail is changing for technological, economic and cultural reasons; online sales are now 27.5% of GB retail.
  • London: CBD pressure (Oxford Street), out-of-centre growth (Westfield Stratford City, 2011), internet shopping.

Mission 7

  • Sustainable urban living conserves water and energy, recycles waste and protects green space; Vauban does this through planning rules and design. The ecological footprint measures a population's demand on land and sea in global hectares.
  • London's strategies (recycling, ULEZ, Elizabeth line, affordable housing and training at Elephant Park, the Tideway) reach millions but have costs and some modelled results.

Mission 8

  • Mumbai: west-coast port city, India's financial centre, an Alpha world city; 20.2 million (UN, 2025).
  • Growth: natural increase + rural–urban migration. Gains: jobs, services. The costs come in the next mission.

Mission 9

  • Mumbai grew fast through migration and natural increase. About half its people lived in slums in 2011, concentrated where land was cheap and marginal, as in M/East.
  • Rapid growth brings opportunities (jobs, services) and challenges (housing, water, waste, pollution, informal work).

Mission 10

  • Mumbai: about 20 million people; 42–52% in slums (2011, census tables differ); informal work, waste and health gaps.
  • Top-down: World Bank-funded railways, Metro Line 3, SRA flats: big and fast, but displacement and developer-led.
  • Bottom-up: SPARC, NSDF, Mahila Milan, Apnalaya: local and trusted, but small and slow.
