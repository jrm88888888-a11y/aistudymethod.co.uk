Why do accents survive TV, radio and the internet?

Accent, dialect and prestige

TermWhat it coversInvented example
Accentpronunciation only'bath' with /æ/ (most of the North) or /ɑ:/ (much of the South)
Dialectvocabulary and grammar of a region or group'I were sat there' (parts of northern England); 'bairn' for child (North East, Scotland)
Standard Englishthe dialect of print and schooling; it can be spoken in any accent'I was sitting there'
Received Pronunciation (RP)a non-regional accent with high social status, used by only a small minority/bɑ:θ/, /t/ fully pronounced in 'water'
  • Overt prestige: open, public status, linked to education and power (Standard English, RP).
  • Covert prestige: the hidden value of local forms, which signal loyalty, toughness and belonging.
Language diversity · accent and dialectScreen 2

Study: Trudgill, Norwich (1968 fieldwork)

Method60 Norwich residents sampled at random; ranked in five social classes; recorded in four styles: word list, reading passage, formal speech, casual speech. One variable: the -ing ending, [ɪŋ] or [ɪn] ('walking' or 'walkin'')
Findings[ɪn] rose down the class scale and from formal to casual style; men used more [ɪn] than women in most class and style groups
Self-evaluationAsked what they said, men often claimed more non-standard forms than they used; women claimed more standard forms
ConclusionWorking-class speech has covert prestige, especially for men
  • Evaluate: a 1968 city may not represent today; a recorded interview makes speech more careful (the observer's paradox); speakers are treated as reflecting class rather than choosing an identity.

Study: Labov, identity and status

StudyWhat Labov didWhat he found
Martha's Vineyard (fieldwork 1961; published 1963)Interviewed islanders on an island filling with summer visitors; measured the first part of the diphthongs in 'right' and 'house'Centralised (older island) vowels were strongest among speakers who identified with the island, such as fishermen and young people planning to stay
New York department stores (published 1966)Asked staff in Saks (expensive), Macy's (middle) and S. Klein (cheap) where something was, to get 'fourth floor'; then asked againMost /r/ after vowels at Saks, least at S. Klein; more /r/ in the careful repeat
  • Accent features act as identity markers: islanders used vowels to sound like Vineyarders, not visitors.
  • Evaluate: store staff were anonymous, so their own class and background are unknown.

IdeaWho and whenKey point

Mission checkScreen 8

?Why do accents survive TV, radio and the internet?

We mostly learn and keep our accents from the people we talk to and identify with, not from people on screens. Accents carry covert prestige and mark belonging, so they persist; they still change, but mainly through contact and levelling with neighbours, with TV at most speeding up changes already under way.

Mission 2

‘Bath’ with a short or long A: how do you write down a sound?

Make a guess: ‘Bath’ with a short or long A: how do you write down a sound?

Language levels · phonetics, phonology and prosodyScreen 1

Three ways into sound

  • Phonetics: how speech sounds are physically made and heard.
  • Phonology: how a language organises sounds into a system. A phoneme is the smallest sound unit that changes meaning: bat and pat differ only in /b/ and /p/, a minimal pair.
  • Prosody (your spec says prosody): intonation, stress, pitch, tempo and volume across an utterance.
  • Phonemes go in slashes, /b/; detailed sounds in square brackets, [ʔ].
  • Spelling is a poor guide: bath, laugh and aunt spell one RP vowel three ways.
Language levels · phonetics, phonology and prosodyScreen 2

Writing a vowel: the BATH question

WordRP (southern)Northern English
bath/bɑːθ//bæθ/
grass/ɡrɑːs//ɡræs/
trap/træp//træp/
  • The IPA (International Phonetic Alphabet) gives one symbol per sound. Edexcel's reference sheet uses RP (Received Pronunciation) symbols.
  • RP bath has the long vowel of palm: ː marks length. Northern bath has the short vowel of trap (many northern speakers say a more open [a]).
  • The two accents use different phonemes in this set of words: the TRAP–BATH split.
Language levels · phonetics, phonology and prosodyScreen 3

Consonants: voice, place, manner

  • Voicing: do the vocal folds vibrate? /p/ is voiceless, /b/ voiced; /θ/ (thin) voiceless, /ð/ (this) voiced.
  • Place: bilabial /p b m/, dental /θ ð/, alveolar /t d s z n/, velar /k ɡ ŋ/, glottal /h/.
  • Manner: plosive /p b t d k ɡ/, fricative /f v θ ð s z ʃ ʒ h/, nasal /m n ŋ/, affricate /tʃ dʒ/, approximant /l r j w/.
  • /ŋ/ is one sound: the end of sing.

Language investigation · question, data, methodScreen 1

From topic to question

  • Start with an area of interest, then narrow it to a research question your own data can answer.
  • A good question is specific, answerable from the data and often comparative (two groups, two settings).
  • You may add a hypothesis, a prediction to test, but your conclusion can reject it.

Language investigation · question, data, methodScreen 3

Collecting data

  • Choose the type: spoken, written, multimodal, word lists, or attitudes (surveys, interviews).
  • Collect enough to see patterns, and keep the conditions comparable.
  • Observer's paradox (Labov, 1972): we want to study how people speak when not being observed, but we can only find out by observing them. Reduce it by recording for longer and using later sections.
  • Transcribe spoken data with a key (pauses, overlaps); give speakers labels.
Language investigation · question, data, methodScreen 4

Choosing methods

  • Quantitative: count features and compare (e.g. number of questions per speaker per hour), shown in tables or charts.
  • Qualitative: close analysis of examples in context, using the language levels and frameworks (e.g. politeness, turn-taking).
  • Strong investigations combine both: counts show the pattern, examples explain it.
  • Evaluate your method: sample size, what you couldn't control, what you'd do next.
Language investigation · question, data, methodScreen 5

Ethics

  • Informed consent: tell people what you're studying and get their permission, spoken or written.
  • Under 16: you also need a parent's or carer's permission.
  • Right to withdraw: anyone can pull out, even after agreeing.
  • Anonymity: no real names or identifying details; use labels (Speaker A, Parent 1). Keep recordings secure.
  • No secret recording: BAAL guidance calls deception unacceptable. If knowing the aim would change how people speak, give the general purpose, or explain straight after and delete the data if anyone refuses.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Accent is pronunciation; dialect is vocabulary and grammar. Standard English and RP carry overt prestige; local forms carry covert prestige.
  • Trudgill (Norwich) and Labov (Martha's Vineyard, New York) link accent to class, style and identity; Giles explains short-term shifts (convergence, divergence).
  • Regional speech changes mainly through contact: dialect levelling (Milton Keynes), Estuary English; TV may speed change but doesn't replace contact.
  • For Component 3, connect these ideas to your own investigation of the pre-released subtopic.
  • Phonetics (how sounds are made), phonology (the sound system and its phonemes), prosody (intonation, stress, pitch, tempo, volume).
  • IPA symbols in slashes record phonemes: RP /bɑːθ/, northern /bæθ/. Consonants are described by voicing, place and manner.
  • Connected speech: elision, assimilation, glottal stop.
  • Your exam supplies an RP symbol list; know the common symbols anyway.
  • Narrow a topic into a specific, answerable, often comparative research question; a hypothesis is optional and can be rejected.
  • Match data and method to the question: combine quantitative counts with qualitative analysis, and evaluate your method's limits. Reduce the observer's paradox.
  • Ethics: informed consent (parents too for under-16s), right to withdraw, anonymity, no secret recording.
