The Elizabethans 1580–1603

This mini-lesson works through the OCR depth study on the later years of Elizabeth I's reign. Elizabeth reigned 1558–1603, but here we focus on c1580–1603: how she governed, the religious threats she faced, Mary Queen of Scots and the Spanish Armada, the age of exploration, and Elizabethan society, poverty and theatre.

The Queen · c1580–1603

Elizabeth, the "Virgin Queen"

By 1580 Elizabeth I was an experienced monarch — the last of the Tudors. She never married, earning the nickname the "Virgin Queen", and had no direct heir, which made the question of the succession a constant worry.

  • She used her image carefully: portraits, jewels and symbols built up the "cult of Gloriana" — presenting her as a powerful, almost divine ruler.
  • Royal progresses — tours around the country staying with nobles — let ordinary people see her and displayed her power (and saved her money).

Why her image mattered: as an unmarried female ruler in a male-dominated age, Elizabeth used propaganda — the "Gloriana" image — to secure loyalty and hide the danger of having no heir.

Government · how she ruled

Ruling with the Privy Council

Elizabeth did not rule alone. She governed with the help of her Privy Council — her most trusted advisers.

  • William Cecil (Lord Burghley) was her chief adviser for decades; his son Robert Cecil took over later in the reign.
  • Sir Francis Walsingham was her spymaster, running a network of spies to uncover Catholic plots.
  • Parliament met only occasionally, mainly to grant taxes. Local government ran through Justices of the Peace (JPs) and Lords Lieutenant.

Balance of power: Elizabeth kept firm control, but relied on able ministers like Cecil and Walsingham. Parliament could grant money but had limited power.

Religion · the middle way

The Religious Settlement

Elizabeth's Religious Settlement of 1559 made England a moderate Protestant country — the Church of England. It was a deliberate "middle way" that tried to satisfy most people and avoid religious civil war.

  • Catholics who refused to attend Church of England services were called recusants and were fined.
  • Puritans — extreme Protestants — thought the settlement did not go far enough and wanted further reform.
  • Missionary Jesuit priests were smuggled into England to keep Catholicism alive; Edmund Campion was executed in 1581.

Misconception alert: the settlement was a moderate middle way, and it was threatened by both Catholics and Puritans — not just one side.

Religion · the great threat

Mary Queen of Scots

Mary Queen of Scots was a Catholic with a strong claim to the English throne. She fled to England and was kept imprisoned for years — but she became the focus of Catholic plots to overthrow Elizabeth.

  • The Babington Plot (1586) aimed to murder Elizabeth and put Mary on the throne. Walsingham's spies intercepted coded letters that incriminated Mary.
  • After much hesitation, Elizabeth agreed to Mary's execution. Mary was executed in 1587.

Misconception alert: this is Mary Queen of Scots (a Catholic claimant), executed 1587 — not "Mary I", Elizabeth's half-sister who died in 1558.

The Armada · why it came

Why Philip II attacked

In 1588, Philip II of Spain — Europe's most powerful Catholic king — sent a huge fleet, the Spanish Armada, to invade England. Several causes built up over the 1580s:

  • Religion — Philip wanted to overthrow Protestant Elizabeth and restore Catholicism.
  • England supported the Dutch rebels fighting Spanish rule in the Netherlands.
  • English "sea dogs" like Sir Francis Drake raided Spanish treasure ships and colonies.
  • The execution of Mary Queen of Scots (1587) — a Catholic queen — outraged Catholic Europe.

Not a single cause: the Armada came from a mix of religion, foreign policy (the Dutch), piracy at sea, and the execution of Mary.

The Armada · defeat

How the Armada was defeated

The Armada was a disaster for Spain. It was defeated by a combination of English tactics and bad weather:

  • English tactics — faster, better-armed ships harassed the Spanish and used fireships at Calais to break their formation.
  • Storms — strong winds (later called the "Protestant wind") scattered the fleet around Scotland and Ireland, wrecking many ships.
  • Elizabeth's rousing Tilbury speech boosted morale, and victory was a huge boost to English and Protestant confidence.

Misconception alert: the Armada was defeated — Spain did not successfully invade or conquer England.

The wider world · exploration

Explorers and the wider world

The later Elizabethan age saw the beginnings of English overseas ambition — voyages, trade and the first attempts at colonies.

  • Sir Francis Drake became the first Englishman to circumnavigate the globe (1577–80), returning to England in 1580 laden with treasure.
  • Sir Walter Raleigh sponsored attempts to found a colony at Roanoke (in "Virginia") in the 1580s — but the colony failed.
  • Trade and exploration grew, laying foundations for later English expansion overseas.

Misconception alert: in this period English colonies were attempts that failed (like Roanoke). A permanent colony (Jamestown, 1607) only came after Elizabeth's reign.

Society · order

An ordered society

Elizabethans believed in a fixed social order — the "Great Chain of Being". Everyone had a place, from the monarch at the top down to labourers and the poor.

  • Below the Queen came the nobility and gentry, then the "middling sort" (merchants, richer farmers), then labourers and the poor.
  • People were expected to know and keep their place; disorder was seen as a threat to God's design.

Why it matters: the "Great Chain of Being" idea helped justify why the monarch and nobles ruled — and made rising poverty feel like a dangerous threat to order.

Society · poverty

Poverty and the Poor Law

Rising population and bad harvests in the 1590s pushed many into poverty. Wandering beggars — vagabonds or vagrants — worried the authorities, who feared crime and disorder.

  • The Elizabethan Poor Laws, ending with the Poor Law of 1601, split the poor into the "deserving" (impotent) poor — old or sick, who were helped — and "sturdy beggars" — able-bodied, who were punished.
  • Help was funded by a local poor rate — a tax collected in each parish.

A landmark law: the 1601 Poor Law was the first national system, and it shaped how poverty was dealt with for centuries afterwards.

Society · culture

The "golden age" of theatre

The later Elizabethan years are often called a "golden age" of theatre. The first purpose-built playhouses opened, and going to the theatre became hugely popular with rich and poor alike.

  • Playwrights such as William Shakespeare wrote for the new theatres; the famous Globe theatre was built in 1599.
  • Plays entertained all classes, from "groundlings" who stood in the yard to nobles in the galleries.
  • But Puritans and some town authorities distrusted theatres, seeing them as immoral and a source of crime and disorder.

A society debate: theatre shows how Elizabethan culture flourished — but also how Puritans clashed with popular entertainment.

Exam skill

What held Elizabeth's reign together

OCR wants you to explain why Elizabeth survived so many threats. Look for these recurring factors:

  • Government & ministers — able advisers (Cecil) and Walsingham's spy network.
  • Religion — a moderate settlement that pleased most people, with recusants fined and plotters caught.
  • Image & propaganda — the "Gloriana" cult and events like the Tilbury speech and Armada victory.
  • Luck & the wider world — storms helped defeat the Armada; sea dogs brought wealth and prestige.

Turning points to argue for: the execution of Mary (1587) removed a Catholic rival, and the defeat of the Armada (1588) secured Elizabeth's throne and reputation.

Exam skill

Using sources on the Elizabethans

You will handle sources — portraits, plot documents, accounts of the Armada, maps — about Elizabeth's reign. Two habits win marks:

  • Ask who made it and why (provenance). A royal portrait is propaganda designed to glorify Elizabeth; an account of the Armada may exaggerate to boost Protestant pride.
  • Use your own knowledge to judge it. Support or challenge a source with facts you know about the later reign.

Interpretations differ: historians disagree about why the Armada failed — English skill, Spanish mistakes, or luck with the weather? Being aware that interpretations vary is exactly what the exam rewards.

Recap

The essentials to remember

The Queen & government: unmarried "Virgin Queen", no heir; ruled with the Privy Council; Cecil (Burghley) advised, Walsingham ran the spies; image = the "Gloriana" cult.

Religion: moderate 1559 Settlement (a "middle way") threatened by both Catholics (recusants, plots) and Puritans; Mary Queen of Scots executed 1587 after the Babington Plot (1586).

The Armada (1588): Philip II's invasion, caused by religion, the Dutch, sea dogs and Mary's execution — defeated by English tactics (fireships) and storms; Spain did NOT invade.

Wider world & society: Drake circumnavigates (returns 1580), Raleigh's failed Roanoke colony; the "Great Chain of Being"; poverty & the Poor Law of 1601; the "golden age" of theatre (Globe 1599). Elizabeth dies 1603 → James I.
