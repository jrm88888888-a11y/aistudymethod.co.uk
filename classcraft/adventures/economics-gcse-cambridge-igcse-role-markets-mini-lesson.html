Mission 1

What is a market?

  • A market: any place or system where buyers and sellers meet to exchange goods or services.
  • It can be a street market, a shop, a website, an app or a phone call.
  • Buyers want low prices; sellers want high prices. Trade happens at a price both accept.
  • Markets help answer the basic questions: prices tell firms what to make, and decide who gets it (those willing and able to pay).
Mission 1

Three sectors of the economy

  • Goods: physical things you can touch (bread, cars). Services: things done for you (a haircut, a bus ride, banking).
  • Primary sector: takes natural resources from the earth or sea: farming, fishing, forestry, mining, oil extraction.
  • Secondary sector: makes or builds goods from raw materials: manufacturing and construction.
  • Tertiary sector: provides services: shops, transport, banking, health care, education, tourism.
  • A chain of production: wheat (primary) → flour and bread made in a bakery (secondary) → sold in a shop and delivered (tertiary).
  • Say: "delivery and transport are tertiary" (a service) — not: secondary, even when they carry goods.

Mission 1

Who owns the firm?

  • Firms can also be grouped by who owns them.
  • Private sector: owned by individuals or shareholders. Usually aims to make a profit. Examples: a family-run shop, a car maker.
  • Public sector: owned or run by the government. Usually aims to provide a service. Examples: state schools, a state-owned railway or water company.
  • A firm has a sector and an owner: a state-owned oil company is primary sector and public sector.
  • Mission 2 looks at market, mixed and public sectors in more depth.

Mission 2

Three questions every economy must answer

  • Resources are scarce, so every society must choose:
  • What to produce? (phones or bus services? how many of each?)
  • How to produce it? (more machines or more workers?)
  • For whom? (who gets the goods and services?)
  • Who answers them? Buyers and sellers in markets, the government, or a mix.
Mission 2

The market economy

  • A market: anywhere buyers and sellers meet to trade, in person or online.
  • In a market economy, resources are privately owned and prices answer the three questions.
  • What: firms make what buyers are willing and able to pay for.
  • How: firms choose the cheapest methods, to make the most profit.
  • For whom: goods go to those who can afford the price.
  • Prices allocate (share out) scarce resources: you will see how in Mission 5.
Mission 2

Consumers, producers and government

  • Consumers (households) buy goods and services. Their spending tells firms what to make.
  • Producers make goods and services. They can be:
  • individuals (a self-employed plumber), firms (a supermarket chain), or the government (state schools, public hospitals).
  • Government sets rules (laws, minimum wage), collects taxes, provides some services and buys goods from firms.
  • They interact: consumers pay firms; firms pay wages to workers (who are also consumers); government taxes both and spends on both.

Mission 2

Nationalisation

  • Nationalisation: the government takes a private firm or industry into public ownership.
  • Example: the UK bank Northern Rock was nationalised in 2008 after it ran short of funds.
  • Arguments for: keep essential services running, protect jobs, prevent a private monopoly charging high prices, pursue social aims.
  • Arguments against: cost to taxpayers, weaker incentive to cut costs (no profit motive), political interference, less choice.

Mission 3

Movement or shift?

  • A change in the good's own price → a movement along the demand curve.
  • Step 1: price falls → slide down the curve = extension of demand. Price rises → slide up = contraction of demand.
  • Step 2: a change in anything else → the whole curve shifts. Right (D1 → D2) = increase in demand. Left = decrease in demand.
  • Increase in demand: more is bought at every price.
  • Say: "the price cut caused an extension of demand" — not: "the price cut increased demand".

Too high, too low: market forces

  • Step 1, price too high (4): supplied 400, demanded 200. Excess supply = 400 − 200 = 200 (a surplus). Unsold stock piles up → sellers cut the price.
  • Step 2, price too low (2): demanded 400, supplied 200. Excess demand = 400 − 200 = 200 (a shortage). Queues form → the price is bid up.
  • Step 3: both pressures stop only at Pe = 3. That is disequilibrium pushed back to equilibrium.
  • Excess demand or supply = the gap between quantity demanded and quantity supplied at that price.

Mission 5

Knock-on effects in related markets

  • Markets are linked. A change in one spreads to others.
  • Inputs: cocoa is used to make chocolate. Dearer cocoa → higher costs → supply of chocolate decreases → price of chocolate tends to rise.
  • Substitutes: dearer chocolate → demand for other sweets and snacks increases → their price and sales rise.
  • Complements (used together): if petrol gets much dearer, demand for large, fuel-hungry cars decreases.

Mission 5

The three jobs of price

  • Signal: a rising price tells producers that buyers want more.
  • Incentive: a higher price makes producing more profitable, so firms expand and new firms join.
  • Rationing: when a good is scarce, the price rises until only those willing and able to pay buy it.
  • This price mechanism answers the three questions:
  • What to produce: goods buyers pay for. How: the lowest-cost methods earn the most profit. For whom: those who can afford the price.

Mission 6

One ratio for "how strongly buyers react"

  • Price elasticity of demand (PED): how much quantity demanded responds to a change in price.
  • PED = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price
  • % change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100.
  • Price and quantity demanded move in opposite directions, so PED is negative.
  • Judge the size (ignore the minus sign): bigger than 1 → elastic; smaller than 1 → inelastic.
Mission 6

Worked example: cinema tickets

  • Illustrative data: ticket price rises from 8 to 10. Tickets sold fall from 500 to 450 a week.
  • % change in price = (10 − 8) ÷ 8 × 100 = +25%
  • % change in quantity = (450 − 500) ÷ 500 × 100 = −10%
  • PED = −10 ÷ 25 = −0.4
  • Size 0.4 is less than 1 → demand is inelastic: quantity changes by a smaller % than price.
Quick check
?Illustrative data: a café cuts the price of a smoothie from 5 to 4. Daily sales rise from 200 to 260. What is the PED?

Mission 6

What makes demand elastic or inelastic?

  • Substitutes: many close substitutes → elastic. Buyers can switch.
  • Necessity or luxury: necessities (basic food, medicine) → inelastic.
  • Share of income: a small share of income (salt, matches) → inelastic.
  • Habit or addiction (cigarettes) → inelastic.
  • Time: buyers react more over a long period than straight away.
  • Brand or whole market: one brand of crisps has many close substitutes (other brands), so its demand is very elastic. If one brand puts up its price, its buyers can switch at once. Crisps as a whole have fewer close substitutes, so demand is less elastic.
Mission 6

Real evidence: US petrol

  • Real data (Hughes, Knittel and Sperling, 2008). Economists measured how US drivers reacted to petrol (gasoline) prices.
  • Short-run PED for petrol, 2001–2006: −0.034 to −0.077.
  • In 2001–2006, a 10% price rise cut petrol use by less than 1% in the short run.
  • For 1975–1980 they found −0.21 to −0.34. Inelastic in both periods, but even less elastic later.
  • Why so inelastic? For most journeys there is no close substitute for a car, and petrol is a necessity for many drivers.

Mission 6

PED and total revenue

  • Total revenue (TR) = price × quantity sold. For buyers, the same amount is their total spending.
  • Inelastic demand: price rise → TR rises. Price fall → TR falls.
  • Elastic demand: price rise → TR falls. Price fall → TR rises.
  • Unitary elastic: TR stays the same.

Mission 6

Why PED matters

  • Producers (firms): with inelastic demand, a firm can raise its price and raise revenue. With elastic demand, a price cut may win more revenue.
  • Consumers: when demand is inelastic (fuel, medicines), price rises are hard to avoid: buyers keep paying.
  • Workers: if demand for the product is inelastic, a firm can pass on a pay rise in higher prices with little loss of sales, so jobs are safer.
  • Government – taxes: a tax on a good with inelastic demand (tobacco, fuel) raises a lot of revenue, because sales fall little. It does less to cut use.
  • Government – subsidies: a subsidy that lowers the price raises consumption most when demand is elastic.

Mission 7

What PES measures

  • Price elasticity of supply (PES): how strongly the quantity supplied responds to a change in price.
  • PES = % change in quantity supplied ÷ % change in price
  • Price up → firms supply more. So PES is normally a positive number.
  • Bigger number = stronger response.
  • Say: "the price rise caused an extension of supply" — not: "supply increased" (that means the whole curve shifts).

Mission 7

Reading a straight supply curve

  • Look at where the line meets the axes, not just how steep it is.
  • Starts on the price axis → elastic (PES > 1) all along it.
  • Passes through the origin → unitary (PES = 1) all along it, however steep.
  • Starts on the quantity axis → inelastic (PES < 1) all along it.

Worked example

  • Illustrative data. A strawberry farm sells punnets at £2.00. The price rises to £2.50.
  • Quantity supplied rises from 1,000 to 1,100 punnets a week.
  • % change in Qs = (1,100 − 1,000) ÷ 1,000 × 100 = +10%
  • % change in P = (2.50 − 2.00) ÷ 2.00 × 100 = +25%
  • PES = 10 ÷ 25 = 0.4 → inelastic.
  • Working backwards: PES = 1.5 and price rises 5% → % change in Qs = 1.5 × 5 = +7.5%. A firm supplying 800 now supplies 860.

Mission 7

What makes supply elastic?

  • Spare capacity: idle machines and staff → output can rise quickly.
  • Stocks: goods already made, or that can be stored, can be released fast.
  • Factors of production: if extra workers, machines and materials are easy to find, or can switch from other uses → more elastic.
  • Time: the longer firms have to respond, the more elastic supply becomes.
  • Production time: goods that take a long time to make or grow → less elastic.
Mission 7

Time changes everything

  • Very short run: output is fixed (e.g. fish already caught today). PES = 0.
  • Short run: firms use overtime, spare machines and stocks. Supply is inelastic.
  • Long run: firms build new capacity, and new firms join the market. Supply is more elastic.
  • So a rise in demand → short run: mostly a higher price; long run: more extra output.

Mission 7

Real world: cocoa, 2024

  • Cocoa beans (for chocolate) come mainly from West Africa.
  • Poor West African harvests in 2023–24 cut supply.
  • In March 2024, cocoa prices on the New York futures market (contracts to buy cocoa for later delivery) passed $10,000 a metric ton for the first time. Prices had roughly doubled since January.
  • Why could other growers not fill the gap? Newly planted cocoa trees take a few years to produce beans.
  • So in the short run, cocoa supply was very inelastic. Growers in other countries (e.g. Brazil, Ecuador) are working to expand output in the long run.
Mission check

?Illustrative data. The price of tomatoes rises by 20% and stays there. One week later, a greenhouse grower's quantity supplied is 4% higher than before. One year later, it is 30% higher than before. Which statement is correct?

The same product can have inelastic supply this week and elastic supply next year: time is the key factor.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • A market brings buyers and sellers together.
  • Primary: natural resources. Secondary: making and building. Tertiary: services.
  • In rich economies most jobs are in services.

Mission 2

  • Every economy answers what, how and for whom; markets answer with prices, governments with decisions.
  • Private sector: privately owned, aims at profit. Public sector: state-owned, aims at public service.
  • Privatisation sells state firms to private owners; whether it helps depends on competition and regulation.
  • Nationalisation takes firms into public ownership.

Mission 3

  • Demand is the whole schedule or curve: price on the vertical axis, quantity on the horizontal.
  • Own price changes → extension or contraction (movement along). Any other factor → increase or decrease (shift).
  • Market demand = the total of all buyers' demand at each price.

Mission 4

  • Supply slopes up: a higher price makes extra output profitable.
  • Own price changes → extension or contraction (movement along). Any other factor → increase or decrease in supply (shift).
  • A tax per unit shifts S left; a subsidy shifts S right.
  • Market supply = the total of all firms' supply at each price.

Mission 5

  • Equilibrium: quantity demanded = quantity supplied; above it a surplus, below it a shortage, and market forces push back.
  • A shift moves the equilibrium: more demand raises price and quantity; more supply lowers price and raises quantity.
  • Price signals, gives incentives and rations, which answers what, how and for whom to produce.

Mission 6

  • PED = % change in quantity demanded ÷ % change in price; size above 1 = elastic, below 1 = inelastic.
  • Close substitutes make demand elastic; necessities, habits and small shares of income make it inelastic.
  • Inelastic: a price rise raises total revenue. Elastic: a price rise cuts it.

Mission 7

  • PES = % change in quantity supplied ÷ % change in price. Below 1: inelastic. Above 1: elastic.
  • Supply is more elastic with spare capacity, stocks, easy-to-find factors and, above all, more time.
