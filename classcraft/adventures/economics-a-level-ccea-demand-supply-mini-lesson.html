Mission 1

What demand means

  • Demand: the quantity of a good that buyers are willing and able to buy at each price, over a given time period.
  • Law of demand: as price rises, quantity demanded falls, ceteris paribus (other things equal).
  • A demand schedule lists the quantity demanded at each price. Illustrative data:
Price per cupQuantity demanded (cups per week)
6100
5200
4300
3400
2500
  • Plot it: the demand curve (D) slopes downwards.
  • Demand is the whole curve, not one point on it.
Mission 1

Why the curve slopes down

  • Diminishing marginal utility: each extra unit consumed adds less satisfaction than the one before.
  • So a buyer is willing to pay less for the 4th cup than for the 1st. To sell more, the price must fall.
  • When the price of a good falls, two effects raise quantity demanded:
  • Substitution effect: the good is now cheaper relative to other goods, so buyers switch towards it.
  • Income effect: the same money income now buys more, so real income (purchasing power) rises and buyers can afford more.
  • Say: "the price fall raises real income" — not: "the price fall raises people's income".
Quick check
?The price of bus tickets falls while the price of train tickets is unchanged. Which describes the substitution effect for a commuter?
Mission 1

Movement or shift?

  • A change in the good's own price → a movement along D.
  • A change in anything else that affects buyers → a shift of the whole curve.
  • Step 1: price falls from P1 to P2 → slide along D from point a to point b.
  • Step 2: D1 → D2 to the right: more is demanded at every price.
  • Say: "a fall in price causes an extension of demand (a rise in quantity demanded)" — not: "demand increases".
  • A price rise → contraction of demand. A shift right → increase in demand; shift left → decrease.
Mission 1

What shifts demand

Shift factorIncrease (D shifts right) when…

  • The opposite change shifts D left.

Mission 2

What supply means

  • Supply: the quantity that producers are willing and able to sell at each price, over a given time period.
  • Law of supply: as price rises, quantity supplied rises, ceteris paribus.
  • Why? The profit incentive: a higher price makes extra output more profitable, so firms produce more.
  • Illustrative data: a strawberry farm.
Price per kgQuantity supplied (kg per week)
2100
3200
4300
5400
  • Plot it: the supply curve (S) slopes upwards.
Mission 2

Why supply slopes up: rising marginal cost

  • Marginal cost (MC): the extra cost of producing one more unit.
  • With some inputs fixed (land, machines), extra output gets harder to produce.
  • So each extra unit costs more to make: MC rises.
  • A firm that is a price taker sells every unit at the market price. It produces each unit whose MC is no higher than the price.
  • Illustrative data:
Unit1st2nd3rd4th5th6th
MC2346811
  • Price 6 → produce 4 units (the 5th would cost 8). Price 8 → produce 5.
  • A higher price makes higher-cost units worth producing. So the supply curve traces the MC curve.

?A price-taking firm faces the marginal costs in the table on the previous screen (2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 11 for units 1 to 6). The market price is 7. How many units should it supply?
Mission 2

Movement or shift?

  • A change in the good's own price → a movement along S.
  • A change in anything else that affects producers → a shift of the whole curve.
  • Step 1: price rises P1 → P2 → slide up along S.
  • Step 2: S1 → S2 to the right = increase in supply: more offered at every price.
  • A shift left = decrease in supply.
  • Say: "an increase in supply shifts the curve right" — not: "the supply curve shifts up". A rightward shift looks lower on the graph.
  • Say: "a rise in price causes an extension of supply" — not: "supply increases".
Mission 2

What shifts supply

Shift factorSupply increases (S shifts right) when…
Costs of productionwages, raw material or energy prices fall
Technologybetter methods raise output from the same inputs
Indirect taxes and subsidiesa tax is cut or a subsidy is given
Number of firmsmore firms enter the market
Expectationsproducers expect lower prices later, so they sell more now
Shocksgood weather, no disease, no supply disruption
  • An indirect tax per unit shifts S left (vertically up by the tax). A subsidy shifts S right (detail in a later lesson).

  • At a price of 4: 300 → 200 kg. Same price, less offered → decrease in supply (shift left).
  • To supply 300 kg now, the farm needs a price of 5, not 4. The curve sits higher but has moved left.
Mission 2

Real case: frost hits coffee

  • July 2021: frosts hit coffee-growing areas of Brazil, the world's largest coffee producer.
  • Damaged trees mean less coffee for sale at every price in later harvests.
  • That is a shock to supply: S shifts left, not a movement along it.
  • The price rise that followed is the result of the shift; it does not cause the shift.
Quick check
?The price of oranges rises and orange growers sell more oranges. Nothing else changes. How should an economist describe this?
Mission check

?Which change causes a decrease in the supply of electric scooters?

Only the good's own price moves producers along S. A change in costs, technology, taxes, subsidies or the number of firms shifts it.

Mission 3

Consumer surplus

  • Willingness to pay: the most a buyer would pay for a unit.
  • You would pay 15 for a concert ticket. It costs 10. You gain 5 that you never pay.
  • Consumer surplus = what buyers are willing to pay − what they actually pay.
  • The demand curve shows willingness to pay for each unit.
  • On a graph: the area below the demand curve and above the price, up to the quantity bought.
Mission 3

Producer surplus

  • Each seller has a minimum price they would accept for a unit: its extra cost.
  • The supply curve shows that minimum price for each unit.
  • Producer surplus = price received − minimum price the seller would accept.
  • On a graph: the area above the supply curve and below the price, up to the quantity sold.
  • Say: "producer surplus" — not: "profit". It ignores fixed costs.
Quick check
?Illustrative data: four buyers would each pay at most 12, 10, 8 and 6 for one unit of a good. The market price is 8. What is the total consumer surplus in this market?
Mission 3

Worked example: measuring the areas

  • Illustrative data. Demand meets the price axis at 16. Supply meets it at 4.
  • Equilibrium: price 12, quantity 40 units a week.
  • Both areas are triangles: ½ × base × height.
  • Consumer surplus = ½ × 40 × (16 − 12) = 80.
  • Producer surplus = ½ × 40 × (12 − 4) = 160.
  • Total (community) surplus = 80 + 160 = 240.
  • Height is always measured from the price line.
Mission 3

Total surplus is largest at equilibrium

  • Community surplus = consumer surplus + producer surplus.
  • Up to Qe, each unit is worth more to buyers than it costs to make: trading it adds surplus.
  • Beyond Qe, a unit costs more to make than buyers value it: trading it would reduce surplus.
  • So, with no market failure, a competitive market maximises total surplus at equilibrium.
  • This is allocative efficiency.
  • Lost total surplus from producing the wrong quantity is a deadweight loss.
Mission 3

When demand or supply shifts

  • Build the new equilibrium first, then redraw the areas.
  • Supply rises (costs fall): price falls, quantity rises.
    • Consumer surplus rises: existing buyers pay less, and new buyers join.
    • Producer surplus: the price is lower, but costs are lower and more units sell. With a parallel shift it rises too.
  • Demand rises: price and quantity rise.
    • Producer surplus rises: a higher price on more units.
    • Consumer surplus is measured under the new demand curve. With a parallel shift it rises too, even though price rose.
  • Demand or supply falls: the reverse.
  • Say: "an increase in supply" (a shift) — not: "an extension of supply" (a movement along the curve).
Mission 3Graph lab

Demand rises

Demand for the good rises (a parallel shift right). Supply does not change.

  • Takeaway: a parallel rise in demand raises both surpluses; the higher price is more than offset by more units and a higher demand curve.

Mission 3Evaluate

Is the biggest total surplus always best?

  • It depends on third parties: the areas count only buyers and sellers. External costs and benefits are left out.
  • It depends on who gains: a gain of 1 to a rich person counts the same as 1 to a poor person. Equity is a separate question.
  • It depends on ability to pay: willingness to pay reflects income as well as need.
  • It depends on measurement: real demand and supply curves are estimated, not observed.
Mission check

?Illustrative case: a city caps the number of taxi licences. Fewer rides take place than at the free-market equilibrium, and fares rise. Compared with the free-market equilibrium, what happens to total surplus (consumer plus producer surplus)?

The extra value buyers keep is consumer surplus. Moving surplus from buyers to sellers is a transfer; trades that never happen are a deadweight loss.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Why do people pay for a gym every month and then hardly ever go?

Make a guess: Why do people pay for a gym every month and then hardly ever go?

Mission 4

The rational benchmark

  • Standard theory assumes consumers:
    • are rational: they weigh costs and benefits consistently;
    • maximise utility;
    • have perfect information;
    • are self-interested.
  • Behavioural economics uses psychology to test these assumptions against how people really choose.
  • Where people depart from the benchmark in predictable ways, economists call it a bias.
Mission 4

Bounded rationality

  • Bounded rationality: people try to choose well, but are limited by the information they have, the time they have and the brainpower they can spare.
  • So they often satisfice: choose an option that is "good enough" rather than the best.
  • Say: "bounded rationality" (limited ability to optimise) — not: "irrational" (no reasoning at all).
Mission 4

Rules of thumb and habit

  • A rule of thumb (heuristic): a mental shortcut that saves time.
    • "Buy the brand I bought last time."
    • "Pick the second-cheapest item on the menu."
  • Shortcuts are often sensible, but they can lead to choices a fully informed optimiser would not make.
Mission 4

Anchoring

  • Anchoring: judgements are pulled towards an initial number (the anchor), even an irrelevant one.
  • Classic experiment (Tversky and Kahneman, 1974): people saw a "random" number, then estimated the percentage of African countries in the United Nations.
    • Group shown 10: median estimate 25%.
    • Group shown 65: median estimate 45%.
  • In shops: "Was 200, now 120" makes 120 look cheap.
Quick check
?A sofa shop displays a "Was 1,500" label above each sofa, crossed out, with the sale price of 900 underneath. Shoppers rate 900 as a bargain, even though similar sofas elsewhere cost 850. Which bias is the shop using?
Mission 4

Bounded self-control

  • Bounded self-control: people make plans for their future selves, then fail to stick to them.
  • Present benefits and costs loom larger than future ones (present bias).
  • Evidence (DellaVigna and Malmendier, 2006, US gyms):
    • Members on monthly contracts paid over $70 a month.
    • They went on average 4.3 times a month.
    • Cost per visit: more than $17, though a 10-visit pass cost $10 per visit.
  • When signing up, people overestimate how often their future self will go.
  • Other examples: saving too little for retirement; snacking against a diet plan.
Mission 4

Others' behaviour and social norms

  • Social norms: unwritten rules about what people around us do and approve of.
  • People copy others (herd behaviour): a long queue outside a restaurant signals "it must be good".
  • Fashion, smartphone choices and saving behaviour are all shaped by peers.
  • Demand then depends on what others buy, not only on price, income and own tastes.
Mission 4Evaluate

Should economists drop rationality?

  • It depends on stakes and repetition: with large stakes, practice and feedback, people learn and biases shrink.
  • It depends on market forces: firms that misprice lose to rivals, so some biases are offset at market level. Others are exploited (e.g. gym contracts).
  • It depends on predictability: biases that are systematic (anchoring, present bias) can be built into models and policy.
  • Some behavioural results have been hard to replicate; others, such as anchoring and present bias, are well established.
  • The rational model remains a useful benchmark: it shows what to compare real behaviour against.
Quick check
?A supermarket offers "3 for the price of 2" on cereal boxes that cost 3 each. What percentage discount does a shopper who buys 3 boxes get on the usual price?
Mission check

?Many workers say they should save more for retirement but do not start. In one scheme, workers sign up now to put part of each future pay rise into their pension; nothing leaves today's pay. Many more workers saved more under the scheme. Which bias does the design best get round?

People who mean well today often fail tomorrow; designs that commit the future self can close the gap.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Demand is the whole schedule or curve; its own price moves you along it, every other factor shifts it.
  • Price fall: extension of demand; non-price change: increase or decrease in demand.
  • The curve slopes down because of diminishing marginal utility.
  • A price fall also works through the substitution effect and the income effect.

Mission 2

  • Supply slopes up because a higher price makes extra output profitable; own price moves you along S, every other factor shifts it.
  • Rising marginal cost is why: a price taker supplies each unit whose MC is no higher than the price.
  • Increase in supply = shift right (it looks lower), decrease = shift left.

Mission 3

  • Consumer surplus: below demand, above price. Producer surplus: above supply, below price.
  • Community surplus is maximised at competitive equilibrium (MB = MC): allocative efficiency.

Mission 4

  • Bounded rationality, bounded self-control and bounded selfishness are the three main limits to the rational model.
  • Biases such as rules of thumb, anchoring and availability are predictable departures from rational choice.
