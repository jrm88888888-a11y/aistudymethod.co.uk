Mission 1

Mission 1: Your blood vessels leak fluid all day long. So why don't your tissues flood?

Make a guess: Your blood vessels leak fluid all day long. So why don't your tissues flood?

Mission 1

Three fluids

  • Plasma: the liquid part of blood. Water carrying dissolved substances and plasma proteins (e.g. albumin, fibrinogen).
  • Tissue fluid: bathes every cell in a tissue. Like plasma, but with very little protein and no red cells.
  • Lymph: tissue fluid that has drained into lymph vessels. Contains lymphocytes.
  • Cells exchange substances with tissue fluid, not directly with blood.
Mission 1

Out and back: the big picture

  • At the arteriole end of a capillary bed, blood pressure squeezes fluid out through the capillary wall (pressure filtration).
  • That fluid is tissue fluid. It bathes the cells.
  • At the venule end, most of it returns to the blood.
  • The excess drains into lymph vessels as lymph.
  • Lymph returns to the blood in veins near the heart.
  • Out, around the cells, back: the tissues stay supplied but never flood.
Mission 1

The pressures

  • (1) Hydrostatic pressure (blood pressure) pushes fluid out. High at the arteriole end.
  • Water and small solutes (glucose, amino acids, ions, O2) are forced through gaps in the wall: pressure filtration.
  • Cells and plasma proteins are too large. They stay in the blood.
  • (2) Hydrostatic pressure falls along the capillary, as fluid leaves and blood meets resistance.
  • (3) The proteins left behind pull water in by osmosis.
  • The proteins give the blood a lower water potential than the tissue fluid.
  • Arteriole end: push out > pull in → net outflow. Venule end: pull in > push out → net return.
  • Say: "tissue fluid forms by pressure filtration" — not: "plasma diffuses out".
  • Newer research: in most tissues, little fluid is actually reabsorbed at the venule end; most of it returns through the lymph (Levick and Michel, 2010). Exams still use the model above.
Mission 1

Worked example: net pressure

Illustrative values for one capillary (tissue-fluid pressures ignored):

Oncotic pressure (in) / kPa3.33.3
  • Net = out − in.
  • Arteriole end: 4.7 − 3.3 = +1.4 kPa: fluid moves out.
  • Venule end: 1.3 − 3.3 = −2.0 kPa: fluid moves in.
  • At the venule end, water moves in by osmosis, down the water potential gradient.

?Blood pressure in a capillary is about 4.7 kPa at the arteriole end and about 1.3 kPa at the venule end. How do these values explain the release and reuptake of tissue fluid?
Mission 1

Tissue fluid: the link to every cell

  • Tissue fluid carries O2, glucose, amino acids, fatty acids, ions from blood to cells.
  • Cells take them up by diffusion, facilitated diffusion or active transport.
  • Cells release CO2, urea and other wastes into tissue fluid. These enter the blood at the venule end.
  • Each cell is a short diffusion distance from tissue fluid, and tissue fluid from a capillary.
Quick check
?(Illustrative data.) Samples of blood plasma and of tissue fluid from the same tissue were analysed. Both contained about 5 mmol per litre of glucose and similar concentrations of sodium ions, but plasma contained about 70 grams per litre of protein while tissue fluid contained only a small fraction of that amount. Which explanation of these results is correct?
Mission 1

Lymph: draining the excess

  • Not all tissue fluid returns to the capillaries. The excess enters lymph vessels.
  • Lymph capillaries are blind-ended, with thin walls and gaps that let fluid in.
  • Valves stop lymph flowing backwards.
  • Lymph is moved along as nearby skeletal muscles squeeze the vessels. Larger lymph vessels also contract rhythmically.
  • It passes through lymph nodes, where lymphocytes collect.
  • Lymph returns to the blood, entering veins near the heart.
  • Say: "lymph rejoins the blood" — not: "lymph circulates separately".
Quick check
?Which statement describes how excess tissue fluid is drained and what happens to it?
Mission check

?In lymphatic filariasis, parasitic worms live in lymph vessels and can block them. A person with blocked lymph vessels in one leg develops a badly swollen leg. Which explanation is correct?

Fluid leaks out at one end and returns at the other; lymph vessels carry off the rest. Block the drain and the tissues do flood.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Countries that eat more fat have more heart disease. Does that prove the fat is the cause?

Make a guess: Countries that eat more fat have more heart disease. Does that prove the fat is the cause?

Mission 2

Correlation or cause?

  • Correlation: two variables change together.
  • Causal relationship: a change in one produces a change in the other.
  • A correlation can arise from a confounding variable (a third factor linked to both), from chance, or from cause running the other way.
  • Stronger evidence of cause: a mechanism; a controlled trial; more exposure, more disease (dose–response); many studies agreeing.
  • Say: "is correlated with" — not: "causes", until the evidence shows it.
Mission 2

Correlation coefficients

  • A correlation coefficient runs from −1 to +1.
  • The sign gives direction: + both rise together; − one rises as the other falls.
  • The size gives strength: near ±1 strong; near 0 little or none.
  • Sample size changes how far you can trust a coefficient, not how strong it is.
  • A statistical test tells you whether a coefficient is significant.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. In a study of 20 countries, the correlation coefficient between mean saturated fat intake and the death rate from coronary heart disease was +0.85. What can be concluded from this result alone?

Mission 2

Conflicting evidence: the HRT story

  • HRT (hormone replacement therapy): hormones taken to ease symptoms of the menopause.
  • Early observational studies: women taking HRT had less coronary heart disease. This was read as cause and effect.
  • Later randomised controlled trials: HRT caused a small increase in CHD risk.
  • Why? Women who chose HRT had higher socioeconomic status, which itself lowers CHD risk: a confounding variable.
  • Random allocation spreads confounders evenly between groups, so a trial isolates the treatment's own effect.
  • Say: "the correlation was real but not causal" — not: "the early studies got the numbers wrong".
Quick check
?Early epidemiological studies found that women taking HRT had a lower incidence of coronary heart disease (CHD). Later randomised controlled trials found that HRT slightly increased CHD risk. Which conclusion is best supported?
Mission 2

Judging a study

  • Sample selection: representative of the population; chosen at random; controls matched for age, sex and other factors.
  • Sample size: large, so chance differences matter less.
  • Validity: measures what it claims to; confounding variables controlled or allowed for.
  • Reliability: repeat studies find similar results.
  • Study types: cohort (follow people over time), case–control (compare people with and without the disease), randomised controlled trial.
  • Also ask: peer reviewed? Who funded it?
Mission check

?Illustrative data. A study of 2000 adults found a correlation coefficient of −0.40 between hours of golf played per week and the incidence of coronary heart disease. Which conclusion is best supported?

Fat and heart disease, HRT and heart disease, golf and heart disease: a correlation is where the question starts, not where it ends.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why does a good biology drawing leave out most of what you can see down the microscope?

Make a guess: Why does a good biology drawing leave out most of what you can see down the microscope?

Mission 3

Rules for a scientific drawing

  • Sharp pencil. Clear, continuous single lines. No shading or colouring.
  • Draw only what you see, in the right proportions. Use most of the space.
  • Label lines: ruled, no arrowheads, touching the structure, never crossing.
  • Labels outside the drawing, aligned neatly.
  • Annotation = label + a short note: what the structure does, or a feature you can see.
  • Title: specimen, section (TS or LS), magnification or a scale bar.
Mission 3

Plan diagram or cell drawing?

  • Plan diagram (low power): outlines of tissue regions only. No individual cells.
  • Draw each tissue layer in the right proportion of the whole.
  • High-power drawing: a few representative cells, with the detail you see (walls, nuclei, chloroplasts).
  • Leaf TS, top to bottom: upper epidermis (cuticle) → palisade mesophyll → spongy mesophyll (air spaces) → lower epidermis (stomata).
  • In each vein: xylem towards the upper surface, phloem towards the lower.
Quick check
?Which statement about a plan diagram of a transverse section of a dicotyledonous leaf is correct?
Mission 3

Stem and root in section

  • Stem TS: epidermis → cortex → a ring of vascular bundles → pith in the centre.
  • In each bundle: phloem on the outside, xylem on the inside.
Quick check
?A student is drawing a plan diagram from a micrograph of a transverse section of a dicotyledonous stem. Which description matches what should be drawn?
Mission 3

Worked example: magnification of a drawing

  • Magnification = size of drawing ÷ actual size, both in the same units.
  • A cell spans 12 eyepiece graticule divisions. At this objective, 1 division = 2.5 µm.
  • Step 1: actual length = 12 × 2.5 = 30 µm.
  • Step 2: the drawing is 60 mm long = 60 000 µm.
  • Step 3: magnification = 60 000 ÷ 30 = × 2000.
  • Scale bar instead: a 10 µm bar is drawn 10 × 2000 = 20 000 µm = 20 mm long.
  • Say: "draw a 10 µm scale bar at its magnified length" — not: "draw it 10 mm long".
Mission check

?A student is asked for a plan diagram of a transverse section of an artery seen at low power. The wall has three layers: a thin inner layer, a thick middle layer of muscle and elastic tissue, and a thinner outer layer. Which drawing meets the brief?

Leave out the cells, keep the proportions, and label with care: that is what turns a sketch into evidence.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Cut open an organ or slice it thin: how do you know what you are looking at?

Make a guess: Cut open an organ or slice it thin: how do you know what you are looking at?

Mission 4

Before you cut

  • Tools: scalpel, scissors, forceps, blunt seeker, mounted needle, dissecting board.
  • Cut away from yourself and your fingers, on the board.
  • Keep blades sharp: a blunt blade needs more force and slips.
  • Gloves on; wash hands afterwards; disinfect the board.
  • Blades into a sharps bin; tissue disposed of as instructed.
  • Ethics: organs usually come from a butcher, so no animal is killed for the lesson.
  • Anyone who objects can use models, photographs or video for the same observations.
Mission 4

Find your way round an organ

  • Orient first. Heart: the coronary artery runs diagonally across the front (ventral) surface.
  • Identify by structure, position and connections: not by how dark something looks.
  • Push a blunt seeker into each vessel. Which chamber does it lead to?
  • Artery: thick, elastic wall that stays open. Vein: thin wall that collapses flat.
  • Record: labelled drawing (see Mission 3) or photo with a ruler in view. Measure, e.g. wall thickness.
  • Say: "the left ventricle wall is thicker because it generates higher pressure to pump blood round the whole body" — not: "because it holds more blood".
Mission 4Practical

Dissections each board names

  • Required practical 5: dissection of an animal or plant gas exchange or mass transport system, or of an organ within such a system.
  • Same routine every time: orient → identify → record → measure.
  • Organ detail: heart in an earlier lesson; gills and tracheae in an earlier lesson; kidney in another lesson.
Quick check
?A student dissects a sheep's heart and finds that the wall of the left ventricle is much thicker than the wall of the right ventricle. Which explanation is correct?
Mission check

?A student dissects a set of sheep's lungs. Near the lungs she finds two tubes side by side. Tube P has C-shaped rings of cartilage in its wall and stays open when empty. Tube Q has a thin, soft wall with no cartilage and collapses flat. Which identification do these observations best support?

Structure, position and connections, never shade or size alone: that is how you name what you find, whether cut open or sliced thin.

Mission 5

Mission 5: How does a plant send sugar up to its buds and down to its roots at the same time?

Make a guess: How does a plant send sugar up to its buds and down to its roots at the same time?

Mission 5

Sources and sinks

  • Phloem carries organic substances, mainly sucrose and amino acids: translocation.
  • Source: where sugars enter the phloem. A photosynthesising leaf; a storage root in spring.
  • Sink: where sugars leave the phloem to be used or stored. Roots, meristems, fruits, growing storage organs.
  • Sap flows from source to sink: up or down.
  • Different sieve tubes can carry sap in opposite directions at the same time.
  • Say: "from source to sink" — not: "always down from the leaves".
Mission 5

Sieve tubes and companion cells

  • Sieve tube elements: living cells, end to end, forming sieve tubes.
  • End walls are sieve plates, full of pores: sap flows from cell to cell.
  • No nucleus, few organelles, thin layer of cytoplasm: little blocks the flow.
  • Companion cells: nucleus, dense cytoplasm, many mitochondria: they make the ATP for loading.
  • Many plasmodesmata link each companion cell to its sieve tube element.
  • Walls of cellulose, not lignified.
  • Say: "sieve tube elements are living cells without a nucleus" — not: "dead like xylem".
Quick check
?Which feature of a sieve tube element helps sap to flow along the phloem?
Mission 5

Loading at the source

  • (1) The companion cell uses ATP to pump H⁺ out: active transport.
  • (2) H⁺ diffuses back in through a co-transporter protein, carrying sucrose in with it.
  • (3) Sucrose passes into the sieve tube element through plasmodesmata.
  • At the sink, sucrose is unloaded and used in respiration or converted to starch.
Mission 5

Mass flow

  • (4) At the source, sucrose lowers the water potential of the sieve tube.
  • (5) Water enters from the xylem by osmosis. Hydrostatic pressure rises.
  • (6) At the sink, sucrose leaves; water potential rises; water leaves; pressure falls.
  • (7) Sap flows down the pressure gradient, from source to sink.
  • ATP is spent on loading, not on pushing the sap.
  • This is the mass flow hypothesis.
Quick check
?In spring, sugars stored in the roots of a deciduous tree are converted to sucrose and carried in the phloem to the opening buds. Which statement about this transport is correct?
Mission 5

The evidence

  • Ringing: remove a ring of bark, and with it the phloem, from a woody stem. The xylem is left. Sugars pile up above the ring; the bark swells. Leaves stay turgid.
  • Tracers: give a leaf ¹⁴CO2. It makes ¹⁴C-sucrose. An autoradiograph (photographic film laid on a stem section) shows ¹⁴C in the phloem, then in the sinks.
  • Energy: respiratory inhibitors, lack of oxygen or cold slow translocation. Companion cells are packed with mitochondria.
  • Two-way flow: ¹⁴C given to one leaf moves both up and down the stem.
Mission 5

Weighing up mass flow

  • For: sap in sieve tubes is under pressure (stylets ooze); sucrose concentration is higher at sources than at sinks; blocking ATP production stops translocation.
  • Against: sieve plates should slow mass flow, so why keep them?; different solutes may move at different speeds, which one bulk flow would not predict.

Mission check

?A young leaf near a shoot tip is still expanding. It imports sucrose through the phloem. Weeks later, fully grown, the same leaf exports sucrose. Which explanation is correct?

Up to the buds, down to the roots, in and then out of the same leaf: sap always flows from where sugar is loaded to where it is used.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Tissue fluid is squeezed out at the arteriole end; most returns at the venule end; the excess drains as lymph back into the blood.
  • Hydrostatic pressure falls along the capillary; the plasma proteins left behind draw water back in.

Mission 2

  • A correlation coefficient gives direction (sign) and strength (size); even a strong correlation does not prove cause.
  • Confounding variables explain conflicting evidence, as with HRT; randomised trials test cause.

Mission 3

  • Scientific drawings: clear continuous lines, no shading, ruled label lines, true proportions; annotations add function or features.
  • Plan (block) diagrams show tissue regions, not cells: leaf, stem with a ring of bundles (xylem inside, phloem outside).
  • Magnification = size of drawing ÷ actual size; scale bars are drawn at their magnified length.

Mission 4

  • Dissect safely (cut away, sharp blade, board, gloves) and identify by structure, position and connections; record and measure.

Mission 5

  • Phloem sieve tubes (living, no nucleus, sieve plates) and companion cells (many mitochondria) translocate sucrose from sources to sinks, up or down.
  • Active loading lowers water potential at the source; water follows; pressure drives mass flow to the sink.
  • Ringing, tracers and aphid stylets show sugars move in the phloem, need energy, and flow both ways.
