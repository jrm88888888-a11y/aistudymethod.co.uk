Mission 1

Mission 1: Your body carries trillions of microbes, yet very few make you ill. What makes something a pathogen?

Make a guess: Your body carries trillions of microbes, yet very few make you ill. What makes something a pathogen?

Mission 1

What makes a pathogen

  • A pathogen is an organism that causes disease.
  • Diseases caused by pathogens are infectious: they can pass from host to host.
  • OCR calls these communicable diseases.
  • Four groups include pathogens: bacteria, viruses, fungi, protoctists (protists).
  • Most microbes on and in you cause no disease. Being a microbe does not make something a pathogen.

Mission 1

Direct and indirect transmission

  • Direct transmission: from host to host with nothing in between.
    • Contact: skin to skin, body fluids (HIV), plant touching plant (TMV).
    • Inoculation: through a break in the skin: an animal bite (rabies), a wound, a shared needle.
    • Ingestion: in contaminated food or water.
  • Indirect transmission: via something in between.
    • Fomites: objects such as towels and bedding (athlete's foot).
    • Droplets: coughed or sneezed, then inhaled (TB, influenza).
    • Vectors: a living organism that carries a pathogen between hosts (Anopheles mosquitoes: malaria).
    • Spores: carried by wind, water or soil (late blight, black sigatoka).
  • Living conditions change spread: overcrowding, poor ventilation, poor nutrition, poor sanitation; warm, humid climate (fungi, mosquitoes).
Quick check
?Athlete's foot spreads in changing rooms when people share towels. Which describes this transmission?
Mission check

?One form of sleeping sickness is caused by Trypanosoma brucei rhodesiense, a single-celled eukaryote. It lives in wild antelope and cattle and reaches humans through the bite of tsetse flies. Which statement is correct?

A pathogen is defined by causing disease, not by being small or being a microbe. Then the question is how it gets in.

Mission 2

Mission 2: A plant cannot run away or make antibodies. So how does it stop an infection from spreading?

Make a guess: A plant cannot run away or make antibodies. So how does it stop an infection from spreading?

Mission 2

Defences always in place

  • Plants have no antibodies, lymphocytes or phagocytes.
  • Waxy cuticle: a waterproof layer. Pathogens and water films slide off.
  • Cellulose cell walls: a barrier around every cell.
  • Lignin: waterproof, hard to digest, in woody tissue.
  • Bark: a thick dead outer layer on stems.
  • Stomata can close when a pathogen is detected.
Mission 2

Chemical weapons

  • Many defence chemicals are made only after a pathogen is detected.
  • Hydrolytic enzymes: e.g. chitinases digest chitin in fungal walls; glucanases digest other wall polysaccharides.
  • Defensins: small proteins that disrupt pathogen membranes.
  • Phenols (e.g. tannins): toxic to many microbes and insects.
  • Alkaloids (e.g. caffeine, nicotine): toxic or bitter.
  • Terpenoids: e.g. essential oils; some are antifungal or antibacterial.
  • Saponins: disrupt fungal cell membranes.
Quick check
?After infection, many plants make chitinases. How do these enzymes defend the plant?
Mission 2

Detecting an attack

  • (1) Receptors on plant cells detect molecules from the pathogen, e.g. chitin from fungal walls.
  • (2) Signalling molecules switch on defence genes.
  • (3) The infected cell and its neighbours respond: new walls, callose, defence chemicals.
  • Signals (e.g. salicylic acid) also travel to other parts of the plant, which become more resistant.
  • Detection is non-specific: whole classes of molecule, not individual antigens.
Mission 2

Callose: sealing the breach

  • Callose: a polysaccharide (β-1,3-glucan), made quickly after attack.
  • At the point of attack: callose builds papillae between the cell wall and membrane. A barrier to entry.
  • Plasmodesmata: callose plugs them. Pathogens cannot pass to the next cell.
  • Sieve plates: callose blocks them. Spread through the phloem stops.
  • Cell walls nearby are thickened with extra cellulose; lignin can be added to papillae.
  • Say: "callose limits the spread of the pathogen" — not: "callose kills the pathogen".
Quick check
?How does callose deposited in plasmodesmata limit the spread of a virus through a leaf?
Mission 2

Sacrifice and seal

  • Necrosis: cells around the infection are deliberately killed. A pathogen that needs living cells is starved.
  • Canker: sunken, dead patches of bark or woody tissue around an infection.
  • Tyloses: balloon-like swellings that block xylem vessels, stopping spread in the water stream.
  • An oxidative burst: reactive oxygen species attack pathogens and strengthen walls.

Mission check

?Tobacco plants lacking an enzyme that breaks down callose have more callose at their plasmodesmata than normal plants. Both types of plant are infected on one leaf with a virus that moves from cell to cell through plasmodesmata. Which prediction is best supported?

No antibodies needed. A plant fights infection with chemicals, and limits its spread by sealing off cells with callose.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Most germs that land on you never get in, and most that do never get far. What stops them?

Make a guess: Most germs that land on you never get in, and most that do never get far. What stops them?

Mission 3

Skin and mucous membranes

  • Skin: an outer layer of tough, dead cells. Few pathogens can get through: a physical barrier.
  • Oily sebum keeps the skin surface acidic, inhibiting many pathogens: a chemical barrier.
  • Mucous membranes line the airways, gut, reproductive tract and eyes. No dead-cell layer here.
  • Their mucus traps pathogens. In the airways, cilia sweep the mucus up to the throat to be swallowed.
  • These primary defences are always in place, before any infection.
Mission 3

More barriers

  • Expulsive reflexes throw pathogens out: coughing and sneezing (airways), vomiting and diarrhoea (gut).
Quick check
?Chemicals in cigarette smoke stop the cilia in the airways from beating. Why do smokers get more chest infections?
Mission 3

Sealing a cut

  • At a cut, platelets release clotting factors.
  • A cascade of reactions follows, producing the enzyme thrombin.
  • Thrombin converts soluble fibrinogen into insoluble fibrin.
  • Fibrin threads form a mesh that traps red blood cells (erythrocytes): a clot seals the wound.
  • Wound repair: under the scab, skin cells divide by mitosis; collagen and new capillaries rebuild the tissue.
  • Full mechanism: Mission 4.
Quick check
?Which sequence correctly describes the events that seal a cut in the skin?
Mission 3

Inflammation

  • Damaged tissue and mast cells release histamine and cytokines.
  • Arterioles dilate: more blood flows in. Heat and redness.
  • Capillary walls become more permeable: plasma, proteins and white blood cells leave. Swelling.
  • Cytokines attract phagocytes to the site.

Mission 3

Phagocytosis

  • Phagocytes squeeze out of capillaries (amoeboid movement) and move to the infection site.
  • They are drawn there by cytokines.
  • A phagocyte recognises foreign molecules on the pathogen.
  • Opsonins (e.g. antibodies) bound to a pathogen make it easier to recognise.
  • It engulfs the pathogen by endocytosis, enclosing it in a vesicle: a phagosome.
  • Lysosomes fuse with the phagosome and release hydrolytic enzymes, which digest the pathogen.
  • Soluble products are absorbed.
  • Some phagocytes then display the pathogen's antigens (see Mission 5).
  • Say: "digested inside a phagosome by lysosomal enzymes" — not: "enzymes are released onto the pathogen".
Quick check
?Which statement correctly describes how a phagocyte destroys a bacterium?
Mission 3

Two kinds of phagocyte

  • Neutrophils: lobed nucleus; the most common white blood cell; first to arrive; short-lived.
  • Monocytes leave the blood and become macrophages: larger, longer-lived.
  • Macrophages present the pathogen's antigens on their surface: antigen-presenting cells (see Mission 5).
Mission check

?In a phagocyte, the lysosomes cannot fuse with phagosomes, but every other step works normally. What happens to a bacterium that meets this phagocyte?

Barriers keep most pathogens out. For those that get in, a phagocyte must both engulf and digest.

Mission 4

Sealing a cut

  • Clotting is a non-specific defence: it closes the skin barrier, so blood stays in and pathogens stay out.
  • (1) At the cut, platelets stick to the damaged vessel and to each other: a plug. They release clotting factors.
  • (2) A cascade of enzyme reactions produces the enzyme thrombin.
  • (3) Thrombin converts soluble fibrinogen into insoluble fibrin threads.
  • (4) The fibrin mesh traps red blood cells: a clot.
  • Wound repair: the clot dries to a scab; skin cells beneath divide; collagen fibres rebuild the tissue.
  • Say: "thrombin converts fibrinogen to fibrin" — not: "fibrin threads in the blood tangle together".
Quick check
?Fibrinogen circulates in the blood all the time. Why does a clot form at a cut, but not throughout the blood vessels?
Mission 4

Why a cascade?

  • Each activated enzyme activates many molecules of the next.
  • The signal is amplified: a small injury makes a lot of fibrin, fast.
  • The cascade starts only where clotting factors are released: the clot stays at the wound.
  • Fibrinogen circulates all the time, but stays soluble until thrombin acts on it.
Quick check
?Fibrinogen circulates in the plasma of healthy people all the time, yet clots form only where a blood vessel has been damaged. Which statement best explains this?
Mission 4

What is in blood

  • Red blood cells: biconcave discs; no nucleus.
  • Neutrophils: lobed nucleus; granular cytoplasm. Phagocytes.
  • Monocytes: the largest white cells; kidney-shaped nucleus. Become macrophages in tissues.
  • Lymphocytes: a large round nucleus filling most of the cell; thin rim of cytoplasm.
  • Platelets: tiny cell fragments; no nucleus.
Mission 4Practical

Reading a blood film

  • PAG1: examine and draw cells seen in blood smears.
  • A stain colours nuclei and granules. Find cells at low power; identify at high power.
  • Red cells far outnumber white cells: scan several fields.
  • Drawing: sharp pencil; clear, continuous lines; no shading; correct proportions; ruled label lines that do not cross; magnification stated.
  • Differential count (illustrative data): of 200 white cells, 128 had lobed nuclei (64%), 60 were lymphocytes (30%), 12 were monocytes (6%).
Quick check
?In a stained blood smear, a cell is larger than a red blood cell. Its nucleus has three lobes, and its cytoplasm is granular. Which cell is it, and what does it do?
Mission check

?People with haemophilia A lack Factor VIII, one of the clotting factors in the cascade that produces thrombin. Their platelets and fibrinogen are normal. What happens when such a person cuts their skin?

Fibrinogen flows everywhere, harmless. Only at a wound does a cascade make thrombin to turn it into fibrin. Break the cascade and the cut keeps bleeding.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Your body has never met this year's flu virus, yet it makes antibodies that fit it. How?

Mission 5

Self, non-self and antigens

  • Every cell carries surface molecules, mostly proteins and glycoproteins, that identify it.
  • The immune system ignores self molecules and responds to non-self ones.
  • An antigen is a molecule that triggers an immune response, including antibody production.
  • Say: "the antigen is recognised; the antibody is made against it" — not the other way round.
Quick check
?Which statement about antigens is correct?
Mission 5

One lymphocyte, one shape

  • Lymphocytes drive the adaptive (specific) response. They circulate in the blood and gather in lymph nodes.
  • Two kinds: B lymphocytes (B cells) and T lymphocytes (T cells).
  • Each lymphocyte has receptors of one shape. Each B cell can make one type of antibody.
  • You have millions of different lymphocytes, made before any infection.
  • An antigen binds only the few with a complementary receptor. It selects them: clonal selection.
  • Say: "the receptor is complementary to the antigen" — not: "the same shape as the antigen".
  • Say: "the antigen selects a cell that already fits" — not: "the cell designs an antibody to fit".
Quick check
?Which statement about B-lymphocytes and antibodies is correct?
Mission 5

Antigen presentation wakes T helper cells

  • A macrophage engulfs a pathogen and displays its antigens on its surface: an antigen-presenting cell (APC).
  • A T helper cell with a complementary receptor binds the presented antigen and is activated.
  • It divides by mitosis into a clone and releases chemical signals (cytokines).
  • These signals are interleukins: cell signalling between lymphocytes.
Mission 5

T killer cells: the cell-mediated response

  • This is cell-mediated immunity.
  • Stimulated by T helper cells, T killer cells (cytotoxic T cells) divide into a clone.
  • They bind body cells that display the antigen: virus-infected cells, cancer cells, transplanted cells.
  • By direct contact, they release proteins that make pores in the target cell's membrane. The cell dies.
  • T memory cells remain after the infection.
Mission 5

B cells: the antibody response

  • A B cell's receptor binds its complementary antigen. The B cell takes it in and displays it.
  • A T helper cell activated by the same antigen binds the B cell. Only now is the B cell activated.
  • The activated B cell divides by mitosis into a clone: clonal expansion.
  • Most become plasma cells: they secrete antibody. Every cell in the clone makes the same antibody.
  • Some become memory cells: long-lived and ready for next time.
Quick check
?In an experiment, B-lymphocytes specific to an antigen were cultured with that antigen and no other cells. No antibody was produced. When helper T-lymphocytes were added to the culture, antibody production began. What does this show?
Mission 5

Why the first response takes days

  • Only a few B cells fit any one antigen.
  • They must be activated, then divide many times, before there are enough plasma cells.
  • This takes days. Meanwhile the pathogen multiplies, and you feel ill.
  • Memory cells survive for years. Next time they divide at once: the pathogen is usually cleared before symptoms.
Quick check
?After a person is exposed to a new pathogen for the first time, several days pass before antibodies against it can be detected in the blood. Which statement best explains this delay?
Mission 5

Switching off, and attacks on self

  • T regulator cells suppress the immune response once the pathogen is cleared. They also help stop attacks on self.
  • Autoimmune disease: the immune system attacks self antigens as if they were foreign.
  • Example: rheumatoid arthritis: the joint linings are attacked; joints swell and become painful.
  • Another: lupus: connective tissue in many organs is attacked.
Quick check
?In type 1 diabetes, a person's T killer cells destroy their own insulin-secreting cells in the pancreas. Why is this an autoimmune disease?
Mission check

?A child is born with no T lymphocytes. Her B lymphocytes are normal in number and type. Why does she make very little antibody against most pathogens?

The right cells already exist before infection. The antigen selects them, T helper cells switch them on, and mitosis builds the army.

Mission 6

What an antibody is

  • An antibody is a protein made by plasma cells.
  • It binds specifically to one antigen, because its binding site is complementary in shape.
  • Binding forms an antigen–antibody complex, which leads to the antigen's destruction.
Mission 6

Antibody structure

  • Four polypeptide chains: two heavy and two light, so a quaternary structure.
  • Chains held together by disulfide bridges. Overall: a Y shape.
  • Variable region at each tip: the antigen-binding site. Its tertiary structure differs between antibodies.
  • Constant region: the same in all antibodies of one class. Phagocytes bind here.
  • Two binding sites per antibody: it can bind two antigens at once.
  • A flexible hinge region lets the arms bind antigens at different distances apart.
Quick check
?Antibodies against measles and antibodies against tetanus toxin are compared. Which part of the molecule differs between them?
Mission 6

How antibodies disable pathogens

  • Agglutination: each antibody has two binding sites, so it links pathogens together. Bacteria clump.
  • Clumped bacteria cannot spread, and phagocytes engulf many at once: phagocytosis of the complex.
  • Antibodies that cause clumping are agglutinins.
  • Opsonins coat a pathogen; phagocytes bind the constant regions, so engulfing is faster.
  • Anti-toxins bind toxins and neutralise them.
  • Say: "antibodies label and clump pathogens" — not: "antibodies kill or digest bacteria".
Quick check
?Why does an antibody cause bacteria to clump together?
Mission 6

First time slow, second time fast

  • Primary response (first exposure): few specific B cells must be selected, activated and cloned. Days pass; antibody peaks low. You may become ill.
  • Memory cells form and survive for years.
  • Secondary response (same antigen again): memory cells divide at once into plasma cells. Antibody rises sooner, much higher, and stays high longer.
  • The pathogen is usually destroyed before symptoms: you are immune.
  • B memory cells and T memory cells both make the secondary response faster.
  • Say: "memory cells persist" — not: "antibodies stay in the blood for life".

Mission 6

Active and passive immunity

  • Active: your own lymphocytes make antibodies and memory cells. Takes days. Long-lasting.
  • Passive: you receive ready-made antibodies. Works at once. Short-term: the antibodies are broken down and no memory cells form.
  • Natural active: catching the infection. Artificial active: vaccination.
  • Natural passive: antibodies across the placenta, or in colostrum (first breast milk).
  • Artificial passive: injected antibodies, e.g. anti-toxin against tetanus.
Quick check
?After a bite from a possibly rabid dog, a traveller is injected with rabies antibodies and also starts a course of rabies vaccine. Why are both given?
Mission check

?Illustrative data: a vaccine is given in two doses, one month apart. After dose 1, antibody rose slowly over two weeks to a low level. After dose 2, the same antibody rose within a few days to about ten times that level. What best explains the response to dose 2?

The second meeting is faster because memory cells are waiting, not antibodies. That is how a second infection, or a second vaccine dose, works.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • A pathogen is something that causes disease: bacteria, viruses, fungi and protoctists include pathogens; most microbes do not.
  • Transmission is direct (contact, inoculation, ingestion) or indirect (fomites, droplets, vectors, spores).

Mission 2

  • Plants rely on barriers (cuticle, walls, lignin, bark) and on chemicals (enzymes, defensins, phenols, alkaloids, terpenoids, saponins).
  • After detecting a pathogen, they limit its spread: callose papillae and plugs, tyloses, necrosis.

Mission 3

  • Barriers stop entry: skin (physical and chemical), mucus and cilia; clotting seals cuts.
  • Innate defences respond the same way to any pathogen; phagocytes engulf by endocytosis and digest with lysosomal enzymes in a phagosome.

Mission 4

  • Platelets at a wound release clotting factors; a cascade makes thrombin; thrombin turns soluble fibrinogen into a fibrin mesh that traps red blood cells.

Mission 5

  • Antigens select the few lymphocytes with complementary receptors; activated B cells form clones of plasma cells that all make one antibody, plus memory cells.
  • T helper cells, activated by antigen-presenting cells, switch on B cells and T killer cells, which destroy infected and abnormal cells.

Mission 6

  • Antibodies are Y-shaped proteins with two complementary binding sites; they clump pathogens for phagocytes. Active immunity makes memory cells and lasts; passive immunity is borrowed antibody and fades.
  • Memory cells make the secondary response sooner, larger and longer: that is immunity.
