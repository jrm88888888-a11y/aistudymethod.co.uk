Mission 1

Mission 1: How can Christians believe in only one God, yet pray to the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit?

Make a guess: How can Christians believe in only one God, yet pray to the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit?

Mission 1

One God, not three

  • Christians are monotheists, like Jews and Muslims.
  • They hold to the Jewish Shema, which Jesus himself quoted (Mark 12:29): "Hear, Israel: The LORD is our God. The LORD is one." (Deuteronomy 6:4)
  • So the Trinity (Latin trinitas, "threeness": God as three-in-one) never means three gods.
  • The word "Trinity" is not in the Bible. It names a belief Christians find in the Bible.
Mission 1

Three Persons, one God

  • Most Christians believe in one God in three Persons: Father, Son and Holy Spirit.
  • Each Person is fully God, not a third of God.
  • The three are distinct (not the same as each other) but not separate (not three beings).
  • All three are equal and eternal. None was made.
  • Not three gods. Not one God wearing three masks in turn.
  • In short: "three persons in one being".
Mission 1

Each Person

  • Trinitarian Christians describe each Person like this:
  • Father: Creator and source of all; loves and cares for people.
  • Son: Jesus Christ, God become human (the Incarnation); saves humanity.
  • Holy Spirit: God present and active in the world now; guides, strengthens and gives life.

Mission 1

The Nicene Creed

  • The Nicene Creed is built in three parts: Father, Son, Holy Spirit.
  • The Apostles' Creed has the same three-part shape (a later mission).
Mission 1

The Trinity in worship and life

  • Baptism is "in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit".
  • Sign of the cross (Catholic, Orthodox, many Anglicans and Lutherans) traces the same words.
  • Prayer is often to the Father, through the Son, in the Spirit. Many hymns and blessings end with all three ("Glory be to the Father…").
  • Trinity Sunday, the Sunday after Pentecost in Western churches, celebrates the belief.
  • Daily life: God is a communion of love, so Christians aim to live in loving community; they rely on the Spirit's help.
Mission 1Diversity

Christians who reject the Trinity

  • Mainstream churches (Catholic, Orthodox, most Protestant) treat the Trinity as essential.
  • Unitarians: God is one Person; Jesus is a human teacher and example. Today's Unitarians hold a wide range of beliefs.
  • Christadelphians: the Father alone is God; Jesus is God's Son, but did not exist before his birth; the Holy Spirit is God's power, not a Person.
  • Jehovah's Witnesses: Jesus is God's first creation; the Holy Spirit is God's active force.
  • Latter-day Saints: Father, Son and Holy Spirit are three separate beings, one in purpose.
  • These groups see themselves as Christian and say they follow the Bible's original teaching; mainstream churches disagree.
Quick check
?Which of these beliefs is held by Christadelphians?
Mission 1Evaluate

"The Trinity is a contradiction."

  • Reasons for: one cannot equal three. The word "Trinity" is not in the Bible. Deuteronomy 6:4 stresses that God is one. Unitarians and Christadelphians reject it. Every everyday analogy seems to fail.
  • Reasons against: God is one in being and three in Persons: "one" and "three" count different things, so there is no logical contradiction. Scripture treats Father, Son and Spirit as God (Matthew 28:19; John 10:30; Galatians 4:6). Christians call it a mystery: beyond full understanding, but not against reason.
  • Experience: Christians say they meet God as above them (Father), with them (Son) and within them (Spirit).
  • Judge it by: Do "one" and "three" count the same thing? Must a belief be fully understood to be reasonable? Should belief rest on the Bible alone or also on the Church's councils?
Mission check

?A student describes the Trinity as "like one actor playing three roles in a play, one after another". How would most Christians (Catholic, Orthodox and most Protestants) respond?

One being, three Persons, all at once: that is what keeps the Trinity from being three gods or one God in disguise.

Mission 2

What a creed is

  • A creed is a short statement of shared belief. From Latin credo: "I believe".
  • It sums up what a church teaches about God, Jesus and salvation.
  • Said aloud together, it shows who belongs: "this is what we believe".
  • The two most used: the Apostles' Creed and the Nicene Creed.
  • Both are built in three parts: Father, Son, Holy Spirit.
Mission 2

Where the Apostles' Creed came from

  • It grew from the questions asked of new Christians at baptism in Rome: "Do you believe in God the Father…?"
  • An early Roman form was written down by about 340.
  • Its final form was settled in Western Europe by about the 8th century.
  • A legend says each of the twelve apostles wrote one line. Historians see it as a summary of the apostles' teaching, not their words.
  • It is used mainly in Western churches: Catholic, Anglican, Lutheran, Methodist, Reformed.
Mission 2Read the source

The Apostles' Creed

"I believe in God the Father Almighty, Maker of heaven and earth:

And in Jesus Christ his only Son our Lord, Who was conceived by the Holy Ghost, Born of the Virgin Mary, Suffered under Pontius Pilate, Was crucified, dead, and buried, He descended into hell; The third day he rose again from the dead, He ascended into heaven, And sitteth on the right hand of God the Father Almighty; From thence he shall come to judge the quick and the dead.

I believe in the Holy Ghost; The holy catholic Church; The Communion of Saints; The Forgiveness of sins; The Resurrection of the body, And the life everlasting. Amen." (Book of Common Prayer, 1662)

  • Old words: Ghost = Spirit; quick = living; sitteth = sits.

Mission 2

What the Creed affirms

  • Father: Almighty Creator of everything.
  • Son: Jesus is God's Son and Lord; born as a human (Incarnation); died, rose, ascended; will return as judge.
  • "Descended into hell": went to the place of the dead, between his death and resurrection.
  • Holy Spirit: God at work now.
  • "Holy catholic Church": catholic means universal: all Christians everywhere, not one denomination.
  • "Communion of saints": all believers, living and dead, united in Christ.
  • Ends with hope: forgiveness, resurrection of the body, life everlasting.

Mission 2

Creeds in Christian life

  • Baptism and confirmation: candidates (or parents and godparents) answer questions drawn from the Apostles' Creed.
  • Sunday worship: the Nicene Creed at Mass or Eucharist; the Apostles' Creed at Anglican Morning and Evening Prayer.
  • Catholic prayer: the Rosary begins with the Apostles' Creed.
  • Unity: Christians of different churches can say the same words together.
  • Daily life: "forgiveness of sins" → forgive others; "Maker of heaven and earth" → care for creation; "he shall come to judge" → live responsibly; "resurrection of the body" → hope at funerals.
Mission 2Diversity

Different views on creeds

  • Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican, Lutheran, Methodist: creeds are central, trusted summaries of the faith.
  • Orthodox: use the Nicene Creed only, without the Western words "and the Son" about the Spirit.
  • Many Baptist, Pentecostal and independent evangelical churches: hold the same core beliefs, but stress the Bible itself; many rarely recite creeds.
  • Quakers: no creeds; they stress the inward experience of God and fear set words can replace living faith.
  • Unitarians: no creeds; they reject the Trinity.
  • Some liberal Christians: say the creeds, but read some lines (e.g. the virgin birth) as symbols.
Quick check
?Which statement about how Christian groups use creeds is accurate?
Mission 2Evaluate

"The Apostles' Creed is a complete summary of Christian belief."

  • Reasons for: covers God as Trinity, Jesus' birth, death, resurrection, ascension and return, the Church, forgiveness and eternal life. Shared by most Western churches for centuries. Short enough to learn by heart.
  • Reasons against: jumps from "born" to "suffered": nothing on Jesus' teaching, miracles or love of neighbour. Says little about how to live. No mention of the Bible or the Eucharist. Does not say how the three Persons are one (the Nicene Creed goes further). Orthodox and Quakers do not use it.
  • Judge it by: Is a summary meant to be complete, or only the essentials? Can belief be separated from how Christians live? Should "Christian belief" mean what all Christians agree on, or what most do?
Mission 2Worked answer

Explaining a creed's importance

Question: Explain why the Apostles' Creed is important for Christians.

  • Point 1: It sums up core beliefs in one short text. Development: a new Christian at baptism can say, in a few lines, what they believe about the Father, Son and Spirit.
  • Point 2: It unites Christians. Development: Catholics, Anglicans and Methodists can say the same words together, showing one faith across different churches.
  • Point 3: It guides daily life. Development: "the Forgiveness of sins" reminds believers that they are forgiven and must forgive others.
  • Part (b), 6 marks: explain in detail; develop each point with an example.
Mission check

?At a joint service, Catholics, Anglicans and Methodists stand and say the same creed together. Which reason best explains why Christians value doing this?

A creed is a shared, three-part map of belief; that is why the same old words still unite Christians today.

Mission 3

What "Incarnation" means

  • Incarnation comes from Latin in carne: "in flesh".
  • Christians believe God the Son, the second Person of the Trinity, became a human being: Jesus of Nazareth.
  • Cambridge's definition: God taking on human form.
  • Jesus is fully God and fully human: not half and half, not God in disguise, not a man who became God.
  • One person, two natures (divine and human): agreed at the Council of Chalcedon (451).
  • The Nicene Creed: he "was incarnate by the Holy Ghost of the Virgin Mary, and was made man".

Mission 3

The virgin birth

  • Christians believe Jesus was conceived by the Holy Spirit, without a human father, and born of the Virgin Mary (Apostles' Creed).
  • It shows Jesus is from God (divine) and born of a woman (human).
  • Matthew and Luke both tell it; Christians see it as God's initiative, not human achievement.
  • Catholic and Orthodox Christians honour Mary as Theotokos, "God-bearer" (Council of Ephesus, 431).
  • Most Christians accept the virgin birth as a historical event; some liberal Protestants read it as a symbol of Jesus' unique link with God.
Mission 3

Shepherds and wise men

  • Luke 2:1–20: born in Bethlehem, laid in a manger. Angels tell shepherds: "there is born to you today, in David's city, a Saviour, who is Christ the Lord." (Luke 2:11)
  • Matthew 2:1–12: wise men (Magi) from the east follow a star, ask for the "King of the Jews", and give gold, frankincense and myrrh.
  • Significance: the poor (shepherds) and outsiders (non-Jewish Magi) are welcomed first.

Mission 3Diversity

Why it matters, and how Christians differ

  • For all mainstream Christians: God is not distant; Jesus shows what God is like; God understands human life from the inside.
  • Catholic: Mary as Mother of God; the Angelus prayer recalls the Incarnation three times a day; Christmas Midnight Mass.
  • Orthodox: God became human so humans can share God's life (theosis). Athanasius: "He was made man that we might be made God" (On the Incarnation 54).
  • Protestant: often stress the cross more; the Incarnation makes the saving death possible.
  • Non-trinitarians (e.g. Jehovah's Witnesses, Unitarians) reject the belief that Jesus is God incarnate.
  • Impact: worship at Christmas; respect for human bodies and lives; serving the poor, in whom Jesus is met (Matthew 25:40).
Mission 3Evaluate

"The Incarnation is the most important Christian belief."

  • Reasons for: without it, Jesus' death is the death of a good man, not God's saving act. It shows God's love (John 3:16). Only God become human can reveal God fully (John 1:18).
  • Reasons against: Paul says faith depends on the resurrection (1 Corinthians 15:14). Many Protestants see the cross as the centre. The Trinity is the foundation all else rests on.
  • Another view: the beliefs depend on each other: Incarnation, cross and resurrection form one story.
  • Judge it by: Which belief do the others depend on? What do Christians celebrate most (Christmas or Easter)? Which makes most difference to daily life?
Mission check

?A Christmas hymn by Charles Wesley (1739) sings: "Veiled in flesh the Godhead see; hail the incarnate Deity". What belief do mainstream Christian churches take these lines to express?

In flesh, not in disguise: Christians believe Jesus is one person, fully God and fully human.

Make a guess: In less than a day Jesus went from supper with his friends to a cross. What happened, and why does it matter?

Mission 4

The last days

  • It is Passover in Jerusalem: the Jewish festival remembering freedom from slavery in Egypt.
  • Thursday evening: the Last Supper with the Twelve; then prayer in Gethsemane; Jesus is arrested after Judas betrays him.
  • Night and early morning: questioned by the Jewish council (Sanhedrin), then sent to Pontius Pilate, the Roman governor.
  • Friday: sentenced, crucified at Golgotha outside the city, dies in the afternoon, and is buried before the Sabbath.
  • Christians remember these days in Holy Week: Maundy Thursday, Good Friday, then Easter.
Mission 4Read the source

The Last Supper (Matthew 26)

  • At the Passover meal, Jesus says one of the Twelve will betray him; Judas asks, "It isn't me, is it, Rabbi?" (26:21–25)
  • Jesus takes bread, gives thanks, breaks it: "Take, eat; this is my body." (26:26)
  • The cup: "All of you drink it, for this is my blood of the new covenant, which is poured out for many for the remission of sins." (26:27–28)
  • He will not drink wine again until he drinks it new in his Father's Kingdom (26:29).
  • On the Mount of Olives he predicts that Peter will deny him three times (26:31–35).

Show answer

Q2 What is the new covenant?

Show answer

Mission 4

Washing feet; predictions

  • John 13:1–17: Jesus washes his disciples' feet, a slave's job. Peter protests.
  • "If I then, the Lord and the Teacher, have washed your feet, you also ought to wash one another's feet." (John 13:14)
  • "A new commandment I give to you, that you love one another." (John 13:34) Maundy comes from mandatum, "commandment".
  • Jesus predicts his betrayal by Judas, his death, and Peter's denial.
  • The Last Supper is the origin of the Eucharist (Holy Communion, Mass, Lord's Supper).
Mission 4

Gethsemane and the arrest

  • In the garden of Gethsemane Jesus is deeply troubled and prays three times; the disciples fall asleep (Matthew 26:39–44).
  • "My Father, if it is possible, let this cup pass away from me; nevertheless, not what I desire, but what you desire." (Matthew 26:39)
  • Judas arrives with an armed crowd and identifies Jesus with a kiss.
  • A disciple strikes the high priest's servant with a sword.
  • Jesus: "all those who take the sword will die by the sword" (Matthew 26:52). All the disciples flee (26:56).
  • Significance for Christians: fully human anguish; full obedience to the Father.

Mission 4

The trials

  • Before the Jewish council (led by the high priest Caiaphas): false witnesses; the high priest asks whether he is the Christ, the Son of God. His answer is judged blasphemy (Matthew 26:57–68).
  • Meanwhile Peter denies knowing Jesus three times, then weeps (26:69–75).
  • Before Pilate: the charge becomes political: claiming to be "King of the Jews", a threat to Rome.
  • Pilate offers to release a prisoner; the crowd chooses Barabbas. Pilate hands Jesus over to be crucified.
  • Matthew: Pilate washes his hands of the matter (27:24).
  • Churches today teach that the blame cannot be laid on Jews as a people, then or now (Catholic Church, Nostra Aetate, 1965).
Mission 4Read the source

The crucifixion (Matthew 27)

  • Soldiers dress Jesus in a scarlet robe and a crown of thorns and mock: "Hail, King of the Jews!" (27:28–31)
  • Crucified at Golgotha under the sign "THIS IS JESUS, THE KING OF THE JEWS"; passers-by mock him (27:33–44).
  • Darkness from noon to three. "Eli, Eli, lima sabachthani?" That is, "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (27:46)
  • "Jesus cried again with a loud voice, and yielded up his spirit." (27:50)
  • The Temple curtain is torn from top to bottom; the earth shakes; the centurion says, "Truly this was the Son of God!" (27:51–54)

Q1 Which psalm does Jesus' cry quote?

Show answer

Psalm 22, which ends in trust in God.

Mission 4

The burial

  • Joseph of Arimathea, a respected disciple (Luke: a council member who had not agreed with the decision), asks Pilate for the body.
  • He wraps it in linen and lays it in a tomb cut in rock; a large stone is rolled across the entrance.
  • Women who had followed Jesus from Galilee watch where he is laid.
  • Significance: Jesus truly died. The empty tomb on Sunday (see Mission 5) depends on it.
Quick check
?When Jesus died, the Gospels say the Temple curtain was torn in two. What do many Christians take this to mean?
Mission 4

Why the cross matters

  • Christians believe the crucifixion was not a defeat but the centre of God's plan.
  • Salvation: Jesus' death brings forgiveness and restores the relationship with God (atonement; full in Mission 7).
  • Who Jesus is: innocent, obedient, forgiving; the King whose throne is a cross.
  • The Son of God (centurion).
  • God shares suffering: Christians believe God knows human pain from the inside.
  • Example: love that gives itself for others (John 15:13).
Quick check
?Why do Christians call the day of Jesus' crucifixion "Good Friday"?
Mission 4Diversity

Remembering the cross

  • Catholic: the crucifix (Christ's body on the cross); Stations of the Cross; no Mass on Good Friday, but veneration of the cross.
  • Orthodox: Holy Friday services; the Epitaphios, an embroidered burial cloth, is carried in procession.
  • Protestant: often an empty cross, stressing the resurrection; Good Friday services and ecumenical "walks of witness".
  • Eucharist views: Catholic and Orthodox believe Christ is truly present in bread and wine (Catholic: transubstantiation); many Protestants see a memorial; Quakers and the Salvation Army hold no outward sacraments.
  • Impact on daily life: Holy Communion; foot-washing on Maundy Thursday; forgiving others; self-giving service.
Mission 4Evaluate

"The crucifixion was the most important event of Holy Week."

  • Reasons for: Christians believe salvation comes through Jesus' death (atonement). The cross is the central Christian symbol. Paul: "we preach Christ crucified" (1 Corinthians 1:23).
  • Reasons against: without the resurrection, the cross would be a defeat (1 Corinthians 15:14). The Last Supper gave the Eucharist, the centre of most Christian worship. Gethsemane shows the obedience that makes the cross meaningful.
  • Another view: the events form one story, the Paschal Mystery (see Mission 5): none can be ranked alone.
  • Judge it by: Which event do the others depend on? Which shapes worship most? What do the Gospels give most space to?
Mission check

Christians see the cross both as the act that reconciles people to God and as God's presence in human suffering.

Mission 5

What "resurrection" means

  • Christians believe that on the third day after his death God raised Jesus from the dead.
  • Not a ghost: in the Gospels the risen Jesus can be touched and eats food (Luke 24:39–43).
  • Not a resuscitation: Lazarus came back to ordinary life and would die again. Jesus was raised to a new, transformed life that cannot die (Romans 6:9).
  • The same Jesus, still bearing the wounds of the cross, but not always recognised at first.
  • Easter Sunday, the most important Christian festival, celebrates it.
Mission 5Read the source

The empty tomb (Luke 24)

  • At dawn on the first day of the week, women bring spices to the tomb. The stone is rolled away; the body is not there (24:1–3).
  • Two men in dazzling clothes ask: "Why do you seek the living amongst the dead? He isn't here, but is risen." (24:5–6)
  • They remind the women that Jesus said he would rise on the third day; the women remember (24:6–8) and tell the eleven (24:9).
  • The apostles think it is nonsense; Peter runs, sees the linen cloths, and goes home amazed (24:11–12).

Q1 Who are the first to hear the news?

Show answer

Women: Mary Magdalene, Joanna, Mary the mother of James and others, 24:10.

Q2 What does "the living" tell us about Jesus?

Show answer

He is alive now, not just remembered.

Mission 5

The appearances

  • On the road to Emmaus: two disciples walk with a stranger; they recognise Jesus when he breaks bread (Luke 24:13–35).
  • To the disciples in Jerusalem: "See my hands and my feet… a spirit doesn't have flesh and bones" (Luke 24:39). He eats fish.
  • Thomas doubts until he sees the wounds: "My Lord and my God!" (John 20:28)
  • In Galilee: Jesus sends the eleven out to the world (Matthew 28:16–20).
  • Paul lists appearances to Peter, the Twelve, more than 500 at once, James, and finally himself (1 Corinthians 15:5–8).

Mission 5

Sent out, with a promise

  • The Great Commission: "Go and make disciples of all nations, baptising them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit… Behold, I am with you always, even to the end of the age." (Matthew 28:19–20)
  • The promise of the Spirit: "I send out the promise of my Father on you. But wait in the city of Jerusalem until you are clothed with power from on high." (Luke 24:49)
  • Also Acts 1:8: "you will receive power when the Holy Spirit has come upon you. You will be witnesses to me… to the uttermost parts of the earth."
  • The promise is kept at Pentecost (see Mission 6).
  • Significance: mission, baptism and evangelism; the Church as witness.
Mission 5Read the source

The ascension (Luke 24)

  • Jesus leads the disciples out as far as Bethany and lifts up his hands to bless them.
  • "While he blessed them, he withdrew from them and was carried up into heaven." (Luke 24:51)
  • They worship him and return to Jerusalem "with great joy", praising God in the Temple (24:52–53).

Q1 How do the disciples feel when Jesus leaves? (Great joy, not grief.) Q2: Why might that be?

Show answer

They now know who he is and that he is with God.

Mission 5

What the ascension means

  • Jesus' earthly appearances end; he returns to the Father.
  • The Creed: he "sitteth on the right hand of God the Father Almighty": a picture of authority, not a place in the sky.
  • His human nature is taken into heaven: Christians see a pledge that humans can be with God.
  • He sends the Holy Spirit, so he is present everywhere, not in one place (John 16:7).
  • He will come again in glory (Acts 1:11).
  • Many Christians read "up" and "cloud" as signs of entering God's presence; others take the account more literally. Ascension Day is the Thursday 40 days after Easter.

Mission 5

Why it matters

  • Who Jesus is: Christians believe the resurrection shows God's approval of Jesus: he is "declared to be the Son of God with power" (Romans 1:4).
  • Victory: Christians believe sin and death are defeated.
  • Hope: Christ is the "first fruit": believers will also be raised (1 Corinthians 15:20).
  • Worship: Christians meet on Sunday, the day of resurrection; Easter is the high point of the year.
  • Life: courage, forgiveness and joy; the first disciples went from hiding to preaching.
Mission 5Diversity

Different understandings

  • Catholic, Orthodox and most Protestant Christians: a real, bodily resurrection and an empty tomb.
  • Some liberal Christians: the resurrection is spiritual: the disciples truly experienced Jesus alive with God; the empty tomb is less central.
  • Orthodox Easter greeting: "Christ is risen!" "Truly he is risen!" Easter Vigil worship in many churches begins in darkness with a new fire and the Paschal candle.
  • Non-religious explanations: the body was stolen (a rumour Matthew 28:11–15 already reports), Jesus did not really die, or visions. Christian replies: Roman executions were thorough; there were many witnesses; disciples suffered for the claim.
  • Muslims believe Jesus was not crucified but raised up to God (Qur'an 4:157–158): a different belief, not a resurrection after death.
Mission 5Evaluate

"Without the resurrection, Christianity would have no meaning."

  • Reasons for: Paul says faith would be "in vain" (1 Corinthians 15:14). The first Christian preaching centres on it (Acts 2:32). Christian hope of eternal life depends on it.
  • Reasons against: Jesus' teaching on love has value in itself; many non-Christians admire it. The Incarnation and the cross also carry meaning. Some liberal Christians, who believe Jesus is alive with God without an empty tomb, find deep meaning in a spiritual reading.
  • Another view: the resurrection gives meaning to the rest, but only together with Jesus' life and death.
  • Judge it by: What did the first Christians say mattered most? Can "Christianity" survive without a risen Jesus? Does "meaning" here mean truth, hope or ethics?

Not a ghost and not a return to old life: Christians believe Jesus was raised bodily to a life beyond death, and then ascended to the Father.

Mission 6

Who is the Holy Spirit?

  • Christians believe the Holy Spirit is the third Person of the Trinity: fully God, with the Father and the Son (see Mission 1).
  • Christians call the Spirit the Spirit of God on Earth: God present and active in the world now.
  • Christians see the Spirit at work in the Old Testament: "God's Spirit" hovers over the waters at creation (Genesis 1:2) and inspires prophets.
  • The promise: Jesus says the Father will send "another Counsellor" (helper) to be with them for ever (John 14:16, 26).
  • Before the Ascension: "But you will receive power when the Holy Spirit has come upon you." (Acts 1:8)
Mission 6Read the source

Pentecost (Acts 2:1–8)

  • Fifty days after Passover, the apostles are together in one house in Jerusalem (Acts 2:1).
  • "Suddenly there came from the sky a sound like the rushing of a mighty wind, and it filled all the house where they were sitting." (Acts 2:2)
  • "Tongues like fire appeared and were distributed to them, and one sat on each of them." (Acts 2:3)
  • They are filled with the Holy Spirit and begin to speak in other languages (Acts 2:4).
  • Jews from many nations, in Jerusalem for the festival, each hear them in their own language, and are amazed (Acts 2:5–8, paraphrase).

Q1 What two signs come before the apostles speak?

Show answer

A sound like a mighty wind; tongues like fire.

Q2 Why are the crowds amazed?

Show answer

Each hears the apostles speaking in his own native language.

Mission 6

What Pentecost means

  • Christians draw these meanings from Pentecost:
  • The promise kept: the Spirit Jesus promised has come.
  • Wind: in Hebrew and Greek, one word means both "wind/breath" and "spirit". God's breath gives life (Genesis 2:7).
  • Fire: God's presence (the burning bush, Exodus 3:2); purifying and giving energy.
  • Languages: the good news is for every nation; many see the confusion of languages at Babel (Genesis 11) undone.
  • Courage: the frightened disciples now preach in public. Peter's sermon leads about 3,000 to be baptised (Acts 2:41).
  • The birthday of the Church: the Church begins its mission.
  • The Jewish festival of Shavuot (Weeks), 50 days after Passover, is the setting; the Greek name "Pentecost" means "fiftieth".

Mission 6

God's power given to believers

  • Christians believe the Spirit lives in every believer, not only in the apostles.
  • The Spirit guides into truth (John 16:13), helps in prayer (Romans 8:26) and gives courage to witness.
  • Catholics, Orthodox and Anglicans link the gift of the Spirit with baptism and confirmation (chrismation for the Orthodox).
  • Pentecostals speak of "baptism in the Spirit": a later experience of being filled with power, often with speaking in tongues.
  • Significance: Christians pray "Come, Holy Spirit" before decisions, worship and study.
Mission 6

The fruit of the Spirit

  • "But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faith, gentleness, and self-control." (Galatians 5:22–23)
  • Fruit: qualities of character that grow slowly in a believer's life, as fruit grows on a tree.
  • Paul writes "fruit" (singular): one harvest with nine flavours.
  • Catholic tradition lists twelve fruits (adding, for example, modesty and chastity).
  • Impact: Christians check their lives by these qualities, not by dramatic experiences.
Mission 6

The gifts of the Spirit

  • Gifts: abilities the Spirit gives for serving others and building up the Church.
  • "But to each one is given the manifestation of the Spirit for the profit of all." (1 Corinthians 12:7)
  • 1 Corinthians 12:8–10: wisdom, knowledge, faith, healing, miracles, prophecy, telling spirits apart, speaking in tongues, interpreting tongues.
  • Seven gifts (Isaiah 11:2–3, Catholic tradition, given in confirmation): wisdom, understanding, counsel, fortitude, knowledge, piety, fear of the Lord.
  • Speaking in tongues (glossolalia): praying or praising in a language the speaker has not learned. Paul says it needs interpreting in public worship (1 Corinthians 14:27–28).
  • Fruit = character; gifts = abilities for service.

Mission 6Diversity

The Spirit in worship

  • Pentecostal and charismatic Evangelical churches: lively, free worship; raised hands; spontaneous prayer; speaking in tongues, prophecy and prayer for healing. Modern Pentecostalism grew from the Azusa Street revival (Los Angeles, 1906).
  • Some other Protestants (cessationists) believe the sign-gifts such as tongues ended with the apostles. Paul: "Let all things be done decently and in order" (1 Corinthians 14:40).
  • Catholic, Orthodox and Anglican liturgy: the Spirit is invoked in set prayers, for example over the bread and wine. The Catholic Charismatic Renewal (from 1967) brought charismatic worship into some Catholic parishes.
  • Quakers: sit in silence until the Spirit moves someone to speak.
Mission 6

Celebrating Pentecost

  • Pentecost (Whit Sunday in Britain) is seven weeks after Easter.
  • Red: vestments and decorations recall the tongues of fire.
  • Kneeling Prayers: in the Orthodox Church, the evening service on Pentecost includes three long prayers said kneeling, the first kneeling since Easter.
  • Baptisms and confirmations are often held: "Whit" may come from the white robes worn by the newly baptised.
  • Social side: in parts of England, Whit walks: churches parade through the streets with banners and bands.
  • Ways differ: Pentecostal churches hold large celebrations; Orthodox churches decorate with green branches (they also call it Trinity Sunday); some churches hold readings in many languages.

Mission 6Evaluate

"Speaking in tongues is the clearest sign of the Holy Spirit."

  • Reasons for: at Pentecost, tongues were the visible sign of the Spirit (Acts 2:4; also Acts 10:46, 19:6). Many Pentecostal churches teach tongues as the first evidence of baptism in the Spirit. It is a gift people can see and hear.
  • Reasons against: Paul asks "Do all speak with various languages?" and expects the answer no (1 Corinthians 12:30). Love is greater than tongues (1 Corinthians 13:1). Jesus: "By their fruits you will know them" (Matthew 7:20): character, not experience. Cessationists hold that the gift has ended.
  • Another view: Catholic and Orthodox Christians see the Spirit most clearly in the sacraments and in holy lives (saints).
  • Judge it by: Does Acts describe what happened then, or set a pattern for always? Is a sign the same as proof? Which matters more: gifts or fruit?
Mission check

?After a terrible fire, Sam, a shy Christian, finds the courage to organise help, speak at a public meeting and comfort strangers. Which belief about the Holy Spirit would most Christians use to explain this?

Pentecost turned frightened followers into bold witnesses. Christians believe the same Spirit gives power, gifts and a changed character today.

Mission 7

Saved from what?

  • Salvation: being saved from sin and its effects, to live with God for ever (eternal life).
  • Sin is anything that goes against God's will. It damages the relationship with God.
Mission 7

Atonement: making "at one"

  • Atonement: sin is dealt with and the broken relationship is repaired: people are made "at one" with God.
  • Christians believe Jesus, fully God and fully human, did this by his death on the cross.
  • Jesus is the Messiah ("Chosen One", "Anointed"): the saviour God promised.
  • Grace: God's free, undeserved love and help. Christians believe it cannot be earned (Ephesians 2:8–9).
  • Forgiveness: Christians believe that through Jesus, God forgives the sins of all who repent (turn back).

Mission 7

The whole story saves

  • Christians (Catholics call it the Paschal Mystery) see Jesus' life, death, resurrection and ascension as one saving work: redemption (being bought back, set free).
  • Life: in the Incarnation God shares human life; Jesus lives in perfect obedience and shows God's love.
  • Death: Jesus gives his life for others; Christians believe it takes away sin.
  • Resurrection: Jesus defeats death; believers are promised new life (see Mission 5).
  • Ascension: Jesus returns to the Father, and the Spirit is sent to the Church.
  • At the Last Supper Jesus speaks of a New Covenant (new relationship between God and people) sealed in his blood (Matthew 26:28; promised in Jeremiah 31:31).
Mission 7Read the source

Jesus and Nicodemus (John 3:10–21)

  • Context: Nicodemus, a Jewish teacher, visits Jesus by night. Jesus says he must be "born again" (from above) by the Spirit (John 3:3–8).
  • Jesus says the Son of Man must be "lifted up", like the bronze snake Moses lifted up to heal the people (John 3:14; Numbers 21:9).
  • "For God so loved the world, that he gave his only born Son, that whoever believes in him should not perish, but have eternal life." (John 3:16)
  • "For God didn't send his Son into the world to judge the world, but that the world should be saved through him." (John 3:17)
  • Those who refuse to believe have chosen darkness instead of light (John 3:18–21, paraphrase).

Show answer

Q2 According to John 3:17, why did God send his Son?

Show answer

Mission 7

Pictures of the cross

  • No single picture says it all. Christians use several theories of atonement, side by side.
  • Sacrifice: as animals were offered in the Temple for sin, Jesus offers himself, the Lamb of God (John 1:29), once for all.
  • Substitution: Jesus takes the punishment sinners deserve, in their place. Stressed by many evangelical Protestants.
  • Ransom: "For the Son of Man also came not to be served, but to serve, and to give his life as a ransom for many." (Mark 10:45)
  • Reconciliation / example: the cross shows God's love so fully that it wins people back and inspires them to love.
  • Most Christians hold several of these together.

Sacrifice and the Last Supper

  • Matthew 26:26–29: at the Last Supper, Jesus gives bread as his body and wine as his blood.
  • "for this is my blood of the new covenant, which is poured out for many for the remission of sins." (Matthew 26:28)

Q1 According to Matthew 26:28, what is Jesus' blood poured out for?

Show answer

For many, for the forgiveness (remission) of sins.

Mission 7Evaluate

"Faith in Jesus is all a person needs to be saved."

  • Reasons for: "By grace you have been saved through faith … not of works" (Ephesians 2:8–9). The criminal crucified beside Jesus was promised paradise with no time for good works (Luke 23:43). The 39 Articles (XI) teach justification "by Faith, and not for our own works". Grace that had to be earned would not be grace.
  • Reasons against: "Faith, if it has no works, is dead" (James 2:17). In Matthew 25:31–46 people are judged by how they treated the hungry and the stranger. Catholics stress the sacraments, especially baptism (John 3:5). Faith that does not change how someone lives may not be real faith.
  • Another view: many Christians say the question is badly posed: real faith always shows itself in love, so faith and works cannot be separated.
  • Judge it by: What does "faith" mean: belief, or trust that changes a life? Which texts carry most weight, and why? Can a person earn what is a gift?

Atonement means the one who was wronged pays the price and opens the way back. Christians believe God did this in Jesus.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Christians believe in one God in three Persons: Father, Son and Holy Spirit, each fully God, distinct but not separate.
  • At Jesus' baptism (Matthew 3:16–17) all three Persons are present.
  • Some groups, such as Unitarians and Christadelphians, reject the Trinity.

Mission 2

  • A creed is a short statement of shared belief; the Apostles' Creed grew from baptism questions in Rome and has three parts: Father, Son, Holy Spirit.
  • Creeds are used at baptism, in worship and prayer; most churches value them, while Quakers and Unitarians do not use them.

Mission 3

  • Christians believe in the Incarnation: God the Son became human in Jesus, fully God and fully human (John 1:14).
  • He was conceived by the Holy Spirit and born of the Virgin Mary (Luke 1:35).

Mission 4

  • Last Supper (bread and cup, new covenant) → Gethsemane → arrest → trials (council, Pilate) → crucifixion → burial.
  • Christians believe Jesus' death was the centre of God's plan: forgiveness, a new covenant, God sharing human suffering.
  • Denominations remember the cross differently (crucifix or empty cross; views of the Eucharist).

Mission 5

  • Christians believe God raised Jesus bodily on the third day: the empty tomb and the appearances.
  • Christians believe the resurrection shows who Jesus is and gives hope that death is defeated (1 Corinthians 15).
  • At the ascension Jesus returns to the Father, promises the Spirit and will come again.

Mission 6

  • Christians believe the Holy Spirit is the third Person of the Trinity, God present and active on Earth, promised by Jesus.
  • Acts 2 tells how, at Pentecost, wind, fire and many languages marked the Spirit's coming and the start of the Church's mission.
  • Christians differ on how the Spirit shows itself: tongues and gifts in Pentecostal worship, sacraments and liturgy in Catholic and Orthodox worship, silence for Quakers.

Mission 7

  • For Christians, salvation is being saved from sin to eternal life with God; atonement is the repairing of the relationship through Jesus.
  • Christians believe law shows sin, grace is God's free, undeserved love, and the Spirit helps believers live it out.
  • Christians picture the cross as sacrifice, ransom and reconciliation; Catholics and many Protestants differ on how faith and works relate.
