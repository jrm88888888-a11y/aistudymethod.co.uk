Mission 1

Elements, compounds, mixtures

  • An element cannot be broken down into simpler substances by chemical means.
  • A compound contains atoms of different elements, bonded in a fixed ratio.
  • A mixture has no fixed ratio, and its components are not bonded to each other.
  • O₂ is an element: two atoms, but of the same element.
  • A compound's properties differ from its elements' (sodium + chlorine → sodium chloride).
  • A mixture keeps its components' properties, and melts or boils over a range of temperatures.
Quick check
?Which substance is a compound, and for the correct reason?
Mission 1

Separating mixtures

  • Mixtures separate by physical methods; no new substance is made.
  • Dissolving is solvation: solvent molecules surround each solute particle.
  • Filtration: insoluble solid from a liquid. Dissolved particles pass through.
  • Evaporation: recovers a dissolved solid; the solvent is lost.
  • Distillation: the vapour is condensed, so the solvent is recovered too.
  • Recrystallization: dissolve in the minimum of hot solvent, then cool; impurities stay in solution.
  • Paper chromatography: components travel different distances because they are attracted differently to the paper and the solvent.
  • Homogeneous mixtures are uniform (salt solution, air); heterogeneous ones show separate phases (sand in water).

Quick check

Mission 1

Particles in the three states

  • Kinetic molecular theory: matter is moving particles with attractions between them.
  • Solid: particles vibrate about fixed positions. Fixed shape and volume.
  • Liquid: particles close together but slide past each other. Fixed volume.
  • Gas: particles far apart, moving fast and randomly. Easily compressed.
Mission 1

Changes of state and state symbols

  • Melting, freezing, vaporization, condensation, sublimation (solid → gas), deposition (gas → solid).
  • Evaporation happens at the surface at any temperature; boiling happens throughout the liquid, at the boiling point.
  • State symbols: (s), (l), (g), and (aq) for dissolved in water.
  • H₂O(l) → H₂O(g) is vaporization. Water itself is never H₂O(aq).
Quick check
?On a cold, clear night, water vapour in the air turns directly into ice crystals on a car windscreen, without first becoming liquid water. What is the name of this change of state?
Mission 1

What temperature measures

  • Temperature in kelvin is proportional to the average kinetic energy of the particles.
  • Absolute zero: 0 K = −273.15 °C. Kelvin temperatures are never negative.
  • T/K = T/°C + 273 (273 is fine for calculations). A change of 1 K equals a change of 1 °C.
  • Particles in a sample have a range of kinetic energies.
  • Heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference; it is not the same as temperature.
Calculate
1Liquid nitrogen boils at 77 K. What is this temperature in °C?
Quick check
?Which statement about temperature is correct?
Mission 1

Why the temperature stops rising

  • For a pure substance, the temperature stays constant during a change of state.
  • The energy supplied overcomes attractions between particles, so average kinetic energy does not rise.
  • Melting and boiling a molecular substance do not break the covalent bonds inside its molecules.
Quick check
?A molecular solid, X, is heated at a constant rate. Its temperature rises to 80 °C, stays at 80 °C for six minutes while the solid melts, and then rises again. Which explains why the temperature stays constant while X melts?
Mission check

?Which statement about the vaporization of water is correct?

The bubbles are water vapour, H₂O(g). The molecules are unchanged; boiling only overcomes the attractions between them.

Mission 2

The nuclear atom

  • Nearly all of an atom's mass is in a tiny, dense nucleus.
  • Nucleus about 10⁻¹⁵ m across; the atom about 10⁻¹⁰ m.

Mission 2

Reading the nuclear symbol

  • In AZX, Z (atomic number) = number of protons. It fixes the element.
  • A (mass number) = protons + neutrons, so neutrons = A − Z.
  • A neutral atom has Z electrons. An ion has Z minus its charge: a 2− ion has Z + 2.
  • 5626Fe³⁺: 26 protons, 30 neutrons, 23 electrons.
  • 168O²⁻: 8 protons, 8 neutrons, 10 electrons.
  • Ions form by losing or gaining electrons; the number of protons never changes.

Mission 2

Isotopes

  • Isotopes: same number of protons, different numbers of neutrons.
  • Same electron arrangement, so the same chemical properties.
  • Different mass, so different physical properties: density, rate of diffusion, melting and boiling points.
Quick check
?Chlorine (atomic number 17) has isotopes with mass numbers 35 and 37. Pure samples of ³⁵Cl₂ gas and ³⁷Cl₂ gas are compared at the same temperature and pressure. Which statement is correct?
Mission 2

Relative atomic mass, a weighted mean

  • Ar = Σ(relative isotopic mass × % abundance) ÷ 100. Ar has no units.
  • Ar lies between the lightest and heaviest isotope, closer to the more abundant one.
  • Worked example, boron: ¹⁰B 19.9% (10.01), ¹¹B 80.1% (11.01).
  • Ar = (10.01 × 19.9 + 11.01 × 80.1) ÷ 100 = (199.2 + 881.9) ÷ 100 = 10.81.
  • Don't divide again by the number of isotopes: the weighted sum is already the mean.
  • Working back from Ar: call one abundance x% and the other (100 − x)%, then solve for x.
Calculate
1Lithium is 7.59% ⁶Li (relative isotopic mass 6.015) and 92.41% ⁷Li (7.016). Calculate the relative atomic mass of lithium to three significant figures.
Calculate
1Gallium has two isotopes, ⁶⁹Ga (relative isotopic mass 68.926) and ⁷¹Ga (70.925). Its relative atomic mass is 69.723. Calculate the percentage abundance of ⁶⁹Ga to three significant figures.

Make a guess: Why do fireworks come in different colours?

Mission 3

Wavelength, frequency, energy

  • Light travels as photons. For light, c = λf (in the data booklet).
  • Shorter wavelength → higher frequency → more energy per photon.
  • Visible light: red has the lowest energy, violet the highest.
  • Beyond violet is ultraviolet; beyond red is infrared.
Mission 3

How atoms give out light

  • An electron absorbs energy and moves up to a higher level: an excited state.
  • When it falls back, it emits a photon. Photon energy = the energy gap.
  • A continuous spectrum contains all wavelengths. An emission line spectrum is bright lines on a dark background.
  • Light is emitted only on the way down, never as the electron moves up.
Quick check
?When sodium compounds are heated in a flame, the flame turns yellow. Which process in the sodium atoms produces this yellow light?
Mission 3

What hydrogen's spectrum proves

  • Hydrogen gives lines, not a continuous band, so its electron has discrete energy levels.
  • Levels get closer together as n rises, so each set of lines converges at high frequency.
  • Falls to n = 1 emit ultraviolet; to n = 2, mainly visible; to n = 3, infrared.
  • One electron, many lines: each line is a different transition, in different atoms of the sample.
Quick check
?Which electron transition in a hydrogen atom emits the photon of highest frequency?
Quick check
?Consider the set of lines in the hydrogen emission spectrum produced by electrons falling to n = 2 from higher levels. Which statement about the lines in this set is correct?
Mission 3

How many electrons a level holds

  • Main levels are numbered n = 1, 2, 3 … outwards from the nucleus.
  • Level n holds at most 2n² electrons: 2, 8, 18, 32.
  • That's n² orbitals, each holding two electrons.
  • The third level holds 18, not 8. "2, 8, 8" is the filling pattern of the first 20 elements, not a capacity.
Quick check

Mission 4: If an electron isn't in an orbit, where is it?

Make a guess: If an electron isn't in an orbit, where is it?

Mission 4

Sublevels

  • Each main level splits into sublevels: s, p, d, f, rising in energy in that order.
  • n = 1: 1s. n = 2: 2s, 2p. n = 3: 3s, 3p, 3d. n = 4 adds 4f.
  • s, p, d, f have 1, 3, 5 and 7 orbitals, so they hold 2, 6, 10 and 14 electrons.
Mission 4

Orbitals

  • An orbital is a region where an electron is likely to be found, not a path.
  • An s orbital is spherical, centred on the nucleus.
  • The three p orbitals are dumbbells along the x, y and z axes, at 90° to each other.
Quick check
?What is the maximum number of electrons that a 3d sublevel can hold, and why?
Mission 4

Three filling rules

  • Aufbau: fill the lowest energy first: 1s 2s 2p 3s 3p 4s 3d 4p.
  • Pauli: at most two electrons per orbital, with opposite spins.
  • Hund: in orbitals of equal energy, electrons go in singly, spins parallel, before pairing.
  • Hund decides how one sublevel fills; Aufbau decides which sublevel fills next.
Mission 4

Writing configurations

  • Iron, Z = 26: 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶ 3d⁶ 4s², condensed to [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s².
  • 4s fills before 3d, but either written order is accepted: [Ar] 4s² 3d⁶ is also correct.
  • Two exceptions up to krypton: Cr [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹ and Cu [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹.
  • Transition metal ions lose 4s electrons first: Fe³⁺ is [Ar] 3d⁵.
  • Main-group ions change the outer sublevel: S²⁻ is 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s² 3p⁶.

Recap

The big ideas

  • Mixtures separate physically; compounds have bonded atoms in a fixed ratio.
  • Temperature in kelvin tracks average kinetic energy; for a pure substance it stays constant during a change of state.
  • Z fixes the element; isotopes differ in neutrons; Ar is an abundance-weighted mean.
  • Line spectra prove discrete energy levels, which converge at higher energy.
  • Configurations: Aufbau, Pauli, Hund; Cr and Cu are exceptions; 4s is lost first.
