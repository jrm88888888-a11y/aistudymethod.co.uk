Mixed Media

This mini-lesson walks you through the Mixed Media endorsed area of Edexcel International GCSE Art & Design: what mixed media is, the techniques you can use, how to build texture and surface, and how bold experimentation turns into a strong personal response.

Work through each screen, answer the questions as you go (some ask you to name a technique, some to pick a media combination, some to match an Assessment Objective) and collect ⭐ stars. Press Start when you're ready.

The big idea

What is mixed media?

Mixed media means deliberately combining two or more different media or materials in a single piece of work. The Edexcel spec describes it as combining and manipulating two-dimensional and three-dimensional media to communicate ideas.

  • 2D media — drawing, painting, printmaking, photography, text and collage.
  • 3D and found materials — fabric, thread, card, wire, objects and things you find.
  • Digital & non-digital — a scan, a photocopy or a photo layered with hand-made marks.

Watch out: mixed media is a deliberate combination chosen to communicate an idea — it is not mess, and it isn't "throwing everything at the page". Every material should earn its place.

Collage & layering

Collage and layering

Collage is the foundation of much mixed-media work: sticking down papers, photos, fabric or found scraps to build an image. Layering is what gives it life — media stacked so earlier marks show through later ones.

Misconception: layering doesn't cover mistakes up — good layering builds depth. Leave some earlier layers showing so the history of the piece stays visible.

Technique 1

Frottage

Frottage means placing paper over a textured surface and rubbing across it with a pencil, crayon or pastel so the texture transfers onto the paper.

Frottage is a fast way to record real-world texture (AO3) and gives you ready-made textured papers to experiment with (AO2).

Techniques 2 & 3

Image transfer & monoprint

  • Image transfer — lifting a printed image (photocopy or magazine photo) onto another surface using gel medium, solvent or heat, so a ghostly version of the picture becomes part of your artwork.
  • Monoprint — rolling ink onto a smooth plate, drawing or pressing into it, then taking a single unique print. "Mono" = one; each pull is different.

Techniques 4 & 5

Stitching & translucent layers

  • Stitching — sewing thread by hand or machine, into paper as well as fabric. It adds real 3D texture, line and colour, and physically joins layers together.
  • Layering translucent materials — tissue paper, tracing paper, acetate, organza or thin glazes let the layers below show through, so colours and marks mix optically.

The heart of AO2

Experiment and take risks

Mixed media rewards experimentation and risk-taking. Trying an odd combination that doesn't work is still valuable evidence — it shows you explored.

AO2 = refine · explore · experimentrefine ideas by exploring and experimenting with media, materials, techniques and processes

Don't wait to "get it right" first time. Run small trials: paint over a photocopy, stitch into paper, drip ink onto wet glue. Keep them all — experiments count.

Misconception: a "failed" experiment is not wasted. Examiners credit the process under AO2 — a page of trials that didn't work still shows you refined your ideas by testing media.

Texture & surface

Building texture and surface

Mixed media is prized for surface — the physical quality of the artwork. There are two kinds of texture:

  • Actual (tactile) texture — real relief you could feel: sand in paint, stitched thread, torn card, modelling paste.
  • Visual (implied) texture — the look of texture on a flat surface: a frottage rubbing, a photo of tree bark, a printed pattern.

Combining both — a real ridge of paste beside a printed pattern — makes a surface that is rich and rewards close looking.

Toward a personal response

From experiments to a personal outcome

Your trials are not the end point. Mixed media supports a strong personal response (AO4): you select the combinations that worked best and pull them into a resolved final piece that realises your intentions.

The assessment cycle

The four Assessment Objectives

Every Edexcel Art & Design endorsed area — including Mixed Media — is marked against the same four AOs, each worth 25%:

AO2 in mixed media

AO2 is your home turf

Of the four AOs, AO2 — refine work by exploring ideas and experimenting with media, materials, techniques and processes — is where mixed media shines. Combining media is experimenting with media.

What strong AO2 looks like

• Several trials of the same idea in different media combinations.

• Notes on what each combination did and which you'll take forward.

• Evidence you selected media on purpose, not by accident.

Remember: "refine" means improving through repeated experiment — so more trials, thoughtfully chosen, mean stronger AO2.

AO3 · Record

Recording in your sketchbook

Your sketchbook is where mixed media lives. Under AO3 — record ideas, observations and insights relevant to your intentions as work progresses, use it to:

  • Stick in every experiment — even the ones that flopped.
  • Take photos of 3D and stitched pieces that won't lie flat.
  • Annotate: what media you used, what happened, what you'll do next.
  • Record real observation — drawings, frottage, photos of your subject.

A sketchbook that shows your thinking — trials plus notes — is worth far more than one full of neat, unlabelled pictures.

AO1 · Develop

Linking to mixed-media artists

Under AO1 — develop ideas through investigations, showing critical understanding of sources, study artists who combine media, then let their approach feed your own trials. Well-known mixed-media artists include:

  • Robert Rauschenberg — "Combines" fusing paint with photos, print and found objects.
  • Hannah Höch — pioneering photomontage and collage.
  • Peter Blake — collage and popular imagery.
  • Louise Nevelson — assembled found-object relief sculpture.

Critical understanding means more than copying: say why an artist combined those media and how it shapes your response.

Composition

Composing a mixed-media piece

Even with many media, a strong piece needs composition — a focal point, a route for the eye, and balance between busy and quiet areas.

Misconception: "more media = better" is false. A cluttered surface with no focal point is weaker than a considered combination of two or three media.

Recap

The key ideas to know

Mixed media: deliberately combining two or more media/materials.

Collage & layering: stick down and stack; translucent layers build depth.

Techniques: frottage, image transfer, stitching, monoprint, layering.

Texture: actual (tactile) + visual (implied) surface.
