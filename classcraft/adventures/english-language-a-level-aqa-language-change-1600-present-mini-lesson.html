Language change · Early Modern English to nowScreen 4

Why the spelling varies

  • William Caxton set up England's first printing press at Westminster in 1476. Print spread the written English of London and the south-east.
  • But no dictionary or agreed spelling existed yet. One word could be spelled two ways on one page.
  • Compositors, who set the type, spelled by habit, sometimes to fit a line.
  • Typical EModE spellings: final -e (Armes), doubled consonants (Naturall), y for i (heyre), u for v inside words (loue), capitals on nouns (Flesh).
Language change · Early Modern English to nowScreen 5

The sounds moved too: the Great Vowel Shift

  • Between about 1400 and 1700, the long vowels of English changed. Most were said higher in the mouth; the two highest became diphthongs.
WordLong vowel c.1400Today
bite/iː/ (like beet)/aɪ/
meet/eː//iː/
mate/aː//eɪ/
out/uː/ (like oot)/aʊ/
boot/oː//uː/
  • Spelling did not follow the new sounds. That is a major reason English spelling and pronunciation no longer match.

?Shakespeare's Folio prints Heart-ake and heyre. Why so wild?

In 1623 English spelling had not been standardised. Print was spreading one written form of English, but compositors still chose among variant spellings, and the sounds of English had shifted too, so spelling and pronunciation were already drifting apart.

Language change · lexis and semanticsScreen 3

Old words, new meanings

Semantic change is change in what a word means. Four directions to name:

TypeWhat happensExample
Broadeningmeaning widensholiday: Old English "holy day" → any day off work (14th century)
Narrowingmeaning narrowsmeat: "food" → animal flesh (c.1300)
Ameliorationmeaning becomes more positivenice: "foolish" (late 13th century) → "agreeable" (1769)
Pejorationmeaning becomes more negativesilly: "happy, blessed" → "foolish" (1570s)
  • Meanings change through use: speakers stretch a word into new contexts until the new sense takes over.

Language change · lexis and semanticsScreen 6

Reading an old meaning in context

  • In the 1640s condescension meant, in Etymonline's words, a voluntary inclining to equality with inferiors: a person of rank being gracious to those below. Collins uses it in this sense.
  • The negative sense, a mere show of stooping to others, came later: pejoration.
  • Austen's irony works with the old sense: Collins sincerely admires behaviour the novel lets readers see as pride.
  • Before claiming a change, check a historical dictionary such as the OED.

Mission checkScreen 9

?Nice once meant "foolish". How does a word flip its meaning?

Through use. Over centuries speakers stretched nice from "foolish" through "fussy", "delicate" and "precise" to "agreeable" (by 1769), each new context nudging the meaning. That positive shift is called amelioration.

Language change · standardisation and grammarScreen 2

Rules written down, and why people followed them

Stage (Haugen, 1966)In English
SelectionLondon and south-eastern written English became the basis
Codificationdictionaries (Johnson) and grammars (Lowth) wrote the norms down
Elaborationthe standard was used for law, science and literature
Acceptanceprint, schools and social ambition spread it
  • Johnson's Dictionary defined about 40,000 words with quotations from writers, but kept forms like musick and publick.
  • Lowth's grammar was prescriptive: it ranked usages. A preposition before its relative (with whom) was, he wrote, "more graceful, as well as more perspicuous". His 1763 edition added that two negatives cancel each other out.
  • People listened because "correct" English signalled education and status.

?Who decided how English should be spelled, and why did anyone listen?

No one person decided. Printers selected London-based forms, dictionaries such as Johnson's (1755) and grammars such as Lowth's (1762) codified them, and people accepted them because print, schools and "correct" English were tied to education and status.

?Is literally meaning "figuratively" a decline, or just change?

Linguists call it change. Literally has been used for emphasis since at least the 19th century, as Chopin's 1899 novel shows, and intensifiers often weaken this way. Calling it a decline is a prescriptive attitude; a descriptive answer looks at the evidence first.

Mission 5

Is a full stop in a text message rude?

Make a guess: Is a full stop in a text message rude?

Language change · digital communicationScreen 1

Digital writing: a mixed mode

  • Text messages and chats are written, but often fast, informal and two-way like speech.
  • David Crystal called internet language Netspeak (Language and the Internet, 2001) and argued it is a new mix, neither speech nor writing.
  • Features that stand in for voice and face: emoji, repeated punctuation (!!), capitals for shouting, letter stretching (sooo).
  • Features that save time: initialisms (lol), phonetic respelling (tho), clipping (info), letter and number homophones (gr8).

Language change · digital communicationScreen 4

The full-stop study

  • Gunraj and colleagues at Binghamton University (2016) showed 126 undergraduates short exchanges ending in one-word replies such as Okay, Sure, Yeah, Yup, with or without a full stop.
  • In text messages, replies with a full stop were rated less sincere. In handwritten notes there was no difference.
  • In a text, pressing send ends the message, so a full stop is optional. Adding it carries pragmatic meaning: finality, even coldness.

?Is a full stop in a text message rude?

Not always, but it can seem so. In texting, sending a message already ends it, so a full stop is optional; using one adds meaning such as finality or coldness. Gunraj and colleagues (2016) found readers rated full-stopped text replies less sincere, but not handwritten ones.

Recap

Big ideas

  • English is usually split into Old, Middle, Early Modern (c.1500–1700) and Late Modern English; the boundaries are rough.
  • Printing (Caxton, 1476) spread written English, but EModE spelling still varied: compositors chose forms such as Naturall and heyre.
  • The Great Vowel Shift (c.1400–1700) changed the long vowels, leaving many spellings behind their sounds.
  • New words come from borrowing, coinage, compounding, blending, affixation and conversion, usually to meet new needs.
  • Semantic change has four directions to name: broadening, narrowing, amelioration and pejoration.
  • In an older text, check a word's meaning at the time (e.g. Austen's condescension) before judging it.
  • Standardisation took centuries: Caxton's press (1476), dictionaries (Cawdrey 1604, Johnson 1755) and grammars (Lowth 1762).
  • Haugen's stages (selection, codification, elaboration, acceptance) explain why a standard spreads; prestige and schooling made people follow it.
  • EModE grammar differs from ours in pronouns (thou), inflections (-st, -eth), questions and negatives without do, and the subjunctive.
  • Prescriptivism judges usage against rules and sees change as decay; descriptivism records usage and sees change as natural.
  • Theories of change: the S-curve (how it spreads), random fluctuation, functional theory and substratum theory (why it happens).
  • Aitchison's metaphors (damp spoon, crumbling castle, infectious disease) name common worries, which she argued are mistaken.
  • Digital communication mixes speech and writing; Crystal called it Netspeak.
  • In texting the full stop has gained a pragmatic meaning (finality, coldness): Gunraj et al. (2016) found full-stopped replies rated less sincere.
  • Current change includes conversion (to google), blending (podcast), compounding (hashtag) and semantic change (troll, cloud).
