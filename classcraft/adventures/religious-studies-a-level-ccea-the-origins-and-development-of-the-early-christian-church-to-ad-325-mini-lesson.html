Mission 1: How did a banned faith with no army grow across an empire, and did its martyrs help?

Make a guess: How did a banned faith with no army grow across an empire, and did its martyrs help?

Mission 1

From a sect to an empire-wide church

  • About AD 30: a small Jewish movement in Jerusalem following Jesus as Messiah.
  • By about 112, the Roman governor Pliny finds Christians in towns and villages of Bithynia (north Turkey) (see Mission 2).
  • By about 300: churches in almost every city of the empire, strongest in the East and North Africa.
  • All this before Constantine favoured the Church (from 312), and while being Christian could cost your life.
  • No reliable census: numbers are estimates. Historians ask: why and how did it spread?
Mission 1

Religious factors

  • A clear message: one God, forgiveness, and a future life with resurrection; open to all.
  • Jewish roots: the Diaspora synagogues across the empire; the Greek Old Testament (Septuagint); "God-fearers", Gentiles already drawn to Jewish monotheism.
  • Community and care: food for widows and orphans, care of the sick and of prisoners, burial of the poor; welcome across class and gender ("bond or free", 1 Corinthians 12:13).
  • Moral life: a demanding ethic that impressed some outsiders.
  • Reported miracles and exorcisms: Acts and later writers claim healing power.
  • Zeal and mission: an exclusive faith that expected every member to witness.
  • Martyrs' witness: courage under death attracted attention.
Mission 1

Political and social factors

  • Pax Romana: two centuries of relative peace; safe travel.
  • Roman roads and sea routes linking cities; Paul's journeys follow them.
  • Common languages: koine Greek in the East, Latin in the West; the New Testament written in Greek.
  • Cities: dense urban networks where news and households mixed.
  • Religious variety: Rome tolerated many cults; Christianity was at first seen as a branch of Judaism.
  • Social stress: plagues (e.g. from 165 and 249) and insecurity; Christians nursing the sick may have survived and won converts.
  • These factors made spread possible; religious factors help explain why people joined.
Mission 1

Two explanations of growth

  • Edward Gibbon (1737–1794), The Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire, vol. 1 (1776), chapter 15, gives five causes (paraphrase):
  • (1) intolerant zeal inherited from Judaism; (2) the doctrine of a future life; (3) miraculous powers claimed by the Church; (4) pure and austere morals; (5) the union and discipline of the Christian community.
  • Gibbon offered these as "secondary" causes, with an ironic tone towards the Church.
  • Rodney Stark (1934–2022), The Rise of Christianity (1996): a sociologist's model.
  • Growth through personal networks (family, friends), not mass meetings; a steady rate of about 40% a decade would turn a few thousand into millions by 300.
  • Care in epidemics, high esteem for women, and a Jewish base explain the steady rate.
  • Critics: Stark's numbers are modelled, not measured; much depends on starting figures.

Mission 1

Polycarp of Smyrna

  • Bishop of Smyrna (Izmir, Turkey); said by Irenaeus to have known the apostle John. Ignatius wrote to him.
  • Martyred about 155–156 (some date it 167); the Martyrdom of Polycarp, a letter from the church of Smyrna, is the earliest martyr account outside the New Testament.
  • The crowd demands him at games; he is found in a farmhouse and arrested.
  • The proconsul urges: swear by the emperor's genius, say "Away with the atheists", revile Christ.
  • His reply (paraphrase): eighty-six years he has served Christ, who has done him no wrong; how can he blaspheme his King who saved him?
  • Burnt; the account says the fire formed an arch around him and he was then stabbed.
  • The letter presents him as imitating Christ and warns against seeking martyrdom rashly.
Mission 1

Justin Martyr

  • Born in Flavia Neapolis (Nablus, Samaria) to a pagan family; studied philosophy (Stoic, Pythagorean, Platonist).
  • Converted after meeting an old man who pointed him to the prophets; Christianity became for him "the true philosophy".
  • Taught in Rome; wrote the First and Second Apology and the Dialogue with Trypho.
  • About 165, under Marcus Aurelius, tried with six companions before the prefect Junius Rusticus; refused to sacrifice; beheaded (Acts of Justin).
  • Impact: a philosopher dying for the faith showed educated Romans that Christianity was not only for the poor; his name became "Martyr".
Mission 1

Blandina of Lyons

  • 177, Lyons and Vienne in Gaul (France). Mob violence, then a governor's trial. Source: a letter of the churches, preserved by Eusebius, Church History 5.1.
  • Blandina: a slave woman, physically weak; her mistress feared she would deny Christ.
  • Tortured from morning to evening; she repeated (paraphrase) "I am a Christian, and nothing wicked is done among us".
  • Hung on a stake in the arena; to the others "she seemed to them like Christ" crucified (paraphrase of the letter).
  • Last to die, after encouraging a fifteen-year-old, Ponticus.
  • Impact: the lowest in Roman society shown as the strongest; women and slaves as heroes of faith.

Mission 1

Why martyrdom had impact

  • Martyr (Greek martys) means witness: first in court, then by death.
  • Public: executions in arenas made the faith visible and talked about.
  • Courage puzzled outsiders: calm deaths seemed to prove sincere belief in resurrection.
  • Identity: martyr stories were read aloud on the anniversary (the "birthday") of death; relics and tombs became places of worship.
  • Theology: Christians believed martyrs imitate Christ's passion and are united with him at once.
  • Tertullian (c. 155–c. 220), Apology 50 (197): "semen est sanguis Christianorum", "the blood of Christians is seed" (literal translation). Often quoted as "the blood of the martyrs is the seed of the Church".
  • But: some Christians lapsed under pressure, and some outsiders (Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 11.3) saw only obstinacy.
Mission 1Evaluate

"Martyrdom was the main reason the Church grew."

  • For: Tertullian's claim; Justin says the courage of Christians facing death helped convert him (Second Apology 12, paraphrase).
  • For: martyr stories built a strong, disciplined identity (Gibbon's fifth cause).
  • Against: persecution was local and occasional before 250; most Christians never saw a martyrdom.
  • Against: Stark: steady growth through networks and care, not dramatic events.
  • Against: many observers were repelled or unmoved; persecution also produced lapsed Christians and division.
  • Judge it by: How common was martyrdom? What did converts say drew them? Does the evidence come mainly from Christian writers who valued martyrs?
Mission 1

Martyrdom within religion

  • Judaism: kiddush ha-Shem, "sanctifying the Name": dying rather than commit idolatry; the Maccabean martyrs (2 Maccabees 7); Rabbi Akiva (died about 135).
  • Christianity: Stephen (Acts 7:59–60); "Be faithful to death, and I will give you the crown of life" (Revelation 2:10). Orthodox and Catholic calendars honour martyrs; Protestant traditions remember e.g. the Reformation martyrs; 20th-century martyrs include Dietrich Bonhoeffer and Óscar Romero.
  • Islam: shahid (witness), honoured by Sunni and Shi'a Muslims; Husayn's death at Karbala (680) is central to Shi'a devotion.
  • Sikhism: shaheed; Guru Arjan (1606) and Guru Tegh Bahadur (1675).
  • Most traditions distinguish martyrdom (accepting death rather than deny faith) from seeking death or killing others.
Mission 1Evaluate

Is martyrdom still important in religion today?

  • For: martyrs give examples of integrity: faith that is worth more than life.
  • For: many believers still face death for their faith; remembering martyrs supports them and challenges complacency.
  • Against: the language of martyrdom can be misused to glorify violence or suicide attacks, which mainstream teachers in each tradition reject.
  • Against: honouring martyrs can encourage a victim identity or division between groups.
  • Against: most believers live faith through ordinary witness; Christian tradition also speaks of "white" (non-bloody) martyrdom, e.g. ascetic life.
  • Judge it by: Does the martyr seek death or accept it? Does the memory build peace or hostility? Is it chosen witness or imposed suffering?

Mission 2

Causes of persecution

  • Religious: Christians refused to sacrifice to the gods or the emperor's image. Romans believed the pax deorum (peace with the gods) kept the state safe, so refusal was dangerous. Christians were called "atheists".
  • Rumours: secret meetings and "body and blood" talk led to charges of cannibalism and incest (calling each other "brother" and "sister").
  • Social: Christians withdrew from festivals, games and some trades; they split families; they attacked idol-making trades (compare Acts 19:23–27).
  • Scapegoating: blamed for disasters. Tertullian, Apology 40 (paraphrase): if the Tiber floods or the Nile does not, the cry is "The Christians to the lion!"
  • Political: unlicensed associations were suspect; Christians honoured a crucified man as "Lord".
  • Separation from Judaism: Jews were exempt from sacrifice by custom; once Christians were seen as a separate group, they lost that cover.
Mission 2

What was the legal basis?

  • No surviving empire-wide law against Christians before 250.
  • Trials were by governors using their own judgement (cognitio), usually after local accusers brought cases.
  • G. E. M. de Ste. Croix (1910–2000), "Why Were the Early Christians Persecuted?" (Past and Present, 1963): Christians were punished for "the name" itself; the deeper cause was fear that their refusal to worship angered the gods. He rejected A. N. Sherwin-White's view that the real offence was obstinacy (contumacia) before the governor.
  • W. H. C. Frend (1916–2005), Martyrdom and Persecution in the Early Church (1965): sets persecution within Jewish martyr traditions and local pagan hostility.
  • So the pattern before 250 was local, sporadic, mob-driven; after 250 it became imperial and systematic.
Mission 2

Nero (54–68)

  • AD 64: the great fire of Rome. Rumour blamed Nero.
  • Tacitus, Annals 15.44 (about 116): Nero shifted blame to the Christians, a group hated for their "abominations"; "Christus" was executed under Pontius Pilate.
  • Convicted "not so much of arson as of hatred of the human race" (Latin odio humani generis, literal translation).
  • Cruel deaths: covered in skins and torn by dogs; crucified; burnt as torches in Nero's gardens. Even Tacitus says this stirred pity.
  • Suetonius, Nero 16: punishment of Christians, "a class of men given to a new and harmful superstition" (literal translation).
  • Tradition: Peter and Paul died in Rome under Nero (1 Clement 5; later writers).
  • Nature: local to Rome, a scapegoat, not an empire-wide policy.
Mission 2

Domitian (81–96)

  • Later Christian writers (Melito, in Eusebius, Church History 4.26; Eusebius 3.17–20) call Domitian the second persecutor.
  • Dio Cassius 67.14: in 95 Domitian executed his cousin Flavius Clemens and exiled his wife Domitilla for "atheism" and drifting into "Jewish ways". Some think they were Christians; evidence is uncertain.
  • Domitian demanded the title "Lord and God" (dominus et deus); emperor-worship was strong in Asia Minor.
  • Revelation (traditionally dated to his reign, Irenaeus) reflects pressure on churches in Asia: "Be faithful to death" (Revelation 2:10).
  • Many historians (e.g. Leonard Thompson, 1990) doubt a systematic persecution: perhaps local pressure, later exaggerated.
  • Nature: uncertain, possibly targeted at a few and local in Asia.

Mission 2Read the source

Pliny's Letter to Trajan (Letters 10.96)

About 112, Pliny the Younger, governor of Bithynia-Pontus, writes to the emperor Trajan (paraphrase of key points):

  • He has never attended trials of Christians and does not know whether to punish the name itself or crimes attached to the name.
  • His method: ask three times, with warning; execute those who persist, because obstinacy deserves punishment; send Roman citizens to Rome.
  • An anonymous pamphlet named many. Those who denied it invoked the gods, offered incense and wine to Trajan's image and cursed Christ, which, he hears, true Christians cannot be forced to do.
  • Former Christians said they met on a fixed day before dawn, sang "carmen… Christo quasi deo", a hymn to Christ as to a god (literal translation), bound themselves by oath not to commit crimes, then met later for ordinary, harmless food.
  • He tortured two slave women called ministrae (deaconesses) and found only a "depraved, excessive superstition".
  • The faith had spread to cities, villages and farms; temples were emptying, but now reviving.

Mission 2

Trajan's reply (Letters 10.97)

  • Pliny acted correctly; no fixed rule can be laid down for every case.
  • Christians must not be hunted out.
  • If accused and convicted, they must be punished.
  • If they deny it and prove it by worshipping the gods, they are pardoned, whatever their past.
  • Anonymous accusations must not be accepted: a bad precedent and "not of our age" (paraphrase).
  • Tertullian (Apology 2) mocked the logic: if they are guilty, why not seek them out? If innocent, why punish them?
  • This rescript shaped policy for over a century: Christianity was punishable, but enforcement depended on accusers.
  • By tradition, Ignatius of Antioch died in Rome under Trajan.
Mission 2

Marcus Aurelius (161–180)

  • A Stoic philosopher-emperor; no new general law, but a time of plague, war and anxiety, when angry crowds sought scapegoats.
  • Melito of Sardis complains of "new decrees" in Asia that let informers seize Christians' property (in Eusebius, Church History 4.26, paraphrase).
  • Justin martyred in Rome about 165 (see Mission 1).
  • Lyons and Vienne, 177: mob attacks, then trials; the governor consulted Marcus, who replied (as Trajan had) that those who denied should be freed and the rest executed. Blandina died then.
  • Polycarp's martyrdom is dated by some to this reign (167), by most to the reign of Antoninus Pius (155–156).
  • Nature: still local, but more frequent and violent, driven by crowds and accusers.

Mission 2

Decius (249–251): the first empire-wide persecution

  • Edict of 250: every inhabitant of the empire must sacrifice to the gods before local commissioners, and receive a certificate (libellus). Dozens of libelli survive on papyrus from Egypt.
  • Aim: restore the pax deorum in a time of invasion and crisis; the edict did not name Christians, but hit them hardest.
  • Bishop Fabian of Rome martyred; Origen tortured; Cyprian of Carthage went into hiding (to lead from a distance, he said).
  • Many Christians lapsed: some sacrificed (sacrificati), some bought false certificates (libellatici).
  • Effects: a crisis over the lapsed: rigorists (Novatian) refused readmission; Cyprian allowed it after penance (On the Lapsed, 251). Schism followed.
  • Later, under Valerian (257–258), clergy were targeted; Cyprian was executed in 258.
Mission 2

Diocletian: the Great Persecution (303–311)

  • Four edicts, 303–304 (urged, Lactantius says, by Diocletian's Caesar Galerius): (1) destroy churches, burn scriptures, remove Christians' legal rights; (2) arrest clergy; (3) free clergy who sacrifice, torture those who refuse; (4) everyone must sacrifice.
  • Severe in the East (Egypt, Palestine, Asia); light in the West under Constantius (Constantine's father).
  • Sources: Lactantius, On the Deaths of the Persecutors; Eusebius, Church History 8 and Martyrs of Palestine.
  • Traditores ("handers-over") gave up scriptures; disputes over them led to the Donatist schism in North Africa.
  • Galerius' edict of toleration, 311; then the "Edict of Milan" agreement of Constantine and Licinius, 313.
  • Change in nature: from local and accusation-led to imperial, systematic, targeting leaders, buildings and books.
Mission 2Evaluate

"Persecution strengthened the Early Church more than it harmed it."

  • For: martyrs inspired; the Church grew through the 3rd century; identity, discipline and the canon of scripture (which books to die for) sharpened.
  • For: the Church survived Diocletian and emerged as the dominant faith within decades.
  • Against: the lapsed and traditores caused bitter schisms (Novatianist, Donatist) lasting centuries.
  • Against: leaders were killed, buildings and books destroyed, families divided; fear made some hide their faith.
  • Against: much growth seems to have come in quiet periods (e.g. 260–303), not during persecution.
  • Judge it by: short-term or long-term effects? Numbers or unity? Can we trust sources mostly written by the winners (Eusebius, Lactantius)?
Mission 2Evaluate

Persecution of believers: a perennial issue

  • Patterns that recur: blaming minorities for disasters; rumour and dehumanising labels; laws requiring public conformity; local mobs acting where the state allows.
  • Today: human rights reports record restrictions and violence against believers of many religions (Christians, Muslims, Jews, Baha'is, Ahmadis, Yazidis, and others) and against non-believers.
  • Christian responses: "Blessed are you when people… persecute you" (Matthew 5:11); pray for persecutors; or work for religious freedom through law and advocacy.
  • Questions: Is persecution always religious, or often ethnic and political? Does it purify faith, or only harm? When is a believer persecuted, and when merely criticised?
  • Judge it by: Who defines "persecution"? Does the Roman pattern (pressure to conform, scapegoating) explain modern cases? What duty do states have to protect belief?

Mission 3

Roots in the New Testament

  • Jewish background: ritual washings for purity; immersion of Gentile converts (debated how early).
  • John the Baptist: "the baptism of repentance for forgiveness of sins" (Mark 1:4). He promised one who "will baptise you in the Holy Spirit" (Mark 1:8).
  • Jesus' baptism by John (Mark 1:9–11): the Spirit descends; a voice calls him "my beloved Son".
  • The command: "make disciples of all nations, baptising them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit" (Matthew 28:19).
  • Acts: "Repent and be baptised… in the name of Jesus Christ for the forgiveness of sins, and you will receive the gift of the Holy Spirit" (Acts 2:38). About 3,000 baptised that day (2:41).
  • Others: the Ethiopian official, at roadside water (Acts 8:36–38); whole households (Acts 16:15, 33).
  • Pattern: hear → believe and repent → baptism → Spirit → community.

Mission 3

The Didache (chapter 7)

  • The Didache ("Teaching of the Twelve Apostles"): a short church manual, probably Syrian, dated about 70–120 (debated).
  • First comes teaching: the "Two Ways", of life and of death (chapters 1–6).
  • Then (paraphrase of chapter 7): baptise in the name of the Father, Son and Holy Spirit in "living" (running) water.
  • If none, other water; cold, or if necessary warm.
  • If there is not enough for immersion, pour water three times on the head in the triple name.
  • Before baptism, the baptiser, the candidate and others who can should fast one or two days.
  • Only the baptised may share the Eucharist (chapter 9).
  • Development: a Trinitarian formula, flexibility of method, and preparation by teaching and fasting.
Mission 3

Justin Martyr (First Apology 61)

  • Written in Rome about 150–155, to explain Christian rites to the emperor.
  • Those persuaded that the teaching is true and who promise to live by it are taught to pray and fast for forgiveness; the community prays and fasts with them.
  • They are brought where there is water and are "born again" as the believers were, washed in the name of the Father, of Jesus Christ, and of the Holy Spirit (paraphrase).
  • He cites John 3 ("unless you are born again…").
  • Baptism is called illumination (Greek photismos): the mind is enlightened.
  • Afterwards the newly baptised are led to the assembly for common prayer, the kiss of peace and the Eucharist (chapter 65).
  • Development: baptism as free choice (not the "necessity" of birth), rebirth and illumination; joined directly to the Eucharist.

Mission 3

Hippolytus: The Apostolic Tradition

  • A church order traditionally attributed to Hippolytus of Rome (died about 235), dated about 215. Authorship, date and unity are debated: many scholars see it as a layered text.
  • Catechumenate (period of instruction): normally three years; candidates' lives and jobs examined; some jobs (e.g. gladiator, idol-maker) must be given up.
  • Final weeks: daily exorcism; fasting on Friday and Saturday; an all-night vigil.
  • At cockcrow, prayer over the water. Children first (parents answer for those who cannot speak), then men, then women. Candidates remove clothing and jewellery.
  • Renunciation of Satan; anointing with the oil of exorcism.
  • Threefold questions ("Do you believe in God the Father…?" "in Christ Jesus…?" "in the Holy Spirit…?") with threefold immersion.
  • Anointing; the bishop lays on hands and anoints again; the kiss of peace; first Eucharist with cups of water and of milk and honey (the promised land).
Mission 3

Tertullian: On Baptism

  • Tertullian of Carthage (c. 155–c. 220), On Baptism (De Baptismo, about 200–206): the oldest surviving treatise on any Christian sacrament.
  • Written against Quintilla, who mocked the idea that plain water could save.
  • "We, little fishes" (paraphrase of ch. 1), after our Fish (ICHTHYS: "Jesus Christ, God's Son, Saviour"), are born in water.
  • Water was the place of the Spirit at creation (Genesis 1:2): God works through simple things.
  • Who may baptise: the bishop, then presbyters and deacons with his authority; laymen in emergency; not women (ch. 17).
  • Delay advised (ch. 18) for young children ("let them come when they understand") and for the unmarried, because sin after baptism is so serious.
  • Baptism given by heretics is not true baptism (ch. 15).
Mission 3

Cyprian of Carthage

  • Cyprian, bishop of Carthage 248–258, martyred under Valerian (see Mission 2).
  • Infant baptism: a council of 66 bishops (Letter 64, to Fidus, about 253) rejected waiting until the eighth day (like circumcision): no infant should be kept from God's grace.
  • Clinical baptism (on a sickbed, by sprinkling) is valid (Letter 69/75, to Magnus).
  • Rebaptism controversy (255–256): baptism by heretics or schismatics is invalid, so converts from them must be baptised in the Church. Pope Stephen of Rome disagreed: they need only laying on of hands.
  • Behind this: the Church is one; "salus extra ecclesiam non est", "outside the Church there is no salvation" (Letter 73.21, literal translation).
  • Development: baptism tied tightly to the one Church and its bishops; infant baptism accepted as normal in North Africa.

Mission 3

How doctrine and practice developed

  • Preparation: from immediate baptism (Acts 2, 8) → fasting (Didache, Justin) → a three-year catechumenate (Hippolytus).
  • Form: water and the Trinitarian name throughout; immersion preferred; pouring allowed (Didache); questions and threefold immersion (Hippolytus).
  • Additional rites: renunciation, exorcism, anointing and laying on of hands by the bishop (later separated as confirmation in the West; kept together as chrismation in the East).
  • Doctrine: forgiveness, new birth, gift of the Spirit (NT) → illumination (Justin) → necessary for salvation and linked to the one Church (Tertullian, Cyprian).
  • Who: adult converts first; children and infants by 200 (Hippolytus, Tertullian's objection, Cyprian).
  • Minister: increasingly the bishop or those he authorises.
Mission 3Evaluate

Did the Early Church baptise infants?

  • Yes: household baptisms (Acts 16:15, 33; 1 Corinthians 1:16) probably included children; Hippolytus and Cyprian assume it; Origen calls it a tradition from the apostles.
  • Joachim Jeremias (1900–1979), Infant Baptism in the First Four Centuries (German 1958): infant baptism was the apostolic practice.
  • No (or not at first): no NT text clearly mentions an infant; the NT pattern is believe, then be baptised; Tertullian's objection shows it was not settled.
  • Kurt Aland (1915–1994), Did the Early Church Baptize Infants? (German 1961): no clear evidence before about 200.
  • Judge it by: Does silence about infants mean absence? Does "household" include babies? Does the theology of grace (Cyprian) or of faith and repentance (Acts 2:38) fit better?
Mission 3

Initiation in Christian life today

  • Catholic and Orthodox: infant baptism; Orthodox give chrismation and communion at once (as in Hippolytus); Catholics confirm later.
  • Anglican, Lutheran, Methodist, Presbyterian: infant baptism with sponsors; later confirmation or profession of faith.
  • Baptist and most Pentecostal and many independent churches: believer's baptism by full immersion, after a personal profession of faith; infants are dedicated.
  • Quakers and the Salvation Army do not practise outward baptism: they stress the inward baptism of the Spirit.
  • Adult converts in Catholic parishes follow the RCIA/OCIA, modelled on the ancient catechumenate.
Mission 3Evaluate

Is initiation still relevant to believers?

  • For: it marks belonging: entry into a community, not just private belief (1 Corinthians 12:13).
  • For: it shapes identity and commitment, and links believers to the tradition back to the apostles.
  • For: in other religions too, initiation (bar/bat mitzvah, Amrit Sanskar, upanayana) expresses commitment and responsibility.
  • Against: infant rites may mean little if not followed by personal faith; many baptised people never practise.
  • Against: Quakers and the Salvation Army show that faithful Christian life is possible without outward rites.
  • Judge it by: Is initiation a cause of faith (grace given) or an expression of it (faith declared)? Does belonging come before believing, or after?

From a river in the Jordan valley to a night vigil in Rome, baptism kept its centre (water, the triple name, new life) while its preparation and rites grew. Next: the Eucharist.

Mission 4: How did a shared supper become the Church's central act of worship within two hundred years?

Make a guess: How did a shared supper become the Church's central act of worship within two hundred years?

Mission 4Read the source

The earliest account

> "For I received from the Lord that which also I delivered to you, that the Lord Jesus on the night in which he was betrayed took bread. When he had given thanks, he broke it and said, 'Take, eat. This is my body, which is broken for you. Do this in memory of me.'" (1 Corinthians 11:23–24, WEBBE)

  • Paul writes about AD 53–55: earlier than any Gospel.
  • The cup: "This cup is the new covenant in my blood" (11:25).
  • "As often as you eat this bread and drink this cup, you proclaim the Lord's death until he comes" (11:26): past (death), present (proclaim), future (until he comes).

Q1 What two actions does Jesus command to be repeated?

Show answer

Eating the bread and drinking the cup, "in memory of me".

Q2 What does "received… delivered" suggest about this account?

Show answer

It is a tradition passed on in the Church before Paul wrote.

Mission 4

The Last Supper in the Gospels

  • Mark 14:22–25 (and Matthew 26:26–29): "Take, eat. This is my body" (Mark 14:22); "This is my blood of the new covenant, which is poured out for many" (Mark 14:24).
  • Luke 22:14–20: closest to Paul, with "Do this in memory of me" (Luke 22:19).
  • Two families of wording: Mark–Matthew and Paul–Luke.
  • Setting: in the Synoptics, a Passover meal (deliverance, covenant, a lamb); in John, Jesus dies as the Passover lambs are killed, and the supper has no bread-and-cup words.
  • Key ideas: covenant (Exodus 24:8), sacrifice ("poured out for many"), memorial (Greek anamnesis), and the coming Kingdom (Mark 14:25).
Mission 4

Meal, sharing and John 6

  • Acts: "the breaking of bread" (Acts 2:42), in homes (2:46), "on the first day of the week" (20:7).
  • Corinth: the Lord's Supper was part of a real meal; rich members ate first while the poor went hungry. Paul: "it is not the Lord's supper that you eat" (1 Corinthians 11:20).
  • Eating "in an unworthy way" without discerning "the Lord's body" brings judgement (11:29).
  • Sharing: "Because there is one loaf of bread, we, who are many, are one body" (1 Corinthians 10:17); the cup is "a sharing of the blood of Christ" (10:16; Greek koinonia).
  • John 6: "the bread which I will give for the life of the world is my flesh" (6:51); "He who eats my flesh and drinks my blood has eternal life" (6:54). Many read it as Eucharistic; others as about faith.
Mission 4

The Didache (chapters 9, 10, 14)

  • Prayers of thanksgiving (eucharistia): the cup first, then the broken bread (chapter 9).
  • Bread prayer (paraphrase): as this broken bread was scattered on the mountains and gathered to become one, so may your Church be gathered from the ends of the earth into your Kingdom.
  • Only the baptised may eat: "do not give what is holy to the dogs" (paraphrase, using Matthew 7:6).
  • Chapter 10: thanksgiving after being filled (probably a real meal); ends with "Maranatha" ("Come, Lord!").
  • Chapter 14: on the Lord's Day gather, break bread, give thanks, confess sins first, be reconciled, so that "your sacrifice may be pure"; it cites Malachi 1:11 ("a pure offering").
  • No words of institution; strong themes of unity, holiness and expectation.

Mission 4

Justin Martyr (First Apology 65–67)

  • Two descriptions: after a baptism (65) and every Sunday (67).
  • Sunday order: memoirs of the apostles (Gospels) or prophets read → the president (proestos) instructs → all stand and pray → bread and wine mixed with water brought → president gives thanks "as he is able" → people answer "Amen" → deacons distribute, and take portions to the absent → a collection for orphans, widows, the sick, prisoners and strangers.
  • Doctrine (66, paraphrase): this food is not received as common bread and drink; as Christ was made flesh by the Word of God, so the food blessed by his word is the flesh and blood of the incarnate Jesus.
  • Only for those who believe, are baptised and live as Christ taught.
  • Why Sunday: the day of creation and of the resurrection.
  • Development: a Sunday service separate from a full meal; a structure close to later liturgy (word, then sacrament).
Mission 4

Hippolytus: the Eucharistic prayer

  • The Apostolic Tradition (c. 215; authorship and date debated) gives a model Eucharistic prayer for a new bishop.
  • Dialogue: "The Lord be with you… Lift up your hearts… Let us give thanks to the Lord" (standard opening, in summary).
  • Thanksgiving for Christ's incarnation, death and descent; the words of institution.
  • Anamnesis: "remembering his death and resurrection, we offer you the bread and the cup" (paraphrase).
  • Epiclesis: prayer that the Holy Spirit be sent on the offering of the Church and on those who receive.
  • Later versions of this prayer became the basis of the Catholic Eucharistic Prayer II (1969) and prayers in many Anglican and Protestant books.
  • Development: a fixed shape of prayer led by the bishop; the language of offering.
Mission 4

Tertullian and Cyprian

  • Tertullian (c. 155–c. 220):
  • Communion received before dawn, from the presider's hand only; anxious care that no crumb or drop falls (On the Crown 3).
  • Some took consecrated bread home to receive during the week (To his Wife 2.5).
  • Against Marcion: "This is my body" means (paraphrase) "the figure of my body" (Against Marcion 4.40); and a figure needs a real body: he argues for Christ's real flesh. Later read both ways in Catholic and Protestant debate.
  • Cyprian (bishop 248–258), Letter 63 (to Caecilius):
  • Some churches used water only (to avoid wine's smell at dawn, or ascetic reasons). Cyprian: the cup must be wine mixed with water, as Christ did.
  • Wine = Christ's blood; water = the people: mixed, Christ and his people are united.
  • The priest acts in Christ's place and offers a true sacrifice to God the Father, imitating what Christ did.
  • Development: clear sacrifice and priesthood language; strict rules on materials.

Mission 4

How doctrine and practice developed

  • Setting: from a meal in homes (1 Corinthians 11) → thanksgiving within a meal (Didache) → a Sunday rite with readings, separate from any meal (Justin) → an early-morning service (Tertullian, Cyprian). The fellowship meal (agape) continued separately for a time.
  • Leader: prophets may "give thanks as they wish" (Didache 10) → the president (Justin) → the bishop with set prayer (Hippolytus) → the priest offering in Christ's place (Cyprian).
  • Presence: "my body" (NT) → "not common bread" but Christ's flesh and blood (Justin) → "figure" with real flesh behind it (Tertullian).
  • Sacrifice: "pure offering" (Didache 14) → "we offer" (Hippolytus) → a true sacrifice (Cyprian).
  • Access: baptised only; confession and reconciliation first.
  • Gregory Dix (1901–1952), The Shape of the Liturgy (1945): early rites kept a four-action shape: Jesus took, blessed (gave thanks), broke, gave.
Mission 4Evaluate

"The early sources show that the Eucharist was a sacrifice, not a memorial."

  • For: "sacrifice" (Didache 14), "offer" (Hippolytus), true sacrifice by the priest (Cyprian); Malachi 1:11 applied to the Eucharist.
  • For: Justin's realism: the food is Christ's flesh and blood.
  • Against: the NT stresses memory, proclamation and sharing ("in memory of me"; "you proclaim the Lord's death"); Hebrews says Christ's sacrifice was "once for all" (Hebrews 10:10).
  • Against: early "sacrifice" may mean thanksgiving and prayer (spiritual sacrifice), not repeating Calvary; Tertullian's "figure" language.
  • Another view: sacrifice and memorial need not compete: anamnesis makes the one sacrifice present, not repeated.
  • Judge it by: Do we read later doctrine back into early texts? Is the earliest source the most authoritative, or the developing tradition?
Mission 4

Understandings of the Eucharist today

  • Catholic: transubstantiation (Fourth Lateran Council 1215; Council of Trent 1551): the substance of bread and wine becomes Christ's body and blood; the Mass makes present the one sacrifice of Calvary.
  • Orthodox: a real change by the Holy Spirit (stress on the epiclesis); treated as mystery, without a philosophical definition.
  • Lutheran: real presence: Christ truly present "in, with and under" bread and wine (sacramental union).
  • Reformed (Calvin): spiritual real presence: believers truly feed on Christ by the Spirit through faith.
  • Anglican: a range, from Catholic-like to Reformed views.
  • Memorialist (Zwingli; many Baptists and evangelicals): a memorial and act of obedience; bread and wine remain symbols.
  • Quakers and Salvation Army: no outward sacrament; every meal and life can be communion with Christ.
Mission 4Evaluate

Can Christians share one understanding of the Eucharist?

  • For: all draw on the same NT words; ecumenical texts such as Baptism, Eucharist and Ministry (World Council of Churches, Lima, 1982) found wide agreement on thanksgiving, memorial (anamnesis), Spirit, communion and the Kingdom.
  • For: most traditions agree it is communion with Christ and with one another (1 Corinthians 10:17).
  • Against: deep divisions remain on presence and sacrifice, and on who may preside; Catholics and Orthodox do not generally share communion with Protestants.
  • Against: for those who see it as Christ's real body, shared communion without shared faith would contradict its meaning.
  • Judge it by: Is the Eucharist a sign of unity already achieved or a means to unity? How much diversity existed in the early sources themselves?
Mission check

?A Christian student says: "We read the Gospel, the leader gives thanks as best he can, everyone says Amen, the deacons take bread and wine to the housebound, and we collect money for the poor." Which early source describes this pattern most fully?

From Paul's "do this in memory of me" to Cyprian's priest offering in Christ's place, the core words stayed while setting, leadership and doctrine developed.

Mission 5: Should a baby be baptised before it can choose, or should baptism wait until a person can say yes?

Make a guess: Should a baby be baptised before it can choose, or should baptism wait until a person can say yes?

Mission 5

What is baptism for?

  • Baptism: the rite of entry into the Church, using water, "in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit" (Matthew 28:19).
  • Almost all Christians baptise. They disagree about who and what it does.
  • Sacrament view (Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican, Lutheran): God acts in baptism; it gives grace, washes away sin and brings new birth.
  • Ordinance view (Baptists, many evangelicals and Pentecostals): an act of obedience and public witness to a faith already received.
  • If baptism gives grace, babies need it. If it declares faith, it should wait for faith.
  • Baptism is held to be once only. Catholics recognise any baptism with water in the name of the Trinity; most Baptist churches baptise believers who were baptised as infants, holding that infant baptism was not true baptism.
Mission 5Read the source

Baptism in the New Testament

> "Repent and be baptised, every one of you, in the name of Jesus Christ for the forgiveness of sins, and you will receive the gift of the Holy Spirit. For the promise is to you and to your children, and to all who are far off" (Acts 2:38–39, WEBBE)

> "He took them the same hour of the night and washed their stripes, and was immediately baptised, he and all his household." (Acts 16:33, WEBBE)

> "We were buried therefore with him through baptism into death, that just as Christ was raised from the dead through the glory of the Father, so we also might walk in newness of life." (Romans 6:4, WEBBE)

Q1 Which words in Acts 2:38–39 support infant baptism, and which support believer's baptism?

Show answer

"To your children" for infants; "Repent" first, for believers.

Q2 Why might both sides cite Acts 16:33?

Show answer

A whole household is baptised, which may have included children; but no child is mentioned.

  • No New Testament text clearly records or forbids the baptism of an infant.
Mission 5

Augustine: baptism and salvation

  • Augustine of Hippo (354–430), especially On the Merits and Forgiveness of Sins, and on the Baptism of Infants (412), against the Pelagians.
  • Original sin: every human inherits Adam's guilt and a damaged nature, before any choice of their own.
  • So infants need forgiveness: baptism washes away the guilt of original sin.
  • Role in salvation: without baptism an infant cannot enter the Kingdom (John 3:5). He held that unbaptised infants are condemned, though to the mildest punishment (Enchiridion 93).
  • The Church's practice proves it: the Church already baptised infants "for the forgiveness of sins"; this only makes sense if infants have sin.
  • Parents and Church: the infant believes through the faith of the Church, voiced by those who bring the child (Letter 98).

Mission 5

What the early Church did

  • Didache 7 (c. 100): baptise in the name of the Trinity, in running water if possible, otherwise pour water three times; fast first. No mention of infants.
  • Justin Martyr, First Apology 61 (c. 155): those who are persuaded and believe are baptised; baptism is "illumination".
  • Hippolytus (attrib.), Apostolic Tradition 21 (early 3rd century): baptise the little ones first; if they cannot speak, parents or relatives answer for them.
  • Tertullian, On Baptism 18 (c. 200): delay is better, especially for little children; let them come when they can know Christ.
  • Cyprian of Carthage, Letter 64 to Fidus (c. 253): an African council rules that infants need not wait until the eighth day; baptise them at once.
  • So infant baptism was practised by about 200, and also debated.
  • Full study of these sources: the early-Church missions.
Mission 5Evaluate

Should infants be baptised?

  • For: grace comes first; God's love does not wait for our choice (sacramental view).
  • For: covenant continuity with circumcision (Zwingli: as Jewish boys received circumcision as the covenant sign, Christian children receive baptism); household baptisms (Acts 16:15, 33); "to your children" (Acts 2:39).
  • For: parents rightly bring children into the family's faith, as into its language and home.
  • Against: no clear New Testament example; repentance and faith come first (Acts 2:38, 41).
  • Against: consent and the example of Christ (Karl Barth: Jesus came to baptism as an adult, and baptism needs the person's own yes); faith cannot be professed by proxy.
  • Against: risks nominal Christianity: baptised but never believing.
  • Is either just a symbolic act? Catholics: no, God really acts. Many Baptists: an act of obedience, though not merely symbolic, since it is commanded by Christ and marks a real commitment.
  • Judgement criteria: What does baptism do? How should silence in the New Testament be weighed? Whose faith counts: the person's or the Church's?

Mission 6

Mission 6: When Christians share bread and wine, what do they believe they are really receiving?

Make a guess: When Christians share bread and wine, what do they believe they are really receiving?

Mission 6

One meal, many names

  • Eucharist (Greek: "thanksgiving"), Mass (Catholic), Divine Liturgy (Orthodox), Holy Communion (Anglican, many Protestants), the Lord's Supper or Breaking of Bread (Baptists, many evangelicals).
  • All look back to Jesus' Last Supper with his disciples, the night before he died.
  • Sacrament: "an outward and visible sign of an inward and spiritual grace" (Book of Common Prayer, Catechism, 1662).
  • Grace: God's free, undeserved love and help.
  • Catholics and Orthodox count seven sacraments; most Protestants two (baptism and the Eucharist); Baptists often say ordinance, not sacrament.
  • The big question: how, if at all, is Christ present in the bread and wine?
Mission 6Read the source

"This is my body"

> "He took bread, and when he had given thanks, he broke and gave it to them, saying, "This is my body which is given for you. Do this in memory of me." Likewise, he took the cup after supper, saying, "This cup is the new covenant in my blood, which is poured out for you."" (Luke 22:19–20, WEBBE)

> "For my flesh is food indeed, and my blood is drink indeed." (John 6:55, WEBBE)

  • Two key phrases: "This is my body" and "in memory of me" (Greek anamnesis, "remembrance"; Catholic and Orthodox teaching takes this as a remembrance that makes the saving event present, memorialists as a recalling of it).

Q1 Which phrase do Catholics stress most, and which do Baptists stress most?

Show answer

Catholics: "This is my body"; Baptists: "Do this in memory of me".

Q2 How does John 6:55 support a literal reading?

Show answer

It calls Jesus' flesh real food and his blood real drink.

Mission 6

Transubstantiation

  • Catholic teaching: at the consecration, the whole substance of the bread and wine is changed into the substance of Christ's body and blood.
  • The accidents (appearance, taste, texture, chemistry) remain those of bread and wine.
  • Terms from Aristotle: substance = what a thing is; accidents = how it appears to the senses.
  • Defined at the Fourth Lateran Council (1215); confirmed at the Council of Trent (1551) against the Reformers.
  • Christ is wholly present in each part, even a crumb; so the consecrated hosts are kept in the tabernacle and adored.
  • This is the Catholic meaning of Real Presence: Christ truly, really and substantially present.
Mission 6Read the source

Aquinas on the change

> "the whole substance of the bread is changed into the whole substance of Christ's body, and the whole substance of the wine into the whole substance of Christ's blood. Hence this is not a formal, but a substantial conversion; nor is it a kind of natural movement: but, with a name of its own, it can be called "transubstantiation."" (Thomas Aquinas, Summa Theologica III, Q. 75, Art. 4, English Dominican trans.)

  • In natural change, one form gives way to another in the same underlying matter (wood becomes ash). Here the whole substance changes, and the appearance remains.
  • Only God, the source of all being, can do this (Aquinas' argument in the same article).

Q1 Why does Aquinas say the change is not "a kind of natural movement"?

Show answer

No natural process changes a whole substance while leaving its appearance.

Q2 Why does the bread still taste like bread?

Show answer

The accidents remain; only the substance has changed.

Mission 6

Memorialism

  • Huldrych Zwingli (1484–1531): the Supper is a memorial of Christ's death and a pledge of the community's faith.
  • "This is my body" means "This signifies my body", as Jesus says "I am the door" (John 10:9).
  • Christ's risen body is at the right hand of God; it cannot be in many places at once.
  • "It is the spirit who gives life. The flesh profits nothing." (John 6:63, WEBBE)
  • Marburg Colloquy (1529): Luther and Zwingli agreed on 14 of 15 articles but split on this one.
  • Baptists today: the Lord's Supper is an ordinance and memorial; Christ is present spiritually among his people, not in the elements. Many stress it is not "merely" a memorial: it is a real meeting with the risen Lord in faith.

Mission 6Evaluate

Is there any common ground?

  • Claim: There is real common ground in contemporary understandings of the Eucharist.
  • For: all hold that Jesus commanded it, that it proclaims his death (1 Corinthians 11:26) and unites believers (1 Corinthians 10:17).
  • For: ecumenical dialogue: Baptism, Eucharist and Ministry (World Council of Churches, Lima, 1982) found agreement on thanksgiving, anamnesis, the Spirit's role and fellowship.
  • For: many memorialists speak of a real spiritual presence; many Catholics stress meaning and relationship.
  • Against: the core question, whether the bread becomes Christ's body, still divides; this affects worship (adoration) and who may share communion.
  • Against: Catholics and Orthodox do not, in general, share communion with Protestants, a sign that differences are not merely verbal.
  • Judgement criteria: Is agreement on practice enough without agreement on presence? Are the theories different answers or different languages?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Religious factors (message, Jewish roots, care, morals, zeal) and political factors (peace, roads, language, cities) explain growth; Gibbon and Stark weigh them differently.
  • Polycarp (c. 155–156, Smyrna), Justin (c. 165, Rome) and Blandina (177, Lyons) show martyrdom's impact across classes.
  • Martyrdom (witness to death) matters in many religions; its importance and risks are debated.

Mission 2

  • Causes: refusal to sacrifice (fear for the pax deorum), rumours, social separation, scapegoating; trials were local before 250.
  • Nero (Rome, 64), Domitian (uncertain), Trajan (Pliny's letter: not sought out, punished if obstinate), Marcus Aurelius (mobs, Lyons 177).
  • Decius (250, universal sacrifice, libelli) and Diocletian (303–311, churches, scriptures, clergy) made it empire-wide; effects included martyrs, lapsed and schism.

Mission 3

  • NT roots: John's baptism, Jesus' baptism, Matthew 28:19, Acts 2:38; Paul: dying and rising with Christ (Romans 6:3–4).
  • Development: Didache (triple name, running water, pouring, fasting); Justin (rebirth, illumination); Hippolytus (catechumenate, exorcism, triple questions, anointing); Tertullian (treatise, delay); Cyprian (infants, one Church, rebaptism).
  • Infant vs believer's baptism is debated then (Jeremias, Aland) and now; initiation remains central to belonging for most Christians.

Mission 4

  • NT origins: the Last Supper (1 Corinthians 11:23–26 earliest; Mark–Matthew and Luke), Passover and covenant setting; breaking of bread in Acts; John 6.
  • Development: Didache (thanksgiving, unity, sacrifice, baptised only); Justin (Sunday rite, not common bread); Hippolytus (bishop's prayer, offering, epiclesis); Tertullian (care, "figure"); Cyprian (mixed cup, priest, true sacrifice).
  • Today: transubstantiation, Orthodox change, Lutheran and Reformed presence, memorial, no outward sacrament; agreement and division both remain.

Mission 5

  • Infant baptism (Catholic): a sacrament of grace that washes away original sin; parents and godparents speak for the child (Augustine).
  • Believer's baptism (Baptist): full immersion after a personal profession of faith; an ordinance and public witness.

Mission 6

  • Transubstantiation (Catholic): the substance becomes Christ's body and blood; the accidents remain (Lateran IV, Aquinas, Trent).
  • Memorialism (Zwingli, most Baptists): "Do this in memory of me"; Christ present spiritually among believers, not in the bread.
