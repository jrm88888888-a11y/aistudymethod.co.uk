Mission 1: When does a developing human start to count as someone, and would that settle the abortion debate?

Make a guess: When does a developing human start to count as someone, and would that settle the abortion debate?

Mission 1

Stages and circumstances

  • Abortion: the deliberate ending of a pregnancy so that the embryo or fetus dies. (A miscarriage is a spontaneous loss.)
  • Conception (fertilisation): a zygote with its own genetic code. Implantation in the womb about 6–10 days later.
  • About 14 days: the primitive streak appears; twinning is no longer possible.
  • Embryo up to 8 weeks after fertilisation (about 10 weeks of pregnancy, as doctors count from the last period); then fetus. Brain and nervous system develop through pregnancy.
  • Viability: the point at which a fetus could survive outside the womb with medical help, now around 22–24 weeks of pregnancy.
Mission 1

When does moral status begin?

  • Moral status: having interests or rights that others must respect. Three main answers:
  • From conception: a new human life begins; development after that is continuous, with no clear later line. Its potential to become a person gives it full status now.
  • Gradualist: status grows with development. Key markers: 14 days, sentience (the capacity to feel), viability.
  • At birth (or later): only then is there an independent being with experiences of its own; before that, the woman's interests decide.
  • Potentiality debated: critics say a potential person is not yet an actual one (an acorn is not an oak). Defenders reply that the embryo is an actual human organism developing its own potential.
  • Twinning objection: before 14 days one embryo can become two, so is it yet "an individual"?

Mission 1Read the source

Life before birth

> "For you formed my inmost being. You knit me together in my mother's womb." (Psalm 139:13, WEBBE)

> "Before I formed you in the womb, I knew you. Before you were born, I sanctified you." (Jeremiah 1:5, WEBBE)

  • Christians who hold the sanctity of life from conception read these as showing that God knows and forms each person before birth.
  • The Didache (late 1st or early 2nd century), an early Christian manual, forbids killing a child by abortion (Didache 2:2, paraphrase).
  • Other Christians note that the psalm is poetry about God's care, not a statement of when personhood begins; and that Exodus 21:22–25 treats causing a miscarriage differently from killing a person, on some readings.

Show answer

Q2 Why do some Christians say these verses do not settle the abortion question?

Show answer

They are poetic or prophetic texts about God's care, not definitions of moral status.

Mission 1

The personhood debate

  • Person: a being with full moral status. Is every human a person, or only those with certain capacities?
  • Mary Anne Warren (1946–2010), "On the Moral and Legal Status of Abortion" (The Monist, 1973): five traits central to personhood: consciousness (especially of pain), reasoning, self-motivated activity, communication, self-awareness. A fetus has at most a rudimentary form of the first (it may feel pain late in pregnancy), so Warren argues it is not a person.
  • Critic: the same test seems to exclude newborns. Warren argued infanticide is still wrong for other reasons.
  • Peter Singer: no fetus is a person; what may matter is sentience, which gives a being interests (fuller treatment in Mission 3).
  • Religious view: personhood is not earned by capacities; it comes with being a human being created by God (the image of God).
Mission 1

The rights of the woman

  • The debate is not only about the fetus. The woman has a right to life, health, and bodily autonomy.
  • Judith Jarvis Thomson (1929–2020), "A Defense of Abortion" (Philosophy & Public Affairs, 1971): grant, for argument's sake, that the fetus is a person.
  • Her violinist example (paraphrase): you wake up connected to a famous violinist who needs your kidneys for nine months to live. You may unplug, though he dies: his right to life does not give him a right to use your body.
  • So abortion may be permissible even if the fetus is a person.
  • Critics: in most pregnancies the woman is not kidnapped (responsibility); abortion kills, while unplugging only stops saving; the fetus is the woman's own child.
  • Rights of the unborn: those who hold the conception view say the right not to be killed outweighs other rights.

Mission 1Diversity

Religious views

  • Catholic Church: human life is to be protected absolutely from conception; direct abortion is gravely wrong (Catechism 2270–2271; Evangelium Vitae, 1995, §62). Indirect cases.
  • Orthodox Churches and many evangelical Christians also hold that abortion is wrong from conception, with life-saving exceptions.
  • Church of England: combines strong opposition to abortion with recognition that, in strictly limited conditions (e.g. a threat to the mother's life or health), it may be morally preferable to any alternative (General Synod statements since the 1980s).
  • Methodist Church in Britain: abortion is not acceptable as a form of contraception, but may be the lesser evil in some circumstances.
  • Judaism: the Mishnah (Ohalot 7:6) puts the mother's life first in a dangerous birth, until most of the child has emerged. Orthodox authorities allow abortion mainly for serious threats to the mother; Reform Judaism allows more.
  • Islam: many scholars teach the soul is breathed in at 120 days (hadith in al-Bukhari and Muslim). After that, abortion is forbidden except to save the mother's life; before, schools differ.
Mission 1

Secular views

  • Humanists UK: supports a woman's right to choose, weighing the welfare of the woman and the stage of development; does not treat the early embryo as a person.
  • Utilitarian views weigh the interests of all affected; Singer gives weight to sentience, so later abortion is more serious than early.
  • Secular opposition exists too: Don Marquis, "Why Abortion Is Immoral" (Journal of Philosophy, 1989): killing is wrong because it takes away a future like ours; a fetus has such a future, so abortion is seriously wrong (paraphrase).
  • Disability-rights critics (religious and secular): abortion for fetal anomaly can send the message that disabled lives are worth less.
  • The religious/secular split is again not tidy.
Mission 1Evaluate

Would settling personhood settle abortion?

  • Claim: If we knew when a person begins, we would know when abortion is wrong.
  • For: Most agree it is wrong to kill an innocent person; status is the key premise of the debate.
  • For: Sanctity-of-life arguments and Warren's argument both turn on what the fetus is.
  • Against: Thomson: even if the fetus is a person, the woman's bodily rights may permit abortion.
  • Against: Marquis: wrongness rests on the loss of a future, not on present personhood.
  • Against: If personhood is uncertain, the question becomes how to act under uncertainty (some argue: give the benefit of the doubt).
  • Judgement criteria: Are status and rights separable? Can a line be drawn without being arbitrary? How much weight does uncertainty carry?

Mission 2: If we can choose or change an embryo's genes, where does curing disease end and designing children begin?

Make a guess: If we can choose or change an embryo's genes, where does curing disease end and designing children begin?

Mission 2

Embryo research and the 14-day rule

  • Embryo research: studying human embryos (usually spare IVF embryos, sometimes made for research) to understand infertility, miscarriage, genetic disease and early development.
  • UK law (HFE Act 1990): research is allowed only under an HFEA licence, and never beyond 14 days after fertilisation, or once the primitive streak appears.
  • Why 14 days? After it, twinning is no longer possible and the nervous system begins to form (Warnock Report, 1984).
  • Embryos used in research may never be placed in a woman.
  • Laws differ: e.g. Germany's Embryo Protection Act (1990) forbids destructive embryo research.
  • Status question: full status, special status, or none?
Mission 2

Stem cells and cord blood

  • Stem cells can divide and become specialised cells. Hope: repairing damaged tissue (e.g. in Parkinson's disease, spinal injury, diabetes).
  • Embryonic stem cells: taken from a 5–6-day embryo (blastocyst); pluripotent (can become almost any cell type). Taking them destroys the embryo. First human lines: 1998.
  • Adult stem cells (e.g. bone marrow): more limited, but used in treatment for decades; no embryo destroyed.
  • Induced pluripotent stem cells (iPS cells): adult cells reprogrammed to an embryonic-like state (Shinya Yamanaka, 2006; Nobel Prize 2012).
  • Cord blood: blood from the umbilical cord and placenta after birth, rich in blood-forming stem cells; used to treat leukaemia and other blood disorders; can be donated to public banks or stored privately.
  • Catholic teaching supports research with adult, iPS and cord-blood stem cells, and opposes embryonic stem-cell research (Dignitas Personae, 2008).

Mission 2

PGD and saviour siblings

  • PGD (pre-implantation genetic diagnosis): one or a few cells are taken from IVF embryos and tested for a serious genetic condition (e.g. cystic fibrosis, Huntington's disease); unaffected embryos are transferred.
  • In the UK the HFEA decides which conditions may be tested for. Sex selection for non-medical reasons is illegal.
  • Saviour siblings: embryos are tested to find one whose tissue matches a sick older child, so cord blood or bone marrow can help them. The House of Lords upheld the HFEA's power to allow this (Quintavalle, 2005, the Hashmi family case).
  • For: prevents serious suffering; parents can have a healthy child.
  • Against: affected embryos are discarded; it may express a judgement that disabled lives are worth less; a saviour sibling might be valued as a means (Kant's objection).
  • Catholic teaching rejects PGD because it involves selecting and discarding embryos (Dignitas Personae §22).
Mission 2

Cloning

  • Cloning by somatic cell nuclear transfer: the nucleus of a body cell is put into an egg whose own nucleus has been removed; the egg develops as an embryo with the donor's DNA.
  • Dolly the sheep (born 1996, Roslin Institute, Scotland): the first mammal cloned from an adult cell.
  • Reproductive cloning (to make a born human clone) is illegal in the UK and most countries.
  • Therapeutic (research) cloning: making a cloned embryo to obtain matched stem cells; possible under HFEA licence; the embryo is destroyed.
  • Objections: risks of abnormality; a clone's sense of identity and an "open future"; making children to a pattern; the embryo's status; "playing God".
  • Replies: a clone would be a separate person, like a late-born identical twin; therapeutic cloning could cure disease.

Mission 2

'Designer' babies

  • 'Designer' baby: a child whose genes are chosen or changed for non-medical traits (e.g. height, intelligence) rather than to avoid disease.
  • Genome editing (e.g. CRISPR) can change genes in embryos. In 2018 He Jiankui announced the birth of twin girls whose genes he had edited; he was widely condemned and imprisoned in China (2019).
  • Editing embryos for reproduction is illegal in the UK.
  • Julian Savulescu, "Procreative Beneficence" (Bioethics, 2001): parents should select, of the possible children, the one expected to have the best life (paraphrase).
  • Michael Sandel, The Case Against Perfection (2007): the deeper problem is not safety but the drive to mastery; it erodes seeing children as gifts and humility before what we are given (paraphrase).
  • Jürgen Habermas, The Future of Human Nature (2003): a designed person may not be able to see themselves as the sole author of their own life (paraphrase).
Mission 2

The theories applied

  • Natural law: the embryo is a human life from conception, so destroying it for research breaks the precept to preserve life, however good the aim; cloning separates reproduction from the marital act. Treating disease is a real good; enhancement may be an apparent good.
  • Situation ethics: Fletcher, The Ethics of Genetic Control (1974), welcomed genetic control and laboratory reproduction as deliberate, responsible, so more human, than chance (paraphrase). Agape may favour research that cures disease; for Fletcher an early embryo is not a person.
  • Virtue ethics: what would a compassionate, wise and humble person do? Compassion supports curing disease; Sandel's humility warns against design; practical wisdom asks where treatment ends.
  • Apply all three to each issue: embryo research, cloning, 'designer' babies.
Mission 2Evaluate

Should embryos be used to cure disease?

  • Claim: It is right to destroy early human embryos in research that may cure serious disease.
  • For: An embryo before 14 days has no nervous system and cannot suffer; the potential relief of suffering is great.
  • For: Spare IVF embryos will perish anyway; using them shows more respect than wasting them.
  • Against: If the embryo has the status of a person (Catholic teaching), no benefit to others justifies killing it.
  • Against: Alternatives (adult, iPS, cord-blood stem cells) may make it unnecessary; and treating embryos as material may weaken respect for all human life.
  • Judgement criteria: What is the embryo's status? Are there alternatives? Does the 14-day rule express respect, or is it an arbitrary line?
  • AO2 tip: apply each theory to the issue, then weigh which response is most convincing, with a reason.

Mission 3: Can ending a pregnancy ever be a side-effect rather than a choice, and what should the law do about it?

Make a guess: Can ending a pregnancy ever be a side-effect rather than a choice, and what should the law do about it?

Mission 3

Natural law applied

  • Primary precepts: preservation of life and reproduction. Secondary precept: never directly kill the innocent.
  • Catholic natural law applies this from conception, so direct abortion (abortion as an end or a means) is always gravely wrong (Evangelium Vitae, 1995, §62).
  • Even a good motive (helping a frightened woman) cannot make a direct killing good.
  • Aquinas himself (following Aristotle) held that the rational soul arrives some weeks after conception; the Church today protects life from conception, given uncertainty and the continuity of development.
Mission 3

Therapeutic abortion and double effect

  • Natural law uses double effect: an act with a good and a bad effect may be done if the act is good in itself, the bad effect is foreseen but not intended, it is not the means to the good, and there is a proportionate reason.
  • Cancerous womb: removing the womb (hysterectomy) treats the mother's disease; the fetus' death is foreseen, not intended. Permitted.
  • Ectopic pregnancy: removing the damaged fallopian tube (salpingectomy) is widely accepted by Catholic moralists; some other methods are debated among them.
  • Not permitted: directly ending the fetus' life to save the mother (e.g. killing it to ease a dangerous birth): here death is the means.
  • Critics: the difference can seem artificial when the fetus dies either way; proportionalists would weigh the outcomes directly.

Mission 3

Situation ethics applied

  • Agape: the most loving act for all the people involved, including the woman, the fetus, and others affected.
  • Fletcher was a strong supporter of legal abortion. In Situation Ethics (1966) he discusses a patient in a psychiatric hospital pregnant after rape, and argues love would permit abortion (paraphrase).
  • Personalism: the woman's life and wellbeing come before a rule.
  • Pragmatism and relativism: the answer depends on the case: rape, risk to health, or a child who could not be cared for may tip the balance.
  • Love might also argue against abortion in some situations; situation ethics does not make every abortion right.
  • Critics: who counts the fetus' interests in the calculation? A choice based on love in one case may be unsafe as a social rule.
Mission 3

Virtue ethics and utilitarianism

  • Rosalind Hursthouse (b. 1943), "Virtue Theory and Abortion" (Philosophy & Public Affairs, 1991): virtue ethics need not settle the fetus' status or the woman's rights first.
  • Her question: in having (or not having) an abortion in these circumstances, would she be acting virtuously or viciously? Callous or light-minded, or serious and responsible?
  • Ending a human life is a serious matter, so regret or grief can be fitting even when abortion is the right choice (paraphrase). Parenthood is part of flourishing for many.

Mission 3

The slippery slope

  • Argument: once abortion is allowed for serious reasons, it will come to be allowed for lesser ones; or the value placed on all vulnerable life will fall.
  • Examples given: abortion for less serious fetal conditions; sex-selective abortion; Singer's views on newborns, cited as the "next step".
  • Replies: the law can and does draw lines (time limits, grounds); allowing one practice does not force allowing another; the evidence is disputed.
  • The argument is mostly about law and society, not about whether a single abortion is right.
Mission 3

The law and the state

  • Great Britain: abortion remains a crime (in England and Wales under the Offences Against the Person Act 1861; in Scotland under common law), except as allowed by the Abortion Act 1967: two doctors must agree that a legal ground applies.
  • Crime and Policing Act 2026: a woman acting in relation to her own pregnancy is removed from the criminal law in England and Wales; the rules for doctors and others are unchanged.
  • Since 1990 (Human Fertilisation and Embryology Act): a 24-week limit for most grounds; no limit where there is risk to the woman's life, grave permanent injury, or a substantial risk of serious fetal disability.
  • Conscientious objection: medical staff may refuse to take part directly, except to save life (Abortion Act 1967, s. 4).
  • Elsewhere: USA: Roe v Wade (1973) overturned by Dobbs (2022), returning the question to the states. Ireland: the 2018 referendum repealed the constitutional ban.
  • State responsibilities: protect life, protect women's health, respect conscience: states weigh these differently.
Mission 3Evaluate

How far should law follow natural law?

  • Claim: Human law on abortion should be based on natural law's protection of life from conception.
  • For: Aquinas: human law derives its authority from natural law; protecting the innocent is a core purpose of law.
  • For: If the unborn are human beings, a law that permits killing them is unjust, whatever most people believe.
  • Against: Aquinas himself held that human law should not forbid every vice, only those most harmful to others, which most can avoid (ST I-II q. 96 a. 2).
  • Against: In a plural society, law cannot rest on a premise (life is sacred from conception) that many reject; banning abortion can push it into unsafe practice.
  • Judgement criteria: Is the issue one of protecting others, or of private morality? Can law be just without consensus? What happens in practice when laws change?

Mission 3Worked answer

Applying a theory

Issue: "Natural law is the most effective approach to abortion."

  • Point: Natural law gives a clear and consistent rule: innocent life is protected from conception, so direct abortion is always wrong.
  • Source: Primary precept of preservation of life; Evangelium Vitae §62.
  • Develop: Double effect keeps it humane in the hardest cases: the cancerous womb and ectopic pregnancy.
  • Counter: Hursthouse and Fletcher: the woman's situation and character matter; a rule may fail the case of rape or serious risk to health.
  • Mini-judgement: Its clarity is a strength only if its premise about the embryo's status is accepted; the essay must test that premise, not just state it.
  • AO2 tip: weigh the strongest form of each side, then reach a reasoned judgement on the issue as set.

Make a guess: If someone in great pain asks to die, whose life is it to end: theirs, a doctor's, or God's?

Mission 4

What the words mean

  • Euthanasia (Greek eu "good" + thanatos "death"): deliberately ending a person's life, or allowing it to end, for that person's sake.
  • Active euthanasia: someone does something that causes death (e.g. a doctor gives a lethal injection).
  • Passive euthanasia: treatment that would keep the person alive is withheld or withdrawn, so that they die.
  • Assisted suicide: the person takes the final act themselves, with means supplied by someone else. Physician-assisted suicide: the helper is a doctor.
  • Assisted dying: the term used in many current laws and bills for physician-assisted suicide for terminally ill adults.
  • Not euthanasia: stopping treatment that no longer helps, where the aim is not death.
Mission 4

Whose choice? Three types

  • Voluntary: the life is ended at the person's request or with their consent. Typical case: an incurable or terminal illness.
  • Non-voluntary: the person cannot consent (e.g. a newborn, or someone in a persistent vegetative state, PVS). Someone representing their interests decides.
  • Involuntary: the person could consent but is not asked, or refuses. Treated as murder in every legal system; almost no one defends it.
  • PVS: the body is kept alive (breathing, sleep–wake cycles) but there are no signs of awareness, for months or years.
  • The two distinctions combine: e.g. voluntary active (lethal injection on request), non-voluntary passive (feeding withdrawn in PVS).
Mission 4Read the source

Sanctity of life

  • Sanctity of life: human life is sacred, holy, because it is given by God and bears God's image. Its religious origin is in the Hebrew Bible.

> "God created man in his own image. In God's image he created him; male and female he created them." (Genesis 1:27, WEBBE)

> "You shall not murder." (Exodus 20:13, WEBBE)

> "The LORD gave, and the LORD has taken away. Blessed be the LORD's name." (Job 1:21, WEBBE)

Mission 4

Sanctity of life in Christian teaching

  • Strong sanctity of life: innocent human life must never be deliberately ended, whatever its quality. Its value does not depend on health, ability or awareness.
  • Christians also cite the body as a "temple of the Holy Spirit" (1 Corinthians 6:19) and Jesus' care for the sick.
  • Catholic teaching today: Catechism of the Catholic Church 2276–2279; John Paul II, Evangelium Vitae (1995), §65.
  • Weak sanctity of life (held by some Christians): life is precious and God-given, but not of absolute value; love may sometimes allow death to come or be hastened.
  • Sanctity of life is not vitalism (keeping alive at all costs): most sanctity views allow burdensome treatment to stop.

Mission 4

Quality of life

  • Quality of life: a life has value because of what it contains or makes possible, not just because it is human.
  • Used in medicine to weigh treatments (e.g. the QALY, a "quality-adjusted life year", used in UK health planning).
  • Jonathan Glover (b. 1941), Causing Death and Saving Life (1977): what matters is not being alive but having a life worth living, chiefly conscious life.
  • Peter Singer (b. 1946), Rethinking Life and Death (1994): the traditional sanctity-of-life ethic is collapsing; the worth of a human life varies (paraphrase).
  • Critics: who judges a life "not worth living"? Disability-rights campaigners warn the idea devalues disabled lives.
  • Some religious believers also use quality-of-life reasoning, alongside sanctity, when deciding about treatment.

Mission 4

Autonomy and rights

  • Autonomy (Greek "self-law"): the right of a competent person to make decisions about their own life and body.
  • Right to die arguments: if I have a right to life, I may waive it; refusing treatment is already lawful, so why not ask for help to die?
  • Critics: Mill himself said one may not sell oneself into slavery, since that ends future freedom; does a choice to die do the same?
  • Is a request to die ever fully free? Pressure from depression, pain, cost or a sense of being a burden.
  • Christian reply: life is held in trust (stewardship), not owned; freedom is for living well with others.
Mission 4

The law: a snapshot

  • England and Wales: Suicide Act 1961: suicide is no longer a crime, but encouraging or assisting suicide is (up to 14 years). Active euthanasia is murder or manslaughter.
  • DPP policy (2010, after the Purdy case, 2009): lists factors that make prosecution for assisting suicide more or less likely, such as a wholly compassionate motive.
  • Airedale NHS Trust v Bland (House of Lords, 1993): feeding could be withdrawn from Tony Bland, in PVS after the Hillsborough disaster; the court treated it as withdrawing treatment no longer in his interests, not as killing.
  • Elsewhere: Netherlands and Belgium (both 2002): euthanasia on request under strict conditions. Oregon (1997): assisted suicide for terminally ill adults. Canada (2016): medical assistance in dying. Switzerland: assisting suicide is not a crime unless done from selfish motives.
  • Recent bills for terminally ill adults: the Scottish Parliament rejected one (March 2026); one for England and Wales passed the Commons (2025) but ran out of time in the Lords (April 2026).
Mission 4

Palliative care

  • Palliative care: care that relieves pain and distress (physical, emotional, spiritual) when a cure is no longer possible. It neither hastens nor postpones death.
  • Cicely Saunders (1918–2005), a Christian nurse and doctor, founded St Christopher's Hospice, London (1967), the first modern hospice. Her idea of "total pain": suffering has physical, emotional, social and spiritual sides.
  • Many opponents of euthanasia argue: good palliative care removes most reasons to ask for it.
  • Supporters reply: some suffering cannot be relieved, and some people value control over the manner and time of death.
Mission 4Diversity

Religious and secular views

  • Catholic and Orthodox Christians: euthanasia and assisted suicide are always wrong; refusing disproportionate treatment and pain relief that may shorten life are allowed.
  • Church of England: has opposed changing the law, stressing palliative care and protection of the vulnerable. But individual Anglicans differ: e.g. Desmond Tutu (1931–2021) publicly supported assisted dying (2014).
  • Liberal Christian voices: e.g. Hans Küng (1928–2021), a dissenting Catholic theologian, argued for a responsible choice to die in A Dignified Dying (1995, with Walter Jens).
  • Judaism: actively hastening death is forbidden, but most authorities allow removing what only prolongs dying. Islam: life belongs to God; most scholars forbid euthanasia, citing e.g. Qur'an 4:29 ("Nor kill (or destroy) yourselves", Yusuf Ali).
  • Secular supporters: Humanists UK and Dignity in Dying back a change in the law, on grounds of autonomy and compassion.
  • Secular opponents: some disability-rights campaigners (e.g. Not Dead Yet UK) and many palliative-care doctors fear pressure on vulnerable people.
  • So the divide is not simply "religious against secular".

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Views on the moral status of the embryo and fetus: from conception, gradualist, or from birth; potentiality and twinning arguments are central.
  • Personhood (Warren, Singer) and the woman's rights (Thomson) are separate questions; Marquis argues against abortion without religious premises.

Mission 2

  • UK law licenses embryo research up to 14 days and PGD for serious disease; reproductive cloning and embryo editing for reproduction are illegal. Embryonic stem cells require destroying embryos; adult, iPS and cord-blood stem cells do not.
  • Natural law (protect embryonic life), situation ethics (Fletcher's welcome to genetic control) and virtue ethics (compassion, humility) give different answers; equal dignity and "giftedness" frame the debate about design.

Mission 3

  • Catholic natural law forbids direct abortion but, through double effect, permits treatments such as removing a cancerous womb or an ectopic tube.
  • Situation ethics decides by agape case by case; Hursthouse asks what a virtuous person would do; Singer and Glover deny that early fetuses have the status of persons.
  • The law in Great Britain rests on grounds and a 24-week limit for most grounds; Northern Ireland's 2020 Regulations differ.

Mission 4

  • Euthanasia can be active or passive, and voluntary, non-voluntary or involuntary; in assisted suicide the person takes the last act.
  • Sanctity of life (religious: life is God-given and bears God's image) and quality of life (secular: life needs certain attributes to have value) give different answers; autonomy adds the person's own choice.
