Mission 1

Mission 1: Why can manganese be +2 to +7, while calcium is only ever +2?

Make a guess: Why can manganese be +2 to +7, while calcium is only ever +2?

Mission 1

Atoms from Sc to Zn

  • Across period 4, after Ca, electrons go into the 3d sub-shell; 4s is already filled.
  • Write 3d before 4s: Mn = [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s²; Fe = [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s².
  • Two exceptions: Cr = [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹; Cu = [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹.
Mission 1

Ions: 4s electrons leave first

  • When a transition metal forms an ion, it loses its 4s electrons first, then 3d.
  • Mn [Ar] 3d⁵ 4s² → Mn²⁺ [Ar] 3d⁵.
  • Fe [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s² → Fe²⁺ [Ar] 3d⁶ → Fe³⁺ [Ar] 3d⁵.
  • Cu [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹ → Cu²⁺ [Ar] 3d⁹.
  • 4s fills first but empties first: once 3d holds electrons, 4s is the higher in energy.
Quick check
?What is the electron configuration of the Cr³⁺ ion?
Mission 1

What makes a transition element

  • The d-block metals from Ti to Cu have partly filled 3d orbitals in their ions: this gives them their typical chemistry.
  • Sc forms only Sc³⁺, [Ar]: empty 3d. Zn forms only Zn²⁺, [Ar] 3d¹⁰: full 3d. Neither counts.
  • "d-block" is a position in the table; "transition" depends on the ions.
Quick check
?Copper, [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹, is a d-block element. Is copper a transition element?
Mission 1

Characteristic properties

  • The incomplete d sub-shell gives transition metals four typical properties:
  • Variable oxidation states: Fe +2 and +3; Mn from +2 to +7.
  • Coloured ions: Cu²⁺(aq) blue, Fe²⁺(aq) pale green.
  • Complex formation with ligands (next screens).
  • Catalytic activity: e.g. Fe in the Haber process (more in the catalysis mission).
  • Compare Ca and Zn: one oxidation state each (+2), and white compounds.
Mission 1

Ligands and complexes

  • A ligand is a molecule or ion that donates a pair of electrons to a central metal atom or ion.
  • Each ligand forms a co-ordinate (dative covalent) bond: both electrons come from the ligand.
  • A complex is a central metal atom or ion surrounded by ligands.
  • Co-ordination number = the number of co-ordinate bonds to the central metal atom or ion.
  • [Cu(H2O)6]²⁺: six O→Cu bonds, so co-ordination number 6.
Quick check
?Which species can act as a ligand in a complex ion?
Mission 1

How many bonds per ligand

  • Monodentate ligands form one co-ordinate bond: H2O, NH3, Cl⁻.
  • Tetrahedral and octahedral complexes are both held by dative bonds: lone pairs from the ligands into the metal ion.
Mission 1

Charge and co-ordination number

  • Charge on the complex = charge on the metal ion + charges on the ligands.
  • [Co(H2O)6]²⁺: water is neutral, so Co is +2; co-ordination number 6.
  • [CuCl4]²⁻: x + 4(−1) = −2, so Cu is +2; co-ordination number 4.
Quick check
?In the complex ion [Cr(NH3)4Cl2]⁺, what are the co-ordination number and the oxidation state of chromium?
Mission 1

Why manganese has so many states

  • The 4s and 3d sub-shells are close in energy.
  • So successive ionisation energies rise gradually, with no huge jump while 4s and 3d electrons remain.
  • Mn: 717, 1509, 3248 kJ mol⁻¹. Ca: 590, 1145, 4912 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • The energy for a higher state is paid back when the ion forms bonds or a lattice, or is hydrated. So Mn is stable from +2 (Mn²⁺) to +7 (MnO4⁻).
  • Calcium's third electron would come from the inner [Ar] core: IE3 is over 4 times IE2, too much to pay back. So Ca stops at +2.
  • Main states: Cr +3, +6 · Mn +2, +4, +7 · Fe +2, +3 · Co +2, +3 · Cu +1, +2.
Mission check

?Scandium, [Ar] 3d¹ 4s², is in the d-block, yet it only ever forms compounds in the +3 state. Its first four ionisation energies are 633, 1235, 2389 and 7091 kJ mol⁻¹. Which statement explains why scandium stops at +3?

Manganese has seven electrons in 4s and 3d, all close in energy, so it can use any number from 2 to 7. Calcium has two and scandium three; the next would come from the [Ar] core.

Mission 2

Mission 2: How can a metal salt with no hydrogen in its formula give away hydrogen ions?

Make a guess: How can a metal salt with no hydrogen in its formula give away hydrogen ions?

Mission 2

Metal-aqua ions

  • Dissolve a metal salt in water: each metal ion is surrounded by six water ligands.
  • Each H2O donates a lone pair from O to the metal ion: a dative (coordinate) bond.
  • Formula: [M(H2O)6]ⁿ⁺, an octahedral metal-aqua ion.
  • The ion keeps the metal's charge: water ligands are neutral.
Mission 2

Colours of aqua ions

  • [Cu(H2O)6]²⁺: blue.
  • [Fe(H2O)6]²⁺: pale green.
  • [Fe(H2O)6]³⁺: pale violet, but its solutions usually look yellow-brown.
  • [Cr(H2O)6]³⁺: violet; chromium(III) solutions in the lab often look green, because other ligands (e.g. sulfate, chloride) replace some water.
Quick check
?Which list gives the usual colours of solutions of Fe²⁺(aq), Fe³⁺(aq) and Cu²⁺(aq), in that order?
Mission 2

Pulled water ligands

  • The positive metal ion attracts the O lone pair strongly.
  • Electron density is drawn from the O–H bonds towards O and the metal.
  • So the O–H bonds become more polar and weaker.
  • A water ligand can now lose an H⁺, leaving an OH⁻ ligand on the metal.
  • The "hydrogen ions" in the opening question come from the water ligands, not the salt's formula.
Mission 2

Bases pull H⁺ off: a neutral precipitate

  • Add a few drops of OH⁻(aq): it removes H⁺ from water ligands (H⁺ + OH⁻ → H2O).
  • Remove as many H⁺ as the ion's charge: the complex becomes neutral.
  • A neutral complex is insoluble: it appears as a precipitate.
  • [Cu(H2O)6]²⁺(aq) + 2OH⁻(aq) → [Cu(H2O)4(OH)2](s) + 2H2O(l)
  • Blue solution → pale blue precipitate.
Mission 2

Worked example: iron(III) with hydroxide

  • Start: [Fe(H2O)6]³⁺. Charge 3+, so remove 3 H⁺.
  • Product: [Fe(H2O)3(OH)3]. Check: 6 ligands; charge 3+ − 3 = 0.
  • Each removed H⁺ meets an OH⁻: 3H2O formed.
  • [Fe(H2O)6]³⁺(aq) + 3OH⁻(aq) → [Fe(H2O)3(OH)3](s) + 3H2O(l)
  • Observation: yellow-brown solution → orange-brown precipitate.
  • Simple formulae are also accepted: Fe³⁺(aq) + 3OH⁻(aq) → Fe(OH)3(s).
Quick check
?A few drops of NaOH(aq) are added to a solution of [Fe(H2O)6]²⁺. Which species is the precipitate?
Mission check

?Gallium is below aluminium in Group 13 and forms the aqua ion [Ga(H2O)6]³⁺. A few drops of NaOH(aq) are added, and a white precipitate forms. Which species is the precipitate?

No hydrogen in the salt's formula, but plenty in its six water ligands. A base pulls off H⁺ until the complex is neutral, and the solid drops out.

Mission 3

Mission 3: How can one metal give solutions of four different shades, one after another, in the same flask?

Make a guess: How can one metal give solutions of four different shades, one after another, in the same flask?

Mission 3

One metal, many states

  • Transition metals' 4s and 3d electrons are close in energy, so they can lose different numbers of them.
  • So one metal can exist in several oxidation states.
  • Each oxidation state, with its ligands, has its own colour.
  • Redox reagents move a metal between states; E⦵ values predict which reagent will work.
  • Example (background, not on your specification): zinc in acid turns a vanadium(V) solution yellow → blue → green → violet as V goes from +5 to +4, +3 and +2.
Mission 3

Colours to know

  • Chromium: Cr³⁺ green (+3) · Cr2O7²⁻ orange (+6) · CrO4²⁻ yellow (+6).
  • Manganese: MnO4⁻ purple (+7) · Mn²⁺ very pale pink (+2).
  • Iron: Fe²⁺ pale green · Fe³⁺ yellow-brown.
  • Cobalt: Co²⁺ pink · Copper: Cu²⁺ blue.
Mission 3

Chromium: +6 and +3

  • Dichromate(VI), Cr2O7²⁻, orange, Cr +6 → Cr³⁺, green, Cr +3.
  • Reduce it with zinc and acid: Cr2O7²⁻ + 14H⁺ + 6e⁻ → 2Cr³⁺ + 7H2O.
  • Oxidise Cr³⁺ back with H2O2 in alkali: green [Cr(OH)6]³⁻ → yellow CrO4²⁻.
  • 2[Cr(OH)6]³⁻ + 3H2O2 → 2CrO4²⁻ + 2OH⁻ + 8H2O
Mission 3

Chromate and dichromate

  • 2CrO4²⁻ + 2H⁺ ⇌ Cr2O7²⁻ + H2O
  • Add acid: yellow → orange. Add alkali: orange → yellow.
  • Cr is +6 on both sides: this is not redox, just an equilibrium shifting.
  • So after oxidising Cr³⁺ in alkali, acidify to get orange dichromate.
Quick check
?Dilute acid is added to yellow sodium chromate(VI) solution and it turns orange. What happens to the chromium?
Mission check

?Green manganate(VI) ions, MnO4²⁻, are unstable in acid: 3MnO4²⁻ + 4H⁺ → 2MnO4⁻ + MnO2 + 2H2O. The solution turns purple and a brown solid forms. What happens to the manganese?

Each colour is a different oxidation state (with its own ligands). A redox reagent moves the metal from one to the next, and E⦵ values tell you how far it can go.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Why does only one of two platinum compounds with the same formula treat cancer?

Make a guess: Why does only one of two platinum compounds with the same formula treat cancer?

Mission 4

Shape from co-ordination number

  • The co-ordinate bonds around the metal ion repel each other and get as far apart as possible.
  • 6 bonds → octahedral, angles 90°.
  • 4 bonds → tetrahedral (109.5°) or square planar (90°).
Mission 4

Octahedral: small ligands

  • Small ligands such as H2O and NH3: six fit around the metal ion.
  • [Cu(H2O)6]²⁺, [Fe(H2O)6]³⁺, [Co(H2O)6]²⁺: octahedral, 90°.
  • [Cu(NH3)4(H2O)2]²⁺: four NH3 + two H2O = six bonds, still octahedral.
  • Draw it with wedges: two bonds up and down in the plane of the page, two wedges (towards you), two dashes (away).
Mission 4

Tetrahedral: larger ligands

  • Cl⁻ is larger than H2O or NH3: only four fit around the metal ion.
  • [CuCl4]²⁻ and [CoCl4]²⁻: tetrahedral, 109.5°.
Mission 4

Square planar

  • Some four-co-ordinate complexes are flat: square planar, all angles 90°.
  • Platinum(II) complexes are square planar, e.g. Pt(NH3)2Cl2.
  • Square planar complexes are beyond your specification, but they answer this mission's question.
Quick check
?Which complex ion has bond angles of 109.5°?
Mission 4

Cis–trans isomers

  • Pt(NH3)2Cl2 exists as two compounds.
  • Cis: the two Cl on the same side, at 90°. Trans: opposite, at 180°.
  • Same formula, same bonds, different arrangement in space: stereoisomers.
  • Isomerism in complexes is beyond your specification, but it answers this mission's question.
  • Octahedral complexes with two ligands of one kind and four of another show it too: [Co(NH3)4Cl2]⁺, cis (violet) and trans (green).
  • A tetrahedral complex never does: every pair of positions is adjacent.
Quick check
?Platinum(II) complexes are square planar; zinc(II) complexes are tetrahedral. Which complex can exist as cis and trans isomers?
Mission 4

Optical isomers

  • Bidentate ligands can wrap an octahedral ion like the blades of a propeller.
  • [Ni(en)3]²⁺: three en ligands, six Ni–N bonds.
  • The propeller can twist left or right: two non-superimposable mirror images.
  • These are optical isomers (enantiomers): they rotate plane-polarised light in opposite directions.
Quick check
?Ethanedioate, C2O4²⁻, is a bidentate ligand. Which complex ion exists as a pair of optical isomers?
Mission 4

How cisplatin works

  • Cisplatin is cis-Pt(NH3)2Cl2, an anti-cancer drug.
  • Inside a cell, its two Cl⁻ ligands are replaced, first by water.
  • Then platinum bonds to N atoms on two neighbouring guanine bases in DNA.
  • This kinks the DNA, so it can't be copied: DNA replication and cell division stop.
  • It also attacks healthy cells that divide quickly (e.g. hair loss); it can also damage the kidneys.
Mission 4

Why only the cis isomer

  • In cisplatin the two leaving groups are at 90°: they can bond to two neighbouring guanines.
  • In transplatin they are at 180°: it can't make the same link, and it is far less effective.
Mission check

?Two different compounds both contain the octahedral complex ion [Cr(H2O)4Cl2]⁺. How can two compounds contain ions with the same formula?

Shape decides function. Only the cis arrangement lets platinum grip two neighbouring guanines, so only cisplatin treats cancer.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Why is the same cobalt(II) ion pink in water but blue in concentrated hydrochloric acid?

Make a guess: Why is the same cobalt(II) ion pink in water but blue in concentrated hydrochloric acid?

Mission 5

Coloured ions

  • Most transition-metal ions are coloured in solution.
  • [Cu(H2O)6]²⁺ blue · [Co(H2O)6]²⁺ pink · [Fe(H2O)6]²⁺ pale green · [Cr(H2O)6]³⁺ violet (often looks green as other ligands replace water).
  • (Purple MnO4⁻ is coloured too, but Mn(VII) is 3d⁰: its colour comes from charge transfer, not d–d transitions.)
  • Ca²⁺(aq) and Zn²⁺(aq) are colourless.
  • So colour helps to identify a transition-metal ion.
Mission 5

Same cobalt, new ligands

  • [Co(H2O)6]²⁺ + 4Cl⁻ ⇌ [CoCl4]²⁻ + 6H2O
  • Pink → blue. Add water and it turns pink again.
  • Cobalt is +2 in both: x + 4(−1) = −2.
  • What changed: the ligands (H2O → Cl⁻) and the co-ordination number (6 → 4).
  • Next: why a new set of ligands means a new colour.
Quick check
?What are the oxidation state of cobalt and its co-ordination number in [CoCl4]²⁻?
Mission 5

Ligands split the d energy levels

  • In a free ion, the five 3d orbitals have the same energy.
  • Ligands' lone pairs approach and split them into groups of different energy.
  • The gap, ΔE, matches the energy of visible light.
  • A d electron absorbs that light and is promoted across the gap.
Mission 5

The colour you see

  • Only some wavelengths are absorbed. The rest are transmitted (or reflected) and reach your eye.
  • The colour you see is complementary to the colour absorbed: opposite on the colour wheel.
  • [Cu(H2O)6]²⁺ absorbs red–orange light → looks blue.
  • [Co(H2O)6]²⁺ absorbs green light → looks pink.
  • The same rule explains any coloured substance, azo dyes included: you see the visible wavelengths not absorbed.
Quick check
?A solution of a transition-metal complex looks blue in white light. On the colour wheel, blue is opposite orange. Which statement explains the blue colour?
Mission 5

Wavelength, frequency and energy

  • c = λν, so ν = c/λ. Then ΔE = hν = hc/λ.
  • Eduqas writes E = hf, with f for frequency.
  • h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s · c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹ · 1 nm = 10⁻⁹ m.
  • [Co(H2O)6]²⁺ absorbs most strongly at about 540 nm.
  • ν = 3.00 × 10⁸ ÷ 5.40 × 10⁻⁷ = 5.56 × 10¹⁴ s⁻¹ (Hz).
  • ΔE = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 5.56 × 10¹⁴ = 3.68 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.
Calculate
?Blue [CoCl4]²⁻ absorbs most strongly at about 690 nm. Calculate ΔE, in units of 10⁻¹⁹ J. (h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹)
Quick check
?A solution of a transition-metal complex appears red. On the colour wheel, red is opposite green. Take the wavelength of green light as 530 nm and of red light as 700 nm, and c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹. What is the frequency of the light absorbed most strongly?
Mission 5

When there is no colour

  • Colour needs a d electron to promote and a space in a higher d orbital.
  • 3d⁰ ions, such as Sc³⁺ and Ti⁴⁺: no d electron to promote.
  • 3d¹⁰ ions, such as Zn²⁺ and Cu⁺: no space to promote into.
  • So these ions are colourless in solution: Sc³⁺(aq), Zn²⁺(aq). Solids such as ZnSO4 and TiO2 are white.
Quick check
?Which ion forms colourless compounds?
Mission 5

Three ways to change the colour

  • Change the oxidation state: Fe²⁺(aq) pale green → Fe³⁺(aq) yellow-brown.
  • Change the ligand: [Cu(H2O)6]²⁺ blue → [Cu(NH3)4(H2O)2]²⁺ deep blue.
  • Change the co-ordination number: [Cu(H2O)6]²⁺ blue → [CuCl4]²⁻ yellow (6 → 4).
  • Each change alters ΔE, so a different set of wavelengths is absorbed.
Mission check

?Aqueous nickel(II), [Ni(H2O)6]²⁺, is green. With excess ammonia it forms [Ni(NH3)6]²⁺, which is blue-violet. Which statement explains the colour change?

For cobalt, Cl⁻ in place of H2O, and four ligands in place of six, shrink ΔE. The complex absorbs orange–red instead of green, so pink turns blue.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Why can carbon monoxide stop blood carrying oxygen, even when there is plenty of oxygen to breathe?

Make a guess: Why can carbon monoxide stop blood carrying oxygen, even when there is plenty of oxygen to breathe?

Mission 6

Swapping ligands

  • In ligand substitution, one ligand replaces another around the metal ion.
  • NH3 and H2O are similar in size and both uncharged: they swap with no change in co-ordination number.
  • [Cu(H2O)6]²⁺ + 4NH3 → [Cu(NH3)4(H2O)2]²⁺ + 4H2O · blue → deep blue.
  • Add ammonia drop by drop: a pale blue precipitate forms first, then dissolves in excess.
Mission 6

Chloride changes the number

  • Cl⁻ is larger than H2O, and charged: only four fit around the metal ion.
  • [Cu(H2O)6]²⁺ + 4Cl⁻ ⇌ [CuCl4]²⁻ + 6H2O · blue → yellow (green part-way).
  • [Co(H2O)6]²⁺ + 4Cl⁻ ⇌ [CoCl4]²⁻ + 6H2O · pink → blue.
  • Co-ordination number 6 → 4; octahedral → tetrahedral.
  • Concentrated HCl supplies the Cl⁻. Add water and the colour change reverses.
Quick check
?Why does the co-ordination number of copper(II) change when Cl⁻ replaces H2O, but not when NH3 replaces H2O?
Mission 6

Haemoglobin carries oxygen

  • Haemoglobin contains haem: an iron(II) complex with a multidentate ligand (four N atoms around Fe²⁺).
  • A fifth bond holds the Fe²⁺ to the protein.
  • At the sixth site, O2 forms a co-ordinate bond to Fe²⁺.
  • In the lungs O2 binds; in the tissues, where O2 is low, it is released. The bond is weak and reversible.
Mission 6

Carbon monoxide takes the seat

  • CO bonds to the same site on Fe²⁺, about 200 times more strongly than O2.
  • It is a ligand substitution: CO replaces O2.
  • HbO2 + CO ⇌ HbCO + O2 has K ≈ 200: even air with just 0.1% CO can tie up about half the haem.
  • A haem with CO bonded can't carry O2.
  • Tissues are starved of oxygen: CO is toxic.
Quick check
?People with carbon monoxide poisoning are given pure oxygen to breathe, sometimes at high pressure. Why does this help?
Mission check

?A few drops of dilute potassium thiocyanate, KSCN, are added to a pale solution of Fe³⁺(aq). It turns deep red as [Fe(SCN)(H2O)5]²⁺ forms, even though water molecules outnumber thiocyanate ions by thousands to one. What does this show?

Being outnumbered doesn't decide a ligand contest. The ligand that bonds more strongly wins the site, so a little CO beats a lot of O2 for the iron in haem.

Mission 7: How can a substance join in a reaction, change, and still come out unchanged at the end?

Make a guess: How can a substance join in a reaction, change, and still come out unchanged at the end?

Mission 7

What a catalyst does

  • A catalyst speeds up a reaction but is not used up overall.
  • It gives a different route with a lower activation energy.
  • It does react: it is used in one step and re-formed in a later one.
  • So its overall amount stays the same. One catalyst particle works again and again.
  • It does not change ΔH or the equilibrium yield.
Mission 7

Two kinds of catalyst

  • Homogeneous: in the same phase as the reactants (e.g. all in solution).
  • It reacts via an intermediate species, then is re-formed.
  • Heterogeneous: in a different phase (usually a solid with gases or liquids).
  • The reaction happens on its surface.
  • Transition metals and their compounds are good at both.
Mission 7

How a solid surface works

  • Adsorption: reactant molecules bond to active sites on the surface.
  • Bonds in the reactants weaken, and the molecules are held close, in a good orientation.
  • They react on the surface.
  • Desorption: products leave, freeing the sites for more reactants.
Mission 7

Catalysts at work

  • Catalysts let industry run at lower temperatures: less energy, less fossil fuel burned, less CO2.
  • Haber process (ammonia): iron, Fe.
  • Contact process (SO2 → SO3): vanadium(V) oxide, V2O5.
  • Hydrogenation of alkenes: nickel, Ni.
  • MnO2 speeds up the decomposition of H2O2.
Quick check
?In which reaction is the catalyst homogeneous?
Quick check
?Iron is used as a catalyst in the Haber process, N2(g) + 3H2(g) ⇌ 2NH3(g). Which statement is correct?
Mission 7

Fe²⁺: a homogeneous catalyst

  • S2O8²⁻ + 2I⁻ → 2SO4²⁻ + I2 is slow: two negative ions repel, so Ea is high.
  • Step 1: S2O8²⁻ + 2Fe²⁺ → 2SO4²⁻ + 2Fe³⁺.
  • Step 2: 2Fe³⁺ + 2I⁻ → 2Fe²⁺ + I2.
  • Each step brings together oppositely charged ions: lower Ea. Fe²⁺ is re-formed.
  • Fe³⁺ is the intermediate species.
  • This is homogeneous catalysis through a variable oxidation state (Fe +2 ⇌ +3).
Mission check

?High in the atmosphere, chlorine atoms destroy ozone in two steps. Overall: O3 + O → 2O2. Which statement is correct?
  • Step 1: Cl• + O3 → ClO• + O2. Step 2: ClO• + O → Cl• + O2.

That's how a catalyst joins in, changes, and comes out unchanged. One chlorine atom can go around this cycle many thousands of times.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Ions lose 4s before 3d. Transition elements (Ti–Cu) have an ion with an incomplete d sub-shell; ligands bond to them by donating lone pairs, and co-ordination number counts those bonds.
  • 4s and 3d electrons are close in energy, so transition metals show variable oxidation states.

Mission 2

  • [M(H2O)6]ⁿ⁺: the metal ion polarises its water ligands, so they can lose H⁺.
  • OH⁻ removes as many H⁺ as the charge: the neutral complex precipitates.

Mission 3

  • Transition metals have several oxidation states, each with its own colour, linked by redox.
  • Cr2O7²⁻ (orange, +6) ⇌ Cr³⁺ (green, +3) by redox; CrO4²⁻ ⇌ Cr2O7²⁻ by acid and alkali, no redox.

Mission 4

  • 6 bonds: octahedral (90°). 4 bonds: tetrahedral (109.5°) with large ligands like Cl⁻, or square planar (90°) for Pt(II).
  • Cis–trans: same formula, ligands at 90° or 180°. Only cisplatin binds two neighbouring guanines.
  • Three bidentate ligands make a chiral "propeller" with optical isomers.

Mission 5

  • Change the oxidation state, ligand or co-ordination number, and the colour changes.
  • Ligands split the d orbitals; a d electron absorbs visible light (ΔE = hν) and you see the complementary colour. 3d⁰ and 3d¹⁰ ions are colourless.

Mission 6

  • Ligand substitution: NH3 ↔ H2O keeps the co-ordination number; Cl⁻ (larger, charged) changes it from 6 to 4. CO replaces O2 at the Fe²⁺ of haem because it bonds about 200 times more strongly.

Mission 7

  • A catalyst reacts in one step and is re-formed in a later one, by a route with lower Ea.
  • Homogeneous: same phase, via an intermediate. Heterogeneous: different phase, on the surface.
