Mission 1

Mission 1: Much of a gene's first RNA copy is cut out before it leaves the nucleus. What is kept, and why does it matter?

Make a guess: Much of a gene's first RNA copy is cut out before it leaves the nucleus. What is kept, and why does it matter?

Mission 1

From pre-mRNA to mature mRNA

  • In eukaryotes, the whole gene, introns included, is transcribed. The product is pre-mRNA.
  • Splicing: the introns are removed and the exons are joined.
  • The mature mRNA leaves the nucleus.
  • These are post-transcriptional changes to mRNA.
  • Say: "introns are removed from the RNA" — not: "introns are cut out of the gene's DNA".

?A eukaryotic gene is transcribed into a pre-mRNA with five sections, in order: exon 1, intron 1, exon 2, intron 2, exon 3. Which is the mature mRNA?

Mission 1

One gene, several proteins

  • Exons from one pre-mRNA can be joined in different combinations: alternative splicing.
  • So one gene can code for several different polypeptides.
  • Different cell types can splice the same gene differently.
  • Example: exons 1 and 4 always kept; exons 2 and 3 each kept or not. That gives 2 × 2 = 4 different mRNAs.
  • Humans have about 20 000 protein-coding genes, yet make far more different polypeptides.
  • Most human genes with several exons are alternatively spliced.
Quick check
?The human genome contains about 20 000 protein-coding genes, yet human cells produce well over 20 000 different polypeptides. Which process accounts for this?
Mission check

?A eukaryotic gene has four exons and three introns. In one family, a single-base change at the edge of intron 2 means intron 2 is no longer recognised for splicing. All other splicing is normal. Which prediction is correct?

Splicing decides which parts of the first RNA copy reach the ribosome. Keep one intron by mistake and the message changes.

Mission 2

Mission 2: DNA has only four letters. How can a gene spell out a protein built from twenty different building blocks?

Make a guess: DNA has only four letters. How can a gene spell out a protein built from twenty different building blocks?

Mission 2

What is a gene?

  • A gene is a sequence of bases on DNA that codes for the amino acid sequence of a polypeptide.
  • Each gene sits at a fixed position on a chromosome: its locus.
  • Say: "a gene codes for a polypeptide" — not: "a gene codes for a characteristic".
Mission 2

Why groups of three?

  • DNA has 4 bases. Polypeptides use 20 amino acids.
  • One base per amino acid: 4 codes. Two bases: 4² = 16. Too few.
  • Three bases: 4³ = 64 codes. Enough for all 20.
  • So a sequence of three bases, a base triplet, codes for one amino acid. This is the genetic code.
  • On mRNA, each triplet is a codon. The sequence is read in codons.
  • 61 codons code for amino acids; 3 are stop codons.
Quick check
?Why does the genetic code use codons of three bases rather than two?
Mission 2

Features of the code

  • Non-overlapping: each base belongs to one triplet only. Triplets are read one after another.
  • Degenerate: most amino acids have more than one codon. Glycine: GGU, GGC, GGA, GGG.
  • AUG is the start codon. It also codes for methionine.
  • Stop codons (UAA, UAG, UGA) code for no amino acid.
  • Say: "degenerate: several codons, one amino acid" — not: "one codon, several amino acids".
Quick check
?The genetic code is described as degenerate. What does this mean?
Mission 2

Worked example: reading the code

mRNA: AUGGCUUGGAAAUAGCC

  • Find the start codon: AUG.
  • Split into triplets from there: AUG | GCU | UGG | AAA | UAG | CC
  • Look up each codon in the table: Met – Ala – Trp – Lys – stop.
  • Stop at the stop codon. The polypeptide has 4 amino acids.
  • Exams give you the codon data you need. You do not learn codons by heart.

Mission 3

Mission 3: A ribosome never sees a single amino acid's name. So how does it build the right protein?

Make a guess: A ribosome never sees a single amino acid's name. So how does it build the right protein?

Mission 3

From gene to ribosome

  • DNA holds the genetic information. In eukaryotic cells it stays in the nucleus.
  • mRNA carries a copy of one gene's information to the ribosomes in the cytoplasm.
  • Translation: the base sequence of mRNA is used to build the amino acid sequence of a polypeptide.
  • The mRNA is read in codons: three bases = one amino acid (or a stop signal).
  • Amino acids are not made from the bases. They already float in the cytoplasm.
  • Say: "translation makes a polypeptide on a ribosome" — not: "the ribosome converts bases into amino acids".
Mission 3

Two RNAs, two shapes

  • mRNA: a single, linear strand; hundreds to thousands of nucleotides; a run of codons. No folded clover-leaf.
  • tRNA: a single strand of about 80 nucleotides, folded into a clover-leaf shape.
  • The fold is held by hydrogen bonds between complementary bases in the same strand.
  • One loop carries the anticodon: three bases.
  • The 3′ end is the amino acid attachment site. Each tRNA carries one specific amino acid.
Mission 3

The ribosome

  • Made of rRNA and protein, in two subunits: small and large.
  • The small subunit binds the mRNA.
  • The large subunit holds two tRNAs at the same time.
  • rRNA gives the ribosome its shape and catalyses peptide bond formation.
Quick check
?A cell contains many different kinds of tRNA molecule. What is the role of a tRNA during translation?
Mission 3

Codon meets anticodon

  • The anticodon is complementary to the codon: A–U, U–A, G–C, C–G.
  • RNA has uracil, not thymine. So codon AUG pairs with anticodon UAC.
  • Codon and anticodon are held by hydrogen bonds: weak, so the tRNA can leave later.
  • The ribosome checks only this pairing. It never checks the amino acid.
  • Say: "complementary to the codon" — not: "the same as the codon".
Mission 3

Elongation, one codon at a time

  • (1) Two tRNAs sit on the ribosome, each paired with its codon.
  • (2) A peptide bond forms between their amino acids, by condensation. The chain now hangs on the second tRNA.
  • (3) The ribosome moves one codon along the mRNA. The empty tRNA leaves.
  • (4) The next tRNA pairs with the newly exposed codon. Repeat.
Quick check
?An antibiotic stops the ribosome from moving along the mRNA but does not affect the binding of tRNAs or the formation of peptide bonds. What effect will this have on translation of an mRNA?
Mission 3

Start, stop and the energy bill

  • Start codon AUG: translation begins here. AUG also codes for methionine, the first amino acid.
  • Stop codons (UAA, UAG, UGA): no tRNA pairs with them. The finished polypeptide is released.
  • Many ribosomes can translate one mRNA at once, so many copies of the polypeptide are made quickly.
Mission 3

Worked example: decoding a gene

  • Template DNA strand: 3′–TAC AAG CCT TTC ACT–5′.
  • Step 1. mRNA is complementary to the template, with U for T: AUG UUC GGA AAG UGA.
  • Step 2. Read from AUG in threes. Use the table: AUG Met · UUC Phe · GGA Gly · AAG Lys · UGA stop.
  • Step 3. Polypeptide: Met–Phe–Gly–Lys. Four amino acids; the stop codon adds none.
  • Step 4. Anticodons used (each written 3′→5′): UAC, AAG, CCU, UUC. None for UGA.
  • Notice: the anticodons have the template's sequence, with U for T.
Quick check
?The coding region of an mRNA, from the first nucleotide of the start codon to the last nucleotide of the stop codon, is 903 nucleotides long. How many amino acids does the polypeptide translated from this region contain?

Mission check

?In a test tube translation system, the tRNAs with the anticodon 3′-ACG-5′ normally carry the amino acid cysteine, and they are the only tRNAs in the system that pair with the codon 5′-UGC-3′. Before translation, the cysteine attached to these tRNAs is chemically changed into a different amino acid, alanine; the tRNAs themselves are unchanged. The ribosome reads codons, never amino acids. The tRNA is the adapter that links the two, so whatever it carries goes in.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Every protein in your body is built from just twenty building blocks. How can so few make so many?

Make a guess: Every protein in your body is built from just twenty building blocks. How can so few make so many?

Mission 4

One plan, twenty versions

  • Amino acids are the monomers of proteins.
  • A central carbon atom carries four groups:
  • Amine group (–NH2), carboxyl group (–COOH), a hydrogen atom, and an R group (side chain).
  • The twenty amino acids in proteins differ only in their R group.
  • Say: "amine group, –NH2" — not: "–NH3". Nitrogen's third bond is to the central carbon.
Quick check
?Which feature is shared by all 20 of the amino acids that are coded for in the genetic code?
Mission 4

The peptide bond

  • (1) Line up two amino acids: the carboxyl group of one beside the amine group of the next.
  • (2) –OH from the carboxyl and –H from the amine leave as one water molecule.
  • (3) C joins N: a peptide bond, –CO–NH–. A covalent bond. Two amino acids make a dipeptide.
  • This is a condensation reaction.
  • Hydrolysis reverses it: adding water breaks the peptide bond.
  • The R groups take no part.
Quick check
?Two amino acids react to form a dipeptide. Which statement describes this reaction?
Mission 4

Worked example: counting bonds and water

  • Many amino acids joined in a chain: a polypeptide.
  • A chain has two ends: a free amine group at one, a free carboxyl group at the other.
  • n amino acids → n − 1 peptide bonds → n − 1 water molecules released.
  • 3 amino acids: 2 bonds, 2 H2O.
  • Hydrolysis of the chain uses the same number of water molecules.
  • Any of the twenty can sit at any position, repeats allowed, in any order, in chains of any length. So the number of possible sequences is effectively unlimited.
Quick check
?A polypeptide of 150 amino acids is assembled by condensation reactions. How does the number of water molecules change as the whole chain is formed?
Mission check

?Two short polypeptides, P and Q, are each made of the same five amino acids, but the amino acids are joined in a different order in P and in Q. Which statement about P and Q is correct?

Twenty kinds, joined in any order, any number of times, to any length. That is how so few make so many.

Make a guess: A protein is only a chain of amino acids. So how does it fold into the exact shape its job needs?

Mission 5

Sequence, shape, job

  • A polypeptide folds into a precise three-dimensional shape.
  • Bonds between parts of the chain hold that shape. Most are weak; together they are strong.
  • The shape decides the function: enzymes, hormones, antibodies, transport, support.
  • The same sequence folds the same way, every time.
  • Different sequences fold differently. So endless sequences give endless proteins.
  • Four levels describe the shape: primary, secondary, tertiary, quaternary.
Mission 5

Primary structure

  • Primary structure: the sequence of amino acids in a polypeptide.
  • Held by peptide bonds. Set by the gene.
  • The sequence, with the exact position of each amino acid, determines every later level.
  • Change one amino acid, anywhere, and the shape can change.
  • Say: "primary structure = the order of amino acids" — not: "which amino acids are present".
Mission 5

Secondary structure

  • The backbone carries polar C=O and N–H groups.
  • Hydrogen bonds form between the C=O of one peptide bond and the N–H of another, at regular positions.
  • α-helix: the chain coils; each turn is held to the next.
  • β-pleated sheet: lengths of chain lie side by side, folded like pleats, held to each other.
  • R groups point away from the backbone and take no part in these bonds, so helices and sheets form in many sequences.
  • Say: "backbone hydrogen bonds" — not: "bonds between R groups".
Quick check
?What stabilises the alpha helices and beta-pleated sheets of a protein's secondary structure?
Mission 5

Tertiary structure: R groups lock the fold

  • The chain, with its helices and sheets, folds further into its overall 3D shape: tertiary structure.
  • Held by bonds between R groups:
  • Hydrogen bonds: between polar R groups. Weak, but many.
  • Ionic bonds: between a –COO⁻ and an –NH3⁺ R group. pH-sensitive.
  • Disulfide bridges: covalent S–S bonds between two cysteines. Strong.
  • Hydrophobic interactions: non-polar R groups cluster together, away from water.
Mission 5

Oily inside, watery outside

  • In a water-soluble protein: non-polar R groups cluster in the core.
  • Polar and charged R groups sit on the surface, in contact with water. So the protein dissolves.
  • Why: water molecules hydrogen-bond to each other and exclude non-polar groups.
  • Say: "non-polar R groups are excluded from water" — not: "they repel water".
Quick check
?In one region of a polypeptide there are two cysteines, an amino acid whose R-group contains a carboxyl group and an amino acid whose R-group contains an amine group. Which statement about the bonds these could form in the tertiary structure is correct?
Mission 5

Quaternary structure

  • Some proteins are built from more than one polypeptide: quaternary structure.
  • The polypeptides are held together by the same kinds of bond as tertiary structure.
  • Example: haemoglobin, four polypeptides (see Mission 6).
  • All four levels together decide what the protein does.
Mission 5

Denaturation

  • (1) A folded protein: its shape held by many weak bonds.
  • (2) High temperature: more vibration breaks hydrogen bonds and ionic bonds. The chain unfolds.
  • (3) pH change: gaining or losing H⁺ changes the charges on R groups. Ionic and hydrogen bonds break.
  • This loss of shape is denaturation. The protein can no longer do its job.
  • Peptide bonds stay intact: the sequence is unchanged.
  • Usually permanent: the chains do not refold correctly. A cooked egg white stays white.
  • Cold does not denature: it slows molecules down, and activity returns on warming.
  • Say: "the enzyme is denatured" — not: "the enzyme is killed".
Quick check
?When egg white is heated, its soluble proteins turn into a white solid. What has happened to the protein molecules?
Quick checkRead the data
?(Illustrative data.) An enzyme's activity at its optimum pH of 7 was 100 units. One sample was placed at pH 2 for 10 minutes, then returned to pH 7: its activity was 4 units after 1 hour and 4 units after 24 hours, and its polypeptide chains were found to be intact. A second sample was cooled to 5 °C for 10 minutes, then returned to 37 °C: its activity was 100 units. What do these results show?
Mission check

?A mutation replaces one amino acid in an enzyme at a position on the surface far from the active site in the folded protein. The enzyme loses most of its activity. What is the best explanation?

Sequence sets shape, and shape sets function. One amino acid in the wrong place can be enough.

  • Both have a precise, repeatable 3D structure.
  • Say: "fibrous proteins have an elongated structure" — not: "fibrous proteins are unfolded".
Mission 6

Haemoglobin

  • A globular protein of four polypeptides: two α and two β. So it has quaternary structure.
  • Each polypeptide holds one haem group: a ring with an Fe²⁺ ion at its centre.
  • Each haem binds one O2. One haemoglobin carries up to four O2.
  • Haem is not made of amino acids. It is a prosthetic group.
  • Polar R groups on the surface keep it soluble inside red blood cells.
Quick check
?A haemoglobin molecule is made of four polypeptides, each holding one haem group that contains an Fe²⁺ ion. Which statement about haemoglobin is correct?
Mission 6

Collagen: built to take a pull

  • Three polypeptides wound round each other: a triple helix.
  • Every third amino acid is glycine, the smallest. So the three chains pack tightly.
  • Hydrogen bonds hold the three chains together. Covalent cross-links join neighbouring molecules into fibrils, then fibres.
  • Long, insoluble, with very high tensile strength.
  • Found in tendons, ligaments, skin, bone, cartilage and artery walls.
Quick check
?Protein X is compact and roughly spherical, dissolves in blood plasma and binds to a specific receptor on target cells. Protein Y forms long insoluble fibres with high tensile strength in tendons. Which statement correctly relates the form of each protein to its function?
Mission check

?Spider dragline silk is a protein. Its long polypeptide chains lie parallel to one another, with many stretches folded into β-pleated sheets that are stacked and held together by hydrogen bonds. It does not dissolve in water. Which statement best explains why it suits the frame of a web?

Long and cross-linked for strength, compact and soluble for work in solution. Shape fits job.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Eukaryotic pre-mRNA is spliced: introns removed, exons joined.
  • Alternative splicing lets one gene code for several polypeptides.

Mission 2

  • A gene is a base sequence coding for a polypeptide's amino acid sequence; it has a fixed locus.
  • The code is a triplet code, read in codons: degenerate and (nearly) universal.
  • It is also non-overlapping: each base is read in one triplet only.

Mission 3

  • mRNA codons pair with complementary tRNA anticodons on the ribosome; each tRNA brings its amino acid, and peptide bonds join them one codon at a time from AUG to a stop codon.

Mission 4

  • Every amino acid: central carbon + amine group + carboxyl group + H + R group; only R differs.
  • Condensation joins carboxyl to amine: a peptide bond and one water per bond; hydrolysis reverses it.

Mission 5

  • The amino acid sequence decides how a chain folds; bonds between parts of the chain hold the shape; the shape decides the job.
  • Primary (sequence) → secondary (backbone H bonds: α-helix, β-pleated sheet) → tertiary (R-group bonds) → quaternary (more than one polypeptide).
  • Heat and pH changes break the weak bonds: the protein is denatured, though its peptide bonds survive.

Mission 6

  • Globular proteins (haemoglobin, insulin, enzymes) are compact and soluble; fibrous proteins (collagen) are long, insoluble and strong.
  • Haemoglobin: four polypeptides, each with a haem prosthetic group holding Fe²⁺; one O2 per haem.
