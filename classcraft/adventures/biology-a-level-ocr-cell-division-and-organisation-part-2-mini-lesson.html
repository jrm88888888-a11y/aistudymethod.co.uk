Mission 1

Mission 1

One cell becomes hundreds of cell types

  • You began as one cell: a zygote.
  • Mitosis made many genetically identical, unspecialised cells.
  • Differentiation: each cell becomes specialised, with a structure suited to one function.
  • A nerve cell and a muscle cell keep the same genes.
  • They differ in which genes are switched on.
  • Visible differentiation comes from expressing genes for tissue-specific proteins.
  • Say: "different genes are expressed" — not: "different genes are kept".
Quick check
?In a human, a neuron and a liver cell are both descended by mitosis from the same zygote. What is the difference between them once they have differentiated?
Mission 1

Cells → tissues → organs → systems

  • Tissue: a group of specialised cells working together to perform the same function.
  • Organ: several tissues grouped together to perform an overall function.
  • Organ system: organs working together, e.g. the digestive system.
  • Stomach (organ): epithelium + muscle + connective tissue.
  • Leaf (organ): epidermis + mesophyll + xylem + phloem.
  • A tissue can hold more than one cell type, e.g. ciliated cells and goblet cells in airway epithelium.
Mission 1

Tissues to recognise

  • Squamous epithelium: one layer of flat cells. Short diffusion path (alveoli, capillary walls).
  • Ciliated epithelium: columnar cells with cilia that waft mucus (trachea, bronchi).
  • Cartilage: a connective tissue. Cells (chondrocytes) in a firm matrix of collagen. Supports the trachea.
  • Muscle: cells that contract. Skeletal, smooth, cardiac.
  • Xylem: carries water and mineral ions; lignified, dead vessels.
  • Phloem: carries sugars (assimilates); living sieve tube elements with companion cells.
Quick check
?The wall of the stomach contains epithelium, smooth muscle and connective tissue. Together they digest and churn food. Which statement is correct?
Mission check

?In a hypothetical species of plant, leaf cells make large amounts of a protein that binds chlorophyll, but the root cells of the same plant make none of it. Which statement best explains this difference?

Same genes in every cell; different genes switched on. That is what makes a leaf cell, a root cell, a nerve cell or a muscle cell.

Mission 2

Read a cell by its organelles

  • Many mitochondria → lots of ATP: for active transport or movement.
  • Lots of RER and Golgi → making and secreting proteins.
  • Microvilli or folded membranes → large surface area.
  • Many lysosomes → digesting engulfed material.
  • Many chloroplasts → photosynthesis.
  • Say: "mitochondria release energy in respiration, making ATP" — not: "mitochondria make energy".
Mission 2

Specialised animal cells

  • Erythrocyte: biconcave disc → large surface area for O2 diffusion. No nucleus → more room for haemoglobin. Flexible → squeezes through capillaries.
  • Neutrophil: lobed nucleus → squeezes between cells. Many lysosomes → digests pathogens it engulfs.
  • Squamous epithelial cell: flat and thin → short diffusion distance.
  • Ciliated epithelial cell: cilia beat to move mucus. Many mitochondria → ATP for beating.
  • Sperm: flagellum to swim. Mitochondria in midpiece. Acrosome enzymes digest a path into the egg.
  • Say: "the biconcave shape gives a large surface area" — not: "it holds more haemoglobin".
Mission 2

Specialised plant cells

  • Palisade cell: column-shaped, packed with chloroplasts, near the upper surface → absorbs most light.
  • Root hair cell: long hair → large surface area for water and mineral ion uptake. Many mitochondria → ATP for active transport of mineral ions.
  • Guard cells: a pair around each stoma. Take in water → swell → bend apart → stoma opens.
  • Guard cell wall is thicker on the pore side, so swelling bends the cells.
  • Water enters a root hair by osmosis, not active transport.
Quick check

Mission 3

What makes a stem cell?

  • A stem cell is undifferentiated. It can do two things:
  • (1) Divide again and again, renewing itself.
  • (2) Differentiate into specialised cell types.
  • So stem cells are a renewing source of undifferentiated cells.
  • You may meet terms for how many cell types a stem cell can make: totipotent (all, including placenta), pluripotent (all body cells), multipotent (a limited range).
  • Most specialised cells cannot do both.
  • Say: "able to divide endlessly" — not: "always dividing". Stem cells can rest between divisions.
Quick check
?Which pair of properties defines a stem cell?
Mission 3

Stem cells in the adult body

  • Bone marrow: stem cells make every type of blood cell.
  • Erythrocytes: as they mature they lose the nucleus and most organelles and fill with haemoglobin. They live about 120 days, so are made constantly.
  • Neutrophils: develop a lobed nucleus and many lysosomes.
Quick check
?Which statement about the production of erythrocytes and neutrophils is correct?
Mission 3

Plant stem cells: meristems

  • Plants keep stem cells all their lives, in meristems.
  • Apical meristems: root and shoot tips. Vascular cambium: a ring between xylem and phloem.
  • Cambium cells divide. Daughters on one side → xylem; on the other → phloem.
  • Xylem vessel: cells elongate; walls thicken with lignin; cells die; end walls break down → a continuous hollow tube.
  • Phloem sieve tube element: loses its nucleus and most organelles; end walls become sieve plates. A companion cell, from the same parent cell, keeps it alive.
Mission check

?A cell is taken from adult bone marrow and grown in a dish. It divides many times. Some daughter cells stay like the parent; others become erythrocytes, neutrophils and other blood cells, but never nerve or muscle cells. Which statement is correct?

An early embryo cell can still become anything; a bone-marrow stem cell is limited to blood.

Mission 4

Mission 4

Three sources of stem cells

  • Embryonic stem cells: from the inner cell mass of an early embryo, often a spare IVF embryo. Can become any body cell type. Collecting them destroys the embryo.
  • Adult stem cells: e.g. bone marrow. Make a limited range of cell types. Can come from the patient.
  • Induced pluripotent stem (iPS) cells: adult body cells reprogrammed to behave like embryonic stem cells. No embryo needed.
Mission 4

What stem cells are used for

  • In routine use: bone marrow (blood stem cell) transplants treat leukaemia and some other blood disorders.
  • Tissue repair: stem cells from the edge of the cornea can restore a burnt eye surface.
  • In clinical trials: dopamine-releasing neurons for Parkinson's; insulin-secreting cells for type 1 diabetes; retinal cells for an eye disease (macular degeneration).
  • Alzheimer's: neurons grown from patients' iPS cells are used to study the disease and test drugs. No stem cell treatment yet.
  • Research: watching stem cells differentiate shows how organisms develop (developmental biology). Human cells for testing drugs.
  • Type 1 diabetes today: insulin made by GM bacteria, injected. Stem cells could replace the destroyed β cells, so the body makes its own insulin.
Quick check
?Bone marrow transplants are used to treat leukaemia. Which statement explains how a transplant replaces the patient's blood cells?
Mission 4

Weighing the case

  • Possible benefits: treatments for conditions with no cure; fewer donor organs needed; human cells for research and drug testing.
  • Safety: leftover undifferentiated cells can form tumours; donor cells may be rejected unless matched; long-term effects are still being studied.
  • Ethics: embryonic stem cells come from embryos that are destroyed.
  • Views differ on the moral status of an early embryo: some hold that it has full status from fertilisation; others that status develops later.
  • Points often raised: spare IVF embryos would otherwise be discarded; iPS and adult cells avoid embryos but have their own limits.
  • Access: cost; and unregulated clinics selling unproven treatments.
  • Evidence shows what stem cells can do. Whether they should be used also depends on values.
Mission check

?In 2014, researchers in Japan treated a patient with macular degeneration, an eye disease. They made iPS cells from the patient's own skin, grew them into a sheet of retinal cells and transplanted it. Which statement about this approach is correct?

Stem cells could repair what the body cannot. Using them well means weighing benefits, risks and values, not evidence alone.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Differentiation: cells with the same genes become specialised by expressing different genes.
  • Specialised cells form tissues; tissues form organs; organs form systems.

Mission 2

  • A cell's shape, size and organelles match its function.
  • Many mitochondria → active transport or movement; microvilli → large surface area; chloroplasts → photosynthesis.

Mission 3

  • A stem cell can divide repeatedly and differentiate into specialised cells.
  • Bone-marrow stem cells make erythrocytes and neutrophils; meristems make xylem and phloem.

Mission 4

  • Stem cells come from embryos, adult tissues, or reprogrammed adult cells (iPS cells).
  • Uses range from routine (bone marrow transplants) to trials (Parkinson's, type 1 diabetes); each use is weighed for benefit, safety and ethics.
