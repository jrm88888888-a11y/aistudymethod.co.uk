Mission 1

Mission 1: If the world looks designed, does that point to God, to many makers, or to nature itself?

Make a guess: If the world looks designed, does that point to God, to many makers, or to nature itself?

Mission 1Read the source

Hume: the house and the universe

David Hume, Dialogues concerning Natural Religion (1779), Part II. Philo, the sceptic, speaks to Cleanthes, who defends design:

> "If we see a house, CLEANTHES, we conclude, with the greatest certainty, that it had an architect or builder …"

> "But surely you will not affirm, that the universe bears such a resemblance to a house, that we can with the same certainty infer a similar cause …"

  • A design argument is only as strong as its analogy. The universe is very unlike a house, so the inference is at most "a guess, a conjecture".

Q1 Why is the house inference certain?

Show answer

We have often seen houses built by builders.

Q2 What weakens the universe inference?

Show answer

The universe does not closely resemble a house, and we have never seen a universe made.

Mission 1

Hume: weak analogy, part and whole

  • Part to whole: design is one cause we see at work in one small part of nature. Can we judge the whole from so small a part? (Part II: "the great disproportion").
  • No experience of world-making: we have seen ships and cities made, never a world (Part II).
  • Other analogies fit as well: "The world plainly resembles more an animal or a vegetable, than it does a watch or a knitting-loom" (Part VII). Then its cause would be growth or generation, not design.
  • Mind needs explaining too: if order needs a cause, so does the order in a designer's mind (Part IV).
  • Your spec: "problems with analogies".
Mission 1

Hume: which designer?

  • Even if the world was designed, we cannot tell the designer is the God of classical theism (one, infinite, perfect, good). From Part V:
  • Finite effect, finite cause: a finite world proves no infinite designer.
  • Apprentice god: the world might be "the first rude essay of some infant deity, who afterwards abandoned it".
  • "Stupid mechanic": like a ship's carpenter who copied an art improved over ages of trial and error.
  • Plurality of gods: "A great number of men join in building a house or ship"; why not many gods for a world?
  • Absent god: the world might be the work of a "superannuated deity", now dead or gone. This is close to deism: a God who made the world and no longer acts in it.
  • Your spec terms: designer not necessarily the God of classical theism; apprentice god; plurality of gods; absent god.
Quick check
?"Ships are built by many workers, so a universe might have been made by several gods." Which of Hume's criticisms is this?
Mission 1

Chance: the Epicurean hypothesis

  • Hume, Dialogues Part VIII: suppose matter is finite and time is infinite.
  • Then every possible arrangement of matter will occur, many times over. A stable, ordered world like ours would appear by chance sooner or later (after the ancient philosopher Epicurus, 341–270 BCE).
  • So order need not come from a mind.
  • Replies: it needs infinite time and finite matter, which current cosmology does not clearly support (the observable universe has a finite age); order that is stable and life-permitting is still very improbable (Swinburne, fine-tuning).
Mission 1

Defending the argument against Hume

  • Paley wrote after Hume (1802; Dialogues 1779). Many read Paley's argument as an inference to the best explanation, not a strict analogy: fitted parts are best explained by design, whatever the likeness to a house.
  • Simplicity: one God is a simpler explanation than many gods (Ockham's razor; Swinburne).
  • Finite world, infinite God: the argument need not prove every divine attribute; other arguments and revelation add more (cumulative case).
  • Hume's own Philo finally allows that the cause of order "probably bear[s] some remote analogy to human intelligence" (Part XII).
Mission 1Read the source

Darwin: natural selection

Charles Darwin (1809–1882), On the Origin of Species (1859), chapter VI:

> "To suppose that the eye … could have been formed by natural selection, seems, I freely confess, absurd in the highest possible degree. Yet reason tells me, that if numerous gradations from a perfect and complex eye to one very imperfect and simple, each grade being useful to its possessor, can be shown to exist …"

  • Natural selection: living things vary; variations are inherited; those better suited to survive leave more offspring. Over vast time, small useful changes add up.
  • Paley's best example, the eye, can be explained without a designer: by many small, useful steps.

Q1 What condition does Darwin set for the eye to have evolved?

Show answer

A series of gradations, each one useful to the creature that has it.

Q2 Why is this a challenge to Paley?

Show answer

It explains fitted parts without an intelligent maker.

Mission 1

Dawkins: the blind watchmaker

  • Richard Dawkins (b. 1941), The Blind Watchmaker (1986), chapter 1.
  • Paley was right that living things look designed and need explaining; he was wrong about the explanation (paraphrase).
  • Natural selection is a "blind watchmaker": an unconscious process with no purpose or foresight.
  • Not pure chance: cumulative selection keeps each small useful change and builds on it, step by step.
  • So the "chance" objection to evolution misreads it; and design is not the only alternative to chance.
Mission 1Diversity

Religious responses to evolution

  • Theistic evolution: God creates through evolution. Frederick Temple (1884): God made things make themselves (paraphrase). Tennant's wider teleology.
  • Catholic teaching: Pius XII, Humani Generis (1950), allowed study of the body's evolution; the soul is created directly by God. John Paul II (1996) called evolution "more than a hypothesis".
  • Intelligent design: Michael Behe, Darwin's Black Box (1996): some structures are irreducibly complex and could not evolve step by step. Most biologists reject this.
  • Creationism: some Christians (and some Muslims and Jews) read the creation accounts literally and reject common descent.
  • Moving the argument: evolution needs stable laws and a fine-tuned universe; design arguments shift there (Swinburne; fine-tuning).

Mission 1

What can an argument from observation show?

  • Probability, not proof: an a posteriori argument can make a designer more or less likely; it cannot prove one.
  • Alternative interpretations: the same order can be read as design, as chance, as evolution by natural selection (Charles Darwin, 1859: living things with useful traits survive and pass them on, so over long ages they come to look designed), or as the work of a deist God who set up the laws and left.
  • Dysteleology: suffering and waste in nature (Hume, Parts X–XI) suggest a designer who is not all-good, or none.
  • A posteriori vs a priori: observation arguments are open to new evidence, which makes them modest but also revisable; a priori arguments promise certainty but may only define words.
Mission 1Evaluate

Can the design argument survive Hume and Darwin?

  • Claim: The design argument still gives good reason to believe in God.
  • For: Order and purpose are real features of the world; design is a natural explanation (Paley).
  • For: Fine-tuning and the laws of nature are not explained by evolution (Swinburne, Tennant).
  • Against: The analogy is weak, and leads at best to a limited, possibly plural or absent designer (Hume).
  • Against: Natural selection explains biological design without a designer (Darwin).
  • Against: Suffering in nature fits poorly with a good designer.
  • Judgement criteria: Is analogy or best explanation the right reading of Paley? Does moving to the laws of nature escape Darwin? How simple is "God" as an explanation? Can the argument reach the God of classical theism?

Mission 2

Mission 2: If science can explain how the universe and life developed, is anything left for a creator to do?

Make a guess: If science can explain how the universe and life developed, is anything left for a creator to do?

Mission 2

The Big Bang

  • The universe is expanding from an extremely hot, dense early state, about 13.8 billion years ago.
  • 1927–31: Georges Lemaître (1894–1966), Belgian physicist and Catholic priest, proposes expansion from a "primeval atom".
  • 1929: Edwin Hubble: distant galaxies are moving away; the further, the faster.
  • 1965: the cosmic microwave background, the cooled afterglow of the early universe, is detected (Penzias and Wilson).
  • The name "Big Bang" was coined by its critic Fred Hoyle on BBC radio in 1949.
  • The theory describes how the universe developed from its early state. It does not say what, if anything, caused that state.
Mission 2

Does the Big Bang help or hurt belief?

  • As an alternative explanation: the universe's development is explained by physics from its early state; no First Cause is needed to explain what happens next. This weakens inductive arguments that start from motion and change.
  • As support: a beginning seems to fit creation. William Lane Craig's Kalam argument cites it as evidence that the universe began to exist.
  • Catholic response: Pope Pius XII (1951) welcomed the theory as consistent with creation; Lemaître warned against using physics to prove theology.
  • Either way: science describes the universe from its earliest moments; whether there is a reason why it exists at all is a further question (Aquinas' Third Way).

Mission 2

Darwin: evolution by natural selection

  • Charles Darwin (1809–1882), On the Origin of Species (1859); humans included in The Descent of Man (1871).
  • Variation: individuals of a species differ.
  • Inheritance: many differences are passed on.
  • Struggle: more offspring are born than can survive.
  • Selection: those better suited to their environment survive and reproduce more.
  • Over vast time, small changes add up to new species: all life shares common descent.
  • This explains the appearance of design (as in Paley's watch) without a designer.
  • It challenges a literal reading of Genesis 1–2 and the idea of humans as a separate creation.
Mission 2Read the source

Darwin's closing sentence

On the Origin of Species, 1st edition (1859), final sentence (opening):

> "There is grandeur in this view of life, with its several powers, having been originally breathed into a few forms or into one;"

2nd edition (1860) onwards:

> "having been originally breathed by the Creator into a few forms or into one;"

  • Darwin later called himself an agnostic (letter, 1879).

Q1 What did Darwin add in 1860?

Show answer

The words "by the Creator".

Q2 Does natural selection, as Darwin states it here, rule out a creator?

Show answer

Not by itself: he describes life's origin as breathed by the Creator, and evolution as what followed.

Mission 2

Dawkins: the blind watchmaker

  • Richard Dawkins (b. 1941): The Blind Watchmaker (1986); The God Delusion (2006).
  • Natural selection is a blind watchmaker: it produces the appearance of design with no foresight or purpose.
  • It is not chance: tiny variations are selected cumulatively, step by step, so complex organs build up gradually.
  • A designer would be even more complex than what it explains: so "who designed the designer?"
  • Darwin, Dawkins says, made it possible to be an intellectually fulfilled atheist (paraphrase, 1986).
  • Responses: many theists reply that evolution explains how life develops, not why a universe exists whose laws allow it.

Mission 2Evaluate

Do scientific origins make God unnecessary?

  • Claim: Scientific explanations of origins make belief in a creator unnecessary.
  • For: The Big Bang and evolution explain the universe's and life's development by natural causes.
  • For: Ockham's razor: do not add a cause that explains nothing extra. Dawkins: a designer is more complex than what it explains.
  • Against: Science starts from laws and an early state; it does not explain why anything exists (Aquinas).
  • Against: Many scientists see evolution as the way God creates; fine-tuning raises further questions.
  • Judgement criteria: Is "creator" a cause inside the chain of events, or the reason the chain exists? Is the scientific or the philosophical explanation more complete?
  • Spec issue: whether scientific explanations are more persuasive than philosophical ones.

A scientific alternative explains the evidence an inductive argument starts from, without claiming to settle whether God exists.

Mission 3: Can you prove God exists just by thinking carefully about what the word "God" means?

Make a guess: Can you prove God exists just by thinking carefully about what the word "God" means?

Mission 3

Arguing from a definition

  • Ontological from Greek on, "being": an argument about God's being from the concept of God.
  • A priori: it does not rely on experience; reasoning alone.
  • Deductive: if the premises are true and the logic valid, the conclusion must be true. A proof, if it works.
  • Compare cosmological and design arguments: a posteriori, inductive.
  • Analytic statement: true by the meaning of its words ("a bachelor is unmarried"). The argument claims "God exists" is analytic: existence belongs to the very concept of God.
Mission 3

Anselm: faith seeking understanding

  • Anselm (1033–1109), monk and abbot of Bec in Normandy; Archbishop of Canterbury from 1093.
  • Proslogion (1077–78): written as a prayer addressed to God.
  • First title: fides quaerens intellectum, "faith seeking understanding". Anselm: "I believe in order to understand" (Proslogion 1, paraphrase of credo ut intelligam).
  • He does not argue his way into faith: he seeks to understand what he already believes.
Mission 3Read the source

The fool and the definition

> "The fool has said in his heart, 'There is no God.'" (Psalm 14:1, WEBBE)

Anselm's definition of God, as Aquinas reports it (Summa Theologica I.2.1, reply to objection 2, English Dominican translation):

> "… something than which nothing greater can be thought …"

  • Latin: aliquid quo nihil maius cogitari possit. Often translated "that than which nothing greater can be conceived".
  • Not "the greatest thing that exists", but the greatest that can be thought.

Q1 Why does Anselm start with the fool?

Show answer

Even the fool understands the word "God"; so God exists at least in his understanding.

Q2 Why does "can be thought" matter?

Show answer

It defines God by what the mind can conceive, without first assuming God exists.

Mission 3

Proslogion 2: in the mind and in reality

  • 1. God is that than which nothing greater can be conceived.
  • 2. Even the fool understands this; so God exists in the understanding.
  • 3. To exist in reality as well as in the understanding is greater than to exist in the understanding alone.
  • 4. If God existed only in the understanding, we could conceive of something greater: the same being existing in reality.
  • 5. That is a contradiction: something greater than that than which nothing greater can be conceived.
  • 6. So God exists both in the understanding and in reality.
  • A reductio ad absurdum: assume the opposite and show it leads to contradiction.
  • Anselm's image (paraphrase): a painter has a picture in mind before painting it; once painted, it exists in both.
  • Your spec: "God as the greatest possible being (Proslogion 2)".

?In Proslogion 2, why does Anselm think God cannot exist only in the understanding?
Mission 3

Proslogion 3: God cannot be thought not to exist

  • A being that cannot be conceived not to exist is greater than one that can.
  • So if God could be conceived not to exist, God would not be that than which nothing greater can be conceived.
  • So God cannot even be conceived not to exist: God exists necessarily.
  • Everything else can be conceived not to exist; God alone cannot (paraphrase).
  • Some scholars (e.g. Norman Malcolm, the later screen) read Proslogion 3 as a second, stronger argument; others as a continuation of chapter 2.
  • Your spec: "God has necessary existence (Proslogion 3)".
Mission 3

Definitions of God: necessary existence and aseity

  • Contingent existence: could exist or not; depends on something else (you, a mountain, a star).
  • Necessary existence: cannot not exist.
  • Aseity (Latin a se, "from itself"): God's existence comes from God, not from anything else. God is self-existent and depends on nothing.
  • Classical theism: God is eternal, without beginning or end; nothing could bring God into or out of existence.
  • The ontological argument claims this definition shows God must exist.
Mission 3

Descartes: the supremely perfect being

  • René Descartes (1596–1650), Meditations on First Philosophy (1641), Meditation V (paraphrase).
  • God is a supremely perfect being. Existence is a perfection. So God has existence.
  • Triangle: having three angles equal to two right angles cannot be separated from the idea of a triangle. Existence cannot be separated from the idea of God.
  • Mountain and valley: we cannot think of a mountain without a valley. Descartes notes this does not show any mountain exists. But with God, what cannot be separated is existence itself.
  • He insists the necessity is in God, not imposed by his thinking.
  • Your spec: "concept of God as supremely perfect being; analogies of triangles and mountains/valleys".
Mission 3

Malcolm: God as unlimited being

  • Norman Malcolm (1911–1990), "Anselm's Ontological Arguments", Philosophical Review (1960).
  • He accepts that Proslogion 2 fails (existence is not a perfection), but defends Proslogion 3.
  • God is an unlimited being: God could not come into existence (that would need a cause) or cease to exist.
  • So: if God does not exist, God's existence is impossible; if God exists, God exists necessarily.
  • God's existence is not impossible (the concept is not self-contradictory).
  • So God's existence is necessary, not just possible.
  • Your spec: "God's existence as necessary rather than just possible".

Mission 4: If you imagine the perfect island, must it exist somewhere?

Make a guess: If you imagine the perfect island, must it exist somewhere?

Mission 4

Gaunilo: the lost island

  • Gaunilo, Benedictine monk of Marmoutier, near Tours: On Behalf of the Fool (c. 1079), a reply to Anselm.
  • Imagine a lost island, more excellent than all other lands (paraphrase).
  • By Anselm's logic: an island existing in reality is more excellent than one only in the mind; so the most excellent island must exist.
  • That is absurd. So the logic must be faulty.
  • A reductio of Anselm's reasoning: the same form of argument proves too much.
  • Your spec: "his analogy of the idea of the greatest island as a ridicule of St Anselm's logic".
Mission 4

Gaunilo: in the mind is not in reality

  • To have an idea "in the understanding" is not to know it exists. Many false or unreal things are in the mind too (paraphrase).
  • We cannot truly understand God: we know God by no likeness, kind or species.
  • So we must first show that the greatest being exists in reality; only then can we say it is greater than all.
  • Gaunilo was himself a believer: he defended the fool's right to an argument, not atheism.
  • Your spec: his rejection of a greatest possible being "that can be thought of as having separate existence outside of our minds".
Mission 4

Anselm replies

  • Anselm's Reply to Gaunilo: the argument applies only to "that than which nothing greater can be conceived". An island is not that (paraphrase).
  • Gaunilo changed the definition to "greater than all": that is a different concept.
  • Anselm: if anyone finds something else, apart from God, that fits the argument, he will find and give them the lost island (paraphrase).
  • Alvin Plantinga (b. 1932) adds: an island has no intrinsic maximum; however many palm trees or perfect beaches, one could always add more. Greatness in power, knowledge and goodness does have a maximum.
Quick check
?What is Plantinga's reply to Gaunilo's island?
Mission 4

Kant: existence is not a real predicate

  • Immanuel Kant (1724–1804), Critique of Pure Reason (1781), "On the Impossibility of an Ontological Proof of the Existence of God".
  • A predicate describes a subject: "The ball is red". A real (or determining) predicate adds something to the concept.
  • "Exists" adds nothing to the concept: it says only that something answers to the concept (paraphrase).
  • A hundred thalers (coins): a hundred real thalers contain no more than a hundred imagined ones. The difference is in my wallet, not in the concept.
  • So, Kant concludes, existence is not a perfection a perfect being must have, and Anselm (P2) and Descartes fail.
  • Your spec: "existence is not a determining predicate: it cannot be a property that an object can either possess or lack".
Mission 4

Kant: denying God is no contradiction

  • "A triangle has three angles" is analytic: deny the angles while keeping the triangle, and you contradict yourself.
  • But reject the triangle together with its angles, and there is no contradiction.
  • In the same way: "God does not exist" rejects God together with all God's predicates. Nothing is left to contradict (paraphrase).
  • So, for Kant, "God exists" cannot be an analytic truth. Every existence claim is synthetic: shown, if at all, by experience.
  • This answers Descartes' triangle analogy (see Mission 3).

Mission 4

Is necessary existence different?

  • Two later versions (see Mission 3): Norman Malcolm argued that an unlimited being's existence is either necessary or impossible, so if possible it is necessary; Alvin Plantinga argued that a maximally great being, if it is possible, exists in every possible world.
  • Malcolm (see Mission 3): Kant refutes Proslogion 2, but necessary existence is a real perfection; Proslogion 3 survives.
  • John Hick (1922–2012), Arguments for the Existence of God (1970): Malcolm confuses logical necessity (true by definition) with factual necessity (if God exists, God is eternal and depends on nothing). The concept only shows that if God exists, God exists necessarily.
  • Hume (Dialogues IX): whatever we conceive as existing we can conceive as not existing; no being's existence is logically necessary.
  • Plantinga's key premise ("possibly a maximally great being exists") can be met by a mirror argument: "possibly no maximally great being exists" gives the opposite conclusion.
Mission 4

Proof, faith and the value of the argument

  • Basis in thought: a priori; if sound, it would be a proof, not a probability (unlike cosmological and design arguments).
  • Does it justify belief? Few are persuaded by it alone; critics call it a play on words; defenders say its premises are as plausible as their denials (Plantinga).
  • Value for faith: Anselm wrote it as a prayer, from faith. Karl Barth (1886–1968), Anselm: Fides Quaerens Intellectum (1931): it is theology within faith, not a proof for outsiders.
  • For many believers it deepens understanding of who God is: the greatest conceivable, necessary being.
  • Reason and faith: Anselm (faith seeking understanding); Aquinas (reason from effects); Kant (reason cannot reach God beyond experience; God as a postulate of moral faith).
Mission 4Evaluate

Does the ontological argument succeed?

  • Claim: The ontological argument proves that God exists.
  • For: It is deductive: if its premises are true, its conclusion is certain, with no need for evidence.
  • For: Defenders say the critics miss its special subject: only the greatest conceivable being has necessary existence (Anselm's reply; later defenders such as Plantinga).
  • Against: Gaunilo: the same form proves absurd things (the perfect island).
  • Against: Kant: existence is not a real predicate; denying God's existence is no contradiction.
  • Judgement criteria: Is existence (or necessary existence) a predicate? Does the parody really match Anselm's form? Can a definition tell us about reality? Does the argument have value for faith even if it fails as proof?
Mission check

?Jo defines a "real unicorn" as "a unicorn that exists". She argues: "By definition, real unicorns exist, so there is at least one unicorn." A critic replies: "Your definition tells us what something would be like if it existed. Whether anything matches it is a separate question." The central question is whether existence can ever belong to a concept. Most critics say no; defenders say God's case is unique.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Hume: the analogy between the world and human artefacts is weak; at best the designer is finite, possibly many, unskilled or absent (Dialogues II, V, VII).
  • Chance (Part VIII) and, after 1859, natural selection offer explanations of order without a designer.
  • Responses: theistic evolution, design moved to the laws and fine-tuning, intelligent design (rejected by most biologists).

Mission 2

  • The Big Bang describes expansion from a hot, dense state (13.8 billion years); used both against and for belief in a creator.
  • Darwin's natural selection explains apparent design; Dawkins' blind watchmaker has no foresight.

Mission 3

  • The ontological argument is a priori and deductive: from the concept of God to God's existence.
  • Anselm (Proslogion 2): that than which nothing greater can be conceived must exist in reality, not just the mind. (Proslogion 3): it cannot be conceived not to exist.
  • Necessary existence and aseity: God's existence depends on nothing else.
  • Descartes (perfect being, triangle, mountain/valley); Malcolm (unlimited being: necessary or impossible).

Mission 4

  • Gaunilo: the same logic would prove a perfect island exists; Anselm replies that only the greatest conceivable being fits the argument (Plantinga: an island has no intrinsic maximum).
  • Kant: existence is not a real predicate (the hundred thalers); denying God's existence, with all God's predicates, is no contradiction.
  • Many philosophers reject it as a proof; for many believers it expresses faith seeking understanding.
