Crime & Punishment

This mini-lesson walks you through the Edexcel International GCSE topic Religious & Ethical Teachings about Crime and Punishment: why crime happens, the six aims of punishment, ideas of justice and forgiveness, the different types of punishment, and the debate over the death penalty.

You'll meet religious and non-religious views side by side, presented fairly. Answer the questions as you go and collect ⭐ stars. Press Start when you're ready.

Fair & balanced: religions and individuals hold a range of views on these issues. This lesson describes different positions accurately without telling you which is "right".

Key terms

The words to get right

Examiners reward precise use of these terms. Learn each definition:

  • Sin — an act that breaks a religious/moral law (e.g. against God's will).
  • Crime — an act that breaks the law of the state and is punishable by it.
  • Justice — bringing about what is fair and right, and giving people what they are due.
  • Punishment — something unpleasant done to a person because they have broken a law.
  • Forgiveness — choosing to stop feeling resentment toward a wrongdoer and to pardon them.

Watch out: not every sin is a crime (e.g. lying may be a sin but not illegal), and not every crime is a sin in every tradition. Keep the two ideas separate.

Causes of crime

Why does crime happen?

The spec asks you to consider reasons why people commit crime. Common causes include:

  • Poverty and need — some steal because they cannot meet basic needs.
  • Upbringing and environment — growing up around crime or without support.
  • Addiction — to drugs, alcohol or gambling.
  • Greed — wanting more money or possessions than one has.
  • Mental health — some crimes are linked to illness or distress.
  • Opposition to an unjust law — some break laws they see as wrong (e.g. peaceful protest).

Religious angle: many believers argue society shares responsibility — reducing poverty and helping addicts can prevent crime. Christians point to Jesus' concern for the poor; the Qur'an urges care for those in need; the Torah commands leaving harvest for the poor (Leviticus 19:9-10).

The aims of punishment

The six aims of punishment

When society punishes, it usually has one or more aims in mind. The Edexcel spec expects all six:

The aims of punishment

What each aim means

  • Retribution — the criminal should pay for what they did; the punishment should fit the crime.
  • Deterrence — punishment is meant to put people off committing crime, either the offender or others.
  • Protection — punishment keeps society safe by removing dangerous offenders (e.g. prison).
  • Reformation — punishment should change the offender so they don't reoffend (rehabilitation).
  • Reparation — the offender should make amends and repair the harm done (e.g. compensation, work).
  • Vindication — punishment shows the law must be respected, so justice is seen to be done.

Misconception: retribution is not the same as revenge. Revenge is personal and can be excessive; retribution is a measured, proportionate response decided by a fair legal system. The Torah phrase "an eye for an eye" (Exodus 21:24) is widely understood as a limit on punishment — no more than the harm caused — not a demand for it.

A spectrum of views

Looking back vs looking forward

Views on punishment often sit on a spectrum. At one end, the focus is on the crime (retribution); at the other, on the person and their future (reformation).

Religious range: Many Christians stress reformation and forgiveness ("love your enemies", Matthew 5:44), yet also accept the state's right to keep order (Romans 13). Islam emphasises justice and mercy together; Buddhism stresses compassion and the offender's chance to change. There is no single "religious view".

Types of punishment

Ways society punishes

Modern legal systems use a range of punishments, matched to the seriousness of the crime:

  • Prison (imprisonment) — removes freedom; aims mainly at protection and retribution, and can allow reformation.
  • Community service — unpaid work benefiting the community; strong on reparation and reformation.
  • Fines — paying money; a form of reparation and deterrence for less serious crimes.
  • Probation / curfews — supervision in the community rather than prison.
  • Corporal punishment — physical pain; legal in some countries, banned in many.
  • Capital punishment (the death penalty) — execution; the most serious and most debated.

Community service is often praised by faith groups because it lets the offender make amends and stay part of society, supporting reform rather than only punishing.

Forgiveness

Forgiveness and treating criminals

Forgiveness is central to many faiths. It does not always mean escaping punishment — a person can be forgiven and still face the consequences of their actions.

  • Christianity — Jesus taught forgiving "seventy times seven" (Matthew 18:22) and forgave those who crucified him (Luke 23:34). Many Christians therefore stress reform and mercy.
  • Islam — Allah is Ar-Rahman ("the Most Merciful"); the Qur'an praises those who forgive (Qur'an 42:40), while also upholding justice for victims.
  • Judaism — teaches teshuvah (repentance): the wrongdoer should repent, make amends and seek forgiveness.

Treatment of criminals: most traditions teach that prisoners keep their human dignity and should be treated humanely — Jesus said "I was in prison and you visited me" (Matthew 25:36). This shapes support for good prison conditions and rehabilitation.

The death penalty

Capital punishment: the debate

The death penalty (capital punishment) is executing someone for a very serious crime. Believers and non-believers are divided. Here are the main arguments on each side:

The death penalty

Religious & secular views

There is no single religious answer. The spec expects you to show this range:

  • Christianity — many oppose it, stressing the sanctity of life, forgiveness and "do not kill" (Exodus 20:13); the Roman Catholic Church now teaches it is "inadmissible". Some Christians accept it, citing Old Testament law and Romans 13 (the state may "bear the sword").
  • Islam — the Qur'an permits capital punishment for certain grave crimes but also praises those who show mercy and forgive; views among Muslims and Muslim-majority countries vary.
  • Judaism — the Torah lists it, but rabbinic law set such strict conditions that it became extremely rare; many Jews today oppose it.
  • Non-religious (Humanist) — most Humanists oppose it, valuing human life, the risk of executing the innocent, and evidence questioning its deterrent effect; some non-religious people support it on retribution or protection grounds.

Misconception: it is wrong to say "religion is for/against the death penalty". Each tradition contains a range of views, and people weigh justice, mercy and the sanctity of life differently.

Justice

Why justice matters to believers

Justice — fairness and giving people their due — is a core value across religions and in secular ethics alike:

  • Christianity — "Let justice roll on like a river" (Amos 5:24); believers are called to defend the weak.
  • Islam — "Stand out firmly for justice" (Qur'an 4:135), even against yourself or family; 'adl (justice) is a duty.
  • Judaism — "Justice, justice you shall pursue" (Deuteronomy 16:20).
  • Non-religious — justice is defended on grounds of fairness, human rights and the good of society.

Justice links the whole topic: a just system tries to give fair punishments, protect the innocent, and balance retribution with mercy and reform.

Exam skill

Writing a balanced answer

Higher-mark questions (e.g. "The death penalty is always wrong. Do you agree?") reward balance. A strong answer:

  • Gives arguments for and against, with reasons.
  • Uses religious teachings and named sources of authority.
  • Shows more than one religious view (they differ!).
  • Includes a non-religious view where relevant.
  • Reaches a justified conclusion that weighs the evidence.

Even-handed: present each view fairly and accurately. You may reach a conclusion, but you must show you understood the opposing case. Never dismiss or misrepresent a belief.

Recap

The essentials to know

Sin vs crime: breaks a religious/moral law vs breaks the law of the state.

Causes of crime: poverty, upbringing, addiction, greed, mental health, opposing unjust laws.

Six aims: retribution, deterrence, protection, reformation, reparation, vindication.

Types: prison, community service, fines, probation, (corporal), capital punishment.

Death penalty: for & against on both religious and secular grounds — no single view.

Key values: justice, forgiveness, mercy, sanctity of life, human dignity of prisoners.
