Mission 1

What is a right?

  • A right is a justified claim: something owed to you, which others have a duty to respect.
  • Natural (moral) rights: held because we are human, whether or not any law recognises them.
  • Legal rights: created and protected by a particular law or state.
  • Negative rights: to be left alone (life, liberty, free speech). Positive rights: to be given something (education, healthcare).
  • Human rights: rights claimed for every human being, everywhere.
  • The big debate: do rights come from nature (or God), from agreement, or only from law?
Mission 1

Hobbes: rights and the sovereign

  • Thomas Hobbes (1588–1679), Leviathan (1651).
  • Without government, in the state of nature, life is a war of all against all: "solitary, poor, nasty, brutish, and short" (ch. 13).
  • The right of nature: each person's liberty to preserve their own life by any means.
  • To escape fear, people make a social contract, handing power to an absolute sovereign who keeps the peace.
  • Subjects keep only what they cannot give away: chiefly the right to defend their own life. Otherwise, liberty is what the law leaves untouched.
  • Rights begin as self-preservation; security comes before freedom.
Mission 1

Locke: life, liberty and property

  • John Locke (1632–1704), Two Treatises of Government (1689).
  • The state of nature already has a law of nature, known by reason: "no one ought to harm another in his life, health, liberty, or possessions" (Second Treatise, §6).
  • People form governments by consent to protect these natural rights ("property" in his broad sense: life, liberty and estate).
  • A government that violates them breaks trust; the people may resist and replace it.
  • Locke grounds natural law in God as creator: humans are God's "workmanship", so no one may destroy another.
  • Influence: the American Declaration of Independence (1776): "life, liberty and the pursuit of happiness".
Mission 1

Paine: the rights of man

  • Thomas Paine (1737–1809), Rights of Man (1791–92), defending the French Revolution against Edmund Burke's Reflections on the Revolution in France (1790).
  • Burke: rights are an inheritance, handed down through a nation's traditions.
  • Paine: every generation has the same natural rights as the first humans; no generation can bind the next.
  • Civil rights (e.g. to property protected by courts) grow out of natural rights that individuals cannot secure alone.
  • Rights belong to all equally, by the "unity of man" (paraphrase); hereditary monarchy and aristocracy violate them.
  • He backed the French Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen (1789).

Mission 1Read the source

Bentham: "nonsense upon stilts"

Jeremy Bentham (1748–1832), Anarchical Fallacies (written c. 1796, on the French Declaration of 1789):

  • Natural rights are "simple nonsense"; natural and imprescriptible rights are rhetorical nonsense, "nonsense upon stilts" (abridged).
  • Rights are the "child of law": real rights are made by real laws, backed by real penalties (paraphrase).
  • Talk of natural rights is dangerous: it encourages people to resist any law they dislike.
  • Laws should be judged by utility: the greatest happiness of the greatest number.

Q1 For Bentham, where do all real rights come from?

Show answer

From the law of a state.

Q2 Why did he think natural-rights talk was harmful, not just mistaken?

Show answer

It invites rebellion against any law, undermining the security laws provide.

Mission 1

The Universal Declaration of Human Rights

  • Written after the Second World War and the Holocaust; adopted by the UN General Assembly on 10 December 1948 (48 for, none against, 8 abstentions).
  • Drafting committee chaired by Eleanor Roosevelt, with René Cassin, Peng Chun Chang and Charles Malik.
  • Article 1: "All human beings are born free and equal in dignity and rights."
  • 30 articles: civil and political (life, liberty, fair trial, religion, expression) and economic and social (work, education, social security).
  • Not itself a treaty, but led to binding covenants (1966), the European Convention on Human Rights (1950) and the UK Human Rights Act (1998).
  • It avoids naming a foundation (God or nature) so that people of many beliefs could sign.
Mission 1Diversity

Christian perspectives

  • Support: every person is made in God's image (Genesis 1:27): a basis for equal dignity. Christians such as Charles Malik and Jacques Maritain helped shape the UDHR.
  • Catholic teaching: John XXIII, Pacem in Terris (1963): every person has rights and duties that flow from human nature.
  • Biblical roots of justice: "Defend the weak, the poor, and the fatherless. Maintain the rights of the poor and oppressed." (Psalm 82:3, WEBBE)
  • Critiques: rights language can be individualistic, forgetting duties, community and the common good.
  • Some Christians object when claimed rights (e.g. to abortion) conflict with their reading of God's law; others champion rights as Christian love in public form.
  • Alasdair MacIntyre (1929–2025), After Virtue (1981): belief in natural rights is like belief in witches and unicorns (paraphrase): no good reason has been given for either.
Mission 1

Secular perspectives

  • Kantian: every rational being has dignity, not a price: rights protect persons as ends in themselves.
  • Contract: rights are what free and equal people would agree to (Hobbes, Locke, and later John Rawls, A Theory of Justice, 1971).
  • Utilitarian: rights are very useful rules for promoting happiness (Mill), not natural facts (Bentham).
  • Humanist: rights rest on shared human needs and capacities, without any need for God.
  • The capabilities approach (Amartya Sen, Martha Nussbaum): rights protect what people are actually able to do and be.
  • Secular and religious views often agree on the list of rights while disagreeing on their foundation.

Mission 1

Cultural and religious critiques

  • Cultural relativism: rights reflect Western, individualist values; American Anthropological Association statement (1947).
  • "Asian values": the Bangkok Declaration (1993) stressed national and regional particularities and duties to community.
  • Cairo Declaration on Human Rights in Islam (1990): rights affirmed but placed under the Shari'ah.
  • Marxist: Karl Marx, "On the Jewish Question" (1843): the "rights of man" protect the separated, self-interested individual.
  • Feminist: early rights thinking protected men in public life and ignored harm in the home.
  • Responses: the Vienna Declaration (1993) calls rights "universal, indivisible and interdependent"; many defenders argue that those claiming cultural exemption are often rulers, not the oppressed.
Mission 1

Rights applied: examples

  • Northern Ireland: the Good Friday (Belfast) Agreement (1998) made human rights central; the Northern Ireland Human Rights Commission (1999); a Bill of Rights for NI is still debated.
  • Religion v other rights: Eweida v UK (European Court of Human Rights, 2013): a British Airways worker's right to wear a small cross was upheld (Article 9).
  • Expression v equality: Lee v Ashers (UK Supreme Court, 2018): no one may be compelled to express a message they disagree with.
  • Life and autonomy: Nicklinson (UK Supreme Court, 2014): the ban on assisted suicide upheld; Parliament left to decide.
  • Rights often conflict: courts weigh them (e.g. proportionality tests).
  • Animal rights.
Mission 1Evaluate

Are human rights universal?

  • Claim: Human rights are universal: they apply to every person in every culture.
  • For: Common humanity: dignity (Kant), the image of God (Genesis 1:27), shared needs; nearly every state has signed rights treaties.
  • For: Relativism can shield abuse (e.g. FGC).
  • Against: The lists reflect Western, liberal history (Cairo, Bangkok); Bentham: no natural rights at all; MacIntyre: no shared foundation.
  • Against: Rights clash (religion v equality), so applying them always involves judgement.
  • Judgement criteria: Universal in foundation or only in agreement? Who decides between conflicting rights? Can a right be universal if its basis is disputed?
Mission check

Whether a right exists without law is the fault line running from Hobbes and Locke through Paine and Bentham to the UDHR.

Mission 2

Who counts morally?

  • Every answer about animals rests on one question: do animals have moral status (do they matter for their own sake)?
  • No direct status: René Descartes (1596–1650), Discourse on the Method (1637), Part V: animals are like machines, without minds.
  • Indirect duties only: Thomas Aquinas (c. 1225–1274) and Immanuel Kant (1724–1804, Lectures on Ethics): animals are for human use; cruelty is wrong mainly because it makes people cruel to humans.
  • Direct status through sentience: Jeremy Bentham (1748–1832), An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation (1789), ch. XVII:

> "The question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?"

  • Sentience = the capacity to feel pain and pleasure.
Mission 2Read the source

Dominion and stewardship

> "Be fruitful, multiply, fill the earth, and subdue it. Have dominion over the fish of the sea, over the birds of the sky, and over every living thing that moves on the earth." (Genesis 1:28, WEBBE)

> "The LORD God took the man, and put him into the garden of Eden to cultivate and keep it." (Genesis 2:15, WEBBE)

> "A righteous man respects the life of his animal, but the tender mercies of the wicked are cruel." (Proverbs 12:10, WEBBE)

  • After the Flood, animals are given as food: "Every moving thing that lives will be food for you." (Genesis 9:3)
  • Dominion: humans rule over animals. Stewardship: for many Christians, that rule is held in trust from God, who owns creation.

Q1 Which verse could a Christian use to defend eating meat?

Show answer

Genesis 9:3; also Genesis 1:28 on dominion.

Q2 Which verse limits how animals may be treated?

Show answer

Proverbs 12:10: the righteous care for their animals; Genesis 2:15: "keep" implies care.

Mission 2

Welfare or rights?

  • Animal welfare: animals may be used, but suffering must be kept to a minimum. Most UK law takes this view.
  • Peter Singer (b. 1946), Animal Liberation (1975), a preference utilitarian: equal interests deserve equal consideration, whatever the species. Favouring humans just for being human is speciesism (term coined by Richard Ryder, 1970). Singer does not rest his case on rights.
  • Tom Regan (1938–2017), The Case for Animal Rights (1983): every subject-of-a-life (a being with beliefs, desires, memory and a welfare of its own) has inherent value and rights. Rights cannot be traded for benefits: he calls for abolition of animal farming, hunting and experiments, not reform.
  • Andrew Linzey (b. 1952), Christianity and the Rights of Animals (1987): theos-rights. Animals have rights because God, their creator, has rights in them.

Mission 2

Can animals have rights like humans?

  • Thomas Hobbes (1588–1679), Leviathan (1651): rights are secured by a social contract; it is impossible to make a covenant with animals, which cannot understand or accept one (paraphrase, ch. 14).
  • Peter Carruthers (b. 1952), The Animals Issue (1992): a modern contract view; animals have no direct moral standing.
  • John Locke (1632–1704): natural rights belong to rational humans; yet in Some Thoughts concerning Education (1693) he warns that children who torment animals grow hard towards people.
  • Bentham called natural rights "nonsense upon stilts", but made suffering, not reason, the test of who counts.
  • The UDHR (1948) protects "all members of the human family" only. No international animal-rights treaty exists.
  • Debate: should rights rest on reason and reciprocity (only humans) or sentience (many animals)?
Mission 2Evaluate

Should animals have rights, or is welfare enough?

  • Claim: Animals should have rights, not just welfare protection.
  • For: Sentient animals have interests of their own (Bentham); subjects-of-a-life have inherent value (Regan).
  • For: Welfare law still permits killing and confinement; only rights would stop it. Linzey: God's rights in creatures demand respect.
  • Against: Contract thinkers argue that rights need reason and reciprocity; animals cannot bear duties.
  • This is the contract view of Hobbes and Carruthers.
  • Against: Equal consideration of interests can protect animals without rights language (Singer); rights would forbid even life-saving research. Catholic teaching gives humans duties of care, not animal rights.
  • Judgement criteria: What grounds moral status (reason, sentience, being a subject-of-a-life, God's creation)? Must rights be reciprocal? Which approach protects animals better in practice?
Mission check

The verdict on any use of animals follows from a prior view of moral status. A later mission: animals in science, cloning and organ transplants.

Mission 3

The traditional Christian framework

  • Traditional Christian teaching: sex belongs within marriage, between one man and one woman, for life.
  • Two purposes of sex in marriage: unitive (binding the couple in love) and procreative (open to children).
  • So the tradition counts as wrong: sex before marriage (fornication), sex with someone other than your spouse (adultery), and same-sex sexual acts.
  • Catholic teaching sets this out in the Catechism of the Catholic Church (1992): e.g. §2353 (fornication), §2380–2381 (adultery), §2357–2359 (homosexuality).
  • Many Protestant and Orthodox Christians have held the same framework, from different sources (scripture first, or scripture with tradition).
  • The question for this mission: what happens when Christians re-read that framework?

Marriage and faithfulness

> "Therefore a man will leave his father and his mother, and will join with his wife, and they will be one flesh." (Genesis 2:24, WEBBE)

> "You shall not commit adultery." (Exodus 20:14, WEBBE)

> "Let marriage be held in honour amongst all, and let the bed be undefiled; but God will judge the sexually immoral and adulterers." (Hebrews 13:4, WEBBE)

  • Jesus quotes Genesis 2:24 on marriage (Mark 10:7–9) and widens adultery to lustful looking (Matthew 5:28).

Mission 3Diversity

Same-sex relationships: a spectrum

  • Catholic: same-sex attraction is not itself sinful, though the Catechism calls the inclination "objectively disordered" (§2358); same-sex acts cannot be approved; people must be treated with "respect, compassion, and sensitivity" (Catechism §2358). Fiducia Supplicans (2023) allows short, non-liturgical blessings of couples, including same-sex couples, without approving the union.
  • Many evangelicals and the Orthodox: same-sex acts are against scripture (Leviticus 18:22; Romans 1:26–27); some stress pastoral care and celibacy.
  • Church of England: does not marry same-sex couples; Prayers of Love and Faith (commended 2023) can be used to pray for same-sex couples.
  • Churches that marry same-sex couples: Quakers in Britain (decision 2009), Scottish Episcopal Church (2017), Methodist Church in Britain (2021), Church of Scotland (2022, for ministers who choose to).
  • Secular perspectives: humanists and most liberal secular ethics: consenting adult relationships are a private good; equal treatment is a matter of justice and rights.
  • Spec: "considering the spectrum of religious and secular perspectives".
Mission 3

Contraception and childlessness

  • Lambeth Conference 1930 (Resolution 15): Anglican bishops accept contraception where there is a morally sound reason. Most Protestant churches now leave it to a couple's conscience.
  • Pope Paul VI, Humanae Vitae (1968): every marital act must stay open to life (§11); artificial contraception is excluded (§14); natural family planning, using infertile periods, is allowed (§16).
  • Many Catholics in Britain and elsewhere do not follow §14: a gap between official teaching and practice.
  • Secular view: contraception gives control over fertility, protects health, and was central to women's equality (see Mission 4).
Mission 3Diversity

Beyond Christianity

  • Islam: sex only within marriage (nikah): "And come not near unto adultery. Lo! it is an abomination and an evil way." (Qur'an 17:32, Pickthall). Mainstream Sunni and Shi'a scholars forbid same-sex acts, citing the story of Lut (e.g. 7:80–81); some progressive Muslim groups dissent. Many scholars permit contraception, citing hadith on withdrawal ('azl).
  • Judaism: sex is good and a duty within marriage; the first command is "Be fruitful, and multiply" (Genesis 1:28, JPS 1917). Orthodox: Leviticus 18:22 binding; contraception often allowed for women with rabbinic advice. Reform and Liberal: many rabbis officiate at same-sex weddings; Liberal Judaism in Britain was an early supporter.
  • Pattern: in each tradition the split falls between those who treat the law as fixed and those who re-read it through ethical principle.
Mission 3

How teachings changed

  • Re-reading scripture: e.g. Leviticus as part of a holiness code for ancient Israel; Romans 1 about idolatry and excess, not loving partnerships (contested by traditionalists).
  • New knowledge: psychology treats sexual orientation as a stable part of identity, not a chosen vice.
  • Experience: Christians knowing gay and lesbian Christians in faithful partnerships.
  • Institutions decide: Church of England House of Bishops, Issues in Human Sexuality (1991): same-sex partnerships fall short of the ideal, but lay people who enter them in conscience may be welcomed; clergy may not.
  • Then: Lambeth 1998 Resolution I.10 (homosexual practice "incompatible with Scripture"); Living in Love and Faith (2020); Prayers of Love and Faith (2023).

Mission 3

Secularism and the law

  • Secularism: religion's influence on law and public life declines; law rests on consent, harm and equality.
  • Sexual Offences Act 1967: male homosexual acts partly decriminalised in England and Wales (Scotland 1980; Northern Ireland 1982).
  • Civil Partnership Act 2004; Marriage (Same Sex Couples) Act 2013 (England and Wales; first weddings 2014); Scotland 2014; Northern Ireland 2020.
  • The 2013 Act protects religious freedom: no religious body must conduct same-sex weddings.
  • The contraceptive pill on the NHS from 1961 (at first for married women); no-fault divorce in England and Wales from 2022, so adultery need no longer be alleged.
  • Debate: did secular change drive church change, or did Christians help drive the law (e.g. some church leaders backed the 1957 Wolfenden Report)?

Mission 4

What is gender justice?

  • Gender justice: equal dignity, rights and opportunities regardless of sex or gender, and an end to harm done because of them.
  • Mary Wollstonecraft, A Vindication of the Rights of Woman (1792): women are rational beings who deserve the same education and rights as men.
  • J. S. Mill, The Subjection of Women (1869): the legal subordination of wives is wrong in itself and holds back human progress.
  • Milestones: votes for some women (1918) and on equal terms (1928); Equal Pay Act 1970; Sex Discrimination Act 1975 (Northern Ireland Order 1976).
  • CEDAW (UN Convention on the Elimination of All Forms of Discrimination against Women, 1979): the international "bill of rights" for women.
  • Gender justice links to the general theory of rights in Mission 1.
Mission 4Read the source

Equal and ordered?

> "God created man in his own image. In God's image he created him; male and female he created them." (Genesis 1:27, WEBBE)

> "There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free man, there is neither male nor female; for you are all one in Christ Jesus." (Galatians 3:28, WEBBE)

> "Wives, be subject to your own husbands, as to the Lord." (Ephesians 5:22, WEBBE)

  • Ephesians 5 also opens with "subjecting yourselves to one another" (5:21) and tells husbands to love their wives "even as Christ also loved the assembly and gave himself up for her" (5:25).

Mission 4Diversity

Christian views on women's roles

  • Complementarian: equal dignity, different roles in family and church. Catholic teaching reserves priesthood to men (Ordinatio Sacerdotalis, 1994) while affirming women's "genius" (Mulieris Dignitatem, 1988); many evangelicals restrict church leadership to men (1 Timothy 2:12).
  • Egalitarian: no role is closed to women. Jesus' women disciples (Luke 8:1–3); Mary Magdalene first witness of the resurrection (John 20:18); Junia "notable amongst the apostles" (Romans 16:7).
  • In Ireland: Presbyterian Church in Ireland ordains women ministers (decision 1973; first ordination 1976); Church of Ireland ordained women priests (1990) and its first woman bishop (2013).
  • Church of England: women priests 1994; women bishops approved 2014 (first consecrated 2015).
  • Both sides cite scripture; the dispute is how to weigh texts against each other and against their cultural setting.

Mission 4

Marriage and parenthood rights

  • Marriage: same-sex marriage in Northern Ireland from 2020, following the Northern Ireland (Executive Formation etc) Act 2019; opposite-sex civil partnerships too.
  • No religious body must conduct same-sex marriages.
  • Parenthood: same-sex couples may adopt (England and Wales from 2005; Northern Ireland after a 2013 court ruling). The Human Fertilisation and Embryology Act 2008 lets two women both be legal parents.
  • Supporters: the right to found a family (UDHR Article 16) belongs to all; what children need is loving, stable parents.
  • Catholic and many other Christians: a child has a right to a mother and a father where possible; most Catholic adoption agencies in England closed or left Church control after the 2007 equality regulations.
  • Rights in tension: Lee v Ashers Baking Co (UK Supreme Court, 2018): a Belfast bakery could refuse to ice a pro-same-sex-marriage slogan; the refusal was to the message, not the customer.
Mission 4

Female genital cutting (FGC)

  • FGC (also called female genital mutilation, FGM): cutting or removing external female genitalia for non-medical reasons, usually on girls (WHO definition, paraphrase).
  • No health benefits; causes pain, infection, lifelong harm in childbirth and sexual health.
  • Found in parts of Africa, the Middle East and Asia and in diaspora communities; it predates Christianity and Islam and is practised by Muslims, Christians and others.
  • Neither the Bible nor the Qur'an requires it. Some classical Muslim jurists (notably in the Shafi'i school) held a form of it obligatory or recommended, citing disputed hadith; many Muslim scholars today, and Christian leaders, condemn it, while some local religious teachers still defend it.
  • Law: illegal in the UK (FGM Act 2003, covering Northern Ireland; Scotland 2005); FGM Protection Orders since 2015. UN General Assembly called for a worldwide ban (2012).
  • Rights violated: bodily integrity, health, the rights of the child.
  • The term "cutting" is used by some as less judgemental; "mutilation" by others to name the harm.
  • Cultural relativism: the view that moral standards are set within each culture, so outsiders should not judge its practices. Universalism: some rights hold everywhere, whatever the culture (more in Mission 1).
Mission 4

Honour killings

  • Honour killing: the murder of someone, usually a woman or girl, by family members who believe she has brought shame on the family (e.g. refusing a forced marriage, a disapproved relationship).
  • Not taught by any religion's scriptures; it occurs in some communities of several religions and none. Religious leaders widely condemn it.
  • Islamic teaching on the sanctity of life: "whosoever killeth a human being … it shall be as if he had killed all mankind" (Qur'an 5:32, Pickthall). Christian: "You shall not murder." (Exodus 20:13, WEBBE)
  • UK law treats it as murder; forced marriage is a crime in England and Wales (2014) and Northern Ireland (2015).
  • Feminist analysis: "honour" as control of women's sexuality and choices by the family, a sign of patriarchy.

Mission 4

Feminist views

  • Liberal feminism (Wollstonecraft, Mill): equal legal and political rights; reform within existing institutions.
  • Radical feminism: inequality comes from patriarchy, a system of male power running through family, sexuality, religion and law. Kate Millett, Sexual Politics (1970); slogan "the personal is political".
  • On sex: some feminists (e.g. Catharine MacKinnon) see pornography as subordination; others defend sexual freedom as liberation.
  • Ethics of care: Carol Gilligan, In a Different Voice (1982): moral thinking built on relationships and care, not only rules and rights.
  • Feminist views of contraception: control of fertility as the foundation of women's freedom (see Mission 3).
Mission 4

Feminist theology

  • Mary Daly (1928–2010), Beyond God the Father (1973): "If God is male, then the male is God." She left Christianity as irredeemably patriarchal (post-Christian feminism).
  • Rosemary Radford Ruether (1936–2022), Sexism and God-Talk (1983): whatever denies the full humanity of women cannot be truly redemptive (paraphrase): a test for reading tradition.
  • Elisabeth Schüssler Fiorenza (b. 1938), In Memory of Her (1983): a "hermeneutics of suspicion": read scripture alert to its male authors, and recover women's hidden roles.
  • "New feminism" (John Paul II, Evangelium Vitae, 1995, §99): women's equal dignity expressed through distinctive gifts; critics call this complementarianism renamed.

Mission 4Evaluate

Are religion and women's rights in conflict?

  • Claim: Religion is a barrier to gender justice.
  • For: Patriarchal texts and structures (e.g. male-only priesthood; Ephesians 5:22 used to justify control); religious cover claimed for FGC and honour.
  • For: Daly: a male God legitimises male power.
  • Against: Genesis 1:27 and Galatians 3:28 ground equal dignity; Christians helped lead suffrage and anti-FGC campaigns.
  • Against: FGC and honour killings are cultural practices that most religious authorities today condemn.
  • Judgement criteria: Distinguish religion from culture; which texts and traditions count; whose voices (including religious women's) are heard; universal rights v respect for cultures (see Mission 1).

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Hobbes: rights begin as self-preservation, traded for security; Locke: natural rights to life, liberty and property limit government; Paine: equal natural rights in every generation; Bentham: natural rights are "nonsense upon stilts".
  • The UDHR (1948) lists universal rights without naming their foundation; Christians and secular thinkers support and critique rights from different starting points.
  • Cultural and religious critiques (relativism, Bangkok 1993, Cairo 1990) challenge universality; courts must weigh rights that conflict.

Mission 2

  • Views on animals depend on moral status: none (Descartes), indirect (Aquinas, Kant), sentience (Bentham, Singer), rights as subjects-of-a-life (Regan) or theos-rights (Linzey).
  • Contract theories of rights (Hobbes, Carruthers) exclude animals; Bentham's test of suffering includes them; the UDHR is for humans only.

Mission 3

  • Traditional Christian teaching: sex within lifelong marriage of a man and a woman, unitive and procreative; churches now range from that view to celebrating same-sex marriage.
  • Change came through re-reading scripture, new knowledge, experience, institutions (Humanae Vitae 1968; Issues in Human Sexuality 1991) and a more secular law.

Mission 4

  • Gender justice: equal dignity and rights; from Wollstonecraft and Mill to CEDAW; marriage and parenthood rights now extend to same-sex couples in NI, with religious freedom protected.
  • FGC and honour killings violate rights; neither is required by the Bible or the Qur'an, and most religious leaders today condemn both.
  • Feminist views: liberal, radical (patriarchy), and theological (Daly, Ruether, Schüssler Fiorenza), with "new feminism" as a Catholic alternative.
