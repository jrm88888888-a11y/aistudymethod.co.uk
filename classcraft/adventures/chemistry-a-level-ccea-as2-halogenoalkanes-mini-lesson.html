Mission 1

Mission 1: How does warm sodium hydroxide turn a bromoalkane into an alcohol?

Make a guess: How does warm sodium hydroxide turn a bromoalkane into an alcohol?

Mission 1

Halogenoalkanes

  • Halogen X is more electronegative than carbon: the C–X bond is polar, Cδ+–Xδ−.
  • The δ+ carbon can be attacked by electron-rich species.
  • Classify by the carbon bonded to X: bonded to 1, 2 or 3 other carbons.
  • Primary: CH3CH2CH2Br · secondary: CH3CHBrCH3 · tertiary: (CH3)3CBr.
Mission 1

Nucleophiles

  • A nucleophile is an electron-pair donor.
  • It donates both electrons of a new covalent bond, usually from a lone pair.
  • Examples: OH⁻, CN⁻, NH3, H2O. No charge needed: NH3 and H2O are neutral.
  • "Nucleus-loving": it is attracted to a δ+ atom.
Quick check
?Which set contains only species that can act as nucleophiles?
Mission 1

Heterolytic fission and curly arrows

  • Heterolytic fission: both bonding electrons go to one atom, so ions form.
  • The more electronegative atom takes the pair and becomes negative: C–Br → C⁺ + Br⁻.
  • A curly arrow (two barbs) shows an electron pair moving.
  • Forming a bond: arrow from a lone pair (or a bond) to the atom it bonds to.
  • Breaking a bond: arrow from the bond to the atom that takes the pair.
Mission 1

The mechanism, step by step

  • Bromoethane + warm aqueous NaOH (or KOH): CH3CH2Br + OH⁻ → CH3CH2OH + Br⁻
  • 1 Draw the dipole: Cδ+–Brδ−.
  • 2 Arrow from a lone pair on O of OH⁻ to the δ+ C: the new C–O bond.
  • 3 Arrow from the C–Br bond to Br: Br leaves as Br⁻, taking the pair.
  • Products: ethanol and bromide ion.
  • Name: nucleophilic substitution. With OH⁻ it is also called hydrolysis.
Quick check
?Hydroxide ions react with 1-chloropropane: CH3CH2CH2Cl + OH⁻ → CH3CH2CH2OH + Cl⁻. Which describes the curly arrows correctly?
Mission 1

Which reacts fastest? Bond enthalpy

  • The C–X bond must break, so its strength controls the rate.
  • Bond enthalpies: C–F 467 · C–Cl 346 · C–Br 290 · C–I 228 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Rate: iodo > bromo > chloro (fluoro hardly reacts).
  • Polarity predicts the opposite order (C–F most polar), so bond enthalpy wins.
Mission 1

Comparing rates with silver nitrate

  • Add the halogenoalkane to aqueous silver nitrate in ethanol (ethanol dissolves both). Warm all tubes equally, e.g. 50 °C water bath.
  • Water is the nucleophile: R–X + H2O → R–OH + H⁺ + X⁻
  • X⁻ meets Ag⁺: Ag⁺(aq) + X⁻(aq) → AgX(s). Time how long until the precipitate appears.
  • Precipitates: AgI yellow (first) · AgBr cream · AgCl white (last).
Quick check
?1-chlorobutane, 1-bromobutane and 1-iodobutane are each added to aqueous silver nitrate in ethanol at 50 °C. Which tube shows a precipitate first, and what colour is it?
Mission 1

Cyanide: a longer chain

  • Reagent: KCN in ethanol (with a little water), heat under reflux.
  • CH3CH2Br + CN⁻ → CH3CH2CN + Br⁻. Product: propanenitrile.
  • Arrow from the lone pair on C of CN⁻ to the δ+ carbon; arrow from C–Br to Br.
  • A nitrile forms, and the carbon chain grows by one.
  • KCN is very toxic.
Mission 1

Ammonia: making amines

  • Reagent: excess ammonia in ethanol, heated in a sealed tube (under pressure).
  • Step 1: lone pair on N of NH3 → δ+ C; C–Br → Br. Forms CH3CH2NH3⁺ + Br⁻.
  • Step 2: a second NH3 removes H⁺ from N: lone pair on N → H; N–H bond → N.
  • Overall: CH3CH2Br + 2NH3 → CH3CH2NH2 + NH4Br. Product: ethylamine, a primary amine.
  • Learn the reagent, conditions and product; the arrows show how the amine forms.
Quick check
?Bromoethane is heated with excess ammonia in ethanol: CH3CH2Br + 2NH3 → CH3CH2NH2 + NH4Br. What does the second NH3 molecule do?
Mission 1

In the lab: making an alcohol

  • Mix the halogenoalkane with aqueous NaOH, add anti-bumping granules.
  • Heat under reflux: hot for a long time, with vapours condensed back so nothing escapes.
  • Then separate the alcohol, e.g. by distillation.
  • E.g. 1-bromobutane → butan-1-ol.
Mission check

?Iodoethane, CH3CH2I, is warmed with aqueous sodium hydroxide, and ethanol forms. Which statement about the electron pairs is correct?

OH⁻ donates a lone pair to the δ+ carbon; the C–X pair leaves with the halogen as X⁻. That is how the warm alkali turns a bromoalkane into an alcohol.

Mission 2

Mission 2: What does acid do to an alcohol that lets its OH group break away?

Make a guess: What does acid do to an alcohol that lets its OH group break away?

Mission 2

Why acid lets the OH go

  • OH⁻ is a strong base: it is a poor leaving group. The C–O bond will not break on its own.
  • H⁺ bonds to a lone pair on the O: –OH becomes –OH2⁺.
  • Now the C–O bond breaks and a neutral, stable H2O molecule leaves.
  • Then either:
  • a neighbouring C loses H⁺ → alkene (elimination)
  • a halide ion bonds to the C → halogenoalkane (substitution)
Mission 2

OH → halogen: halide ions plus acid

  • Alcohol + hydrogen halide → halogenoalkane + water.
  • CH3CH2OH + HBr → CH3CH2Br + H2O
  • HBr is made in the flask: NaBr + H2SO4 → NaHSO4 + HBr.
  • Or use hydrobromic acid. Test tube: warm; a dense oily layer forms.
Mission 2

Phosphorus halides

  • PCl5, room temperature: CH3CH2OH + PCl5 → CH3CH2Cl + POCl3 + HCl
  • Steamy fumes of HCl: a test for an O–H group (alcohols, acids, water all give it).
  • The HCl turns moist blue litmus red; white smoke with ammonia.
Mission 2

Alcohols with sodium

  • 2CH3CH2OH + 2Na → 2CH3CH2O⁻Na⁺ + H2
  • Product: sodium ethoxide.
  • Sodium sinks, fizzes steadily and slowly dissolves. Gas pops with a lighted splint.
  • Calmer than sodium with water.
  • Here the O–H bond breaks, not the C–O bond.
Mission 2

Making and purifying a halogenoalkane

  • 1. Reflux the alcohol with NaBr and conc H2SO4 (e.g. butan-1-ol → 1-bromobutane).
  • 2. Distil off the crude product.
  • 3. Separate in a separating funnel. Check which layer is organic: 1-bromobutane is below the water; 2-chloro-2-methylpropane floats above the acid.
  • 4. Wash with NaHCO3(aq) to remove acid. Vent: CO2 forms.
  • 5. Dry with an anhydrous salt (e.g. Na2SO4) until the liquid is clear.
  • 6. Distil, collecting near the boiling point (1-bromobutane 102 °C; 2-chloro-2-methylpropane 51 °C).
Quick check
?Propan-2-ol is warmed with potassium bromide and sulfuric acid. What is the main organic product?
Quick check
?A few drops of an unknown colourless liquid are added to solid PCl5. Steamy fumes appear. What can you conclude?
Mission check

?Butan-1-ol is reacted separately with sodium, with hydrogen bromide and with phosphorus pentachloride. In which reaction does the O–H bond break while the C–O bond stays intact?

Sodium breaks O–H. Acid (HBr) makes the OH leave as water, so C–O breaks and a halogen takes its place.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Two bromoalkanes share the formula C4H9Br. Why do they swap Br for OH by different routes?

Make a guess: Two bromoalkanes share the formula C4H9Br. Why do they swap Br for OH by different routes?

Mission 3

Same formula, different carbon

  • 1-bromobutane, CH3CH2CH2CH2Br: primary.
  • 2-bromo-2-methylpropane, (CH3)3CBr: tertiary.
  • Both + OH⁻(aq) → an alcohol + Br⁻.
  • Rate experiments show they react by different mechanisms: SN2 and SN1.
  • S = substitution · N = nucleophilic · the number = species in the rate-determining step.
Mission 3

SN2: one step

  • Typical of primary halogenoalkanes.
  • OH⁻ attacks the δ+ carbon from the side opposite Br.
  • Arrows: O lone pair → C; C–Br bond → Br. Both happen at once.
  • Halfway: a transition state [HO···C···Br]⁻, partial bonds, an energy maximum.
  • Two species in the one step: rate = k[RBr][OH⁻].
Mission 3

SN1: two steps

  • Typical of tertiary halogenoalkanes.
  • Step 1 (slow): C–Br breaks heterolytically → (CH3)3C⁺ + Br⁻. Arrow: C–Br bond → Br.
  • (CH3)3C⁺ is a carbocation intermediate, planar around C⁺.
  • Step 2 (fast): lone pair on O of OH⁻ → C⁺ → (CH3)3COH.
  • Only RBr is in the slow step: rate = k[RBr]. Zero order in OH⁻.
Quick check
?2-bromo-2-methylpropane, (CH3)3CBr, reacts with aqueous hydroxide ions by the SN1 mechanism. Which describes the first step?
Mission 3

Evidence from rate equations

  • Change one concentration at a time and measure the initial rate.
  • Primary: double [OH⁻] → rate doubles. OH⁻ is in the slow step → SN2.
  • Tertiary: double [OH⁻] → rate unchanged. OH⁻ is not in the slow step → SN1.
  • In both, doubling [RBr] doubles the rate.
  • The rate equation tells you which species are in the rate-determining step.
Quick check
?A halogenoalkane, RX, is hydrolysed by OH⁻(aq). Illustrative initial-rate data:
Which rate equation and mechanism fit?
  • Exp 1: [RX] 0.010, [OH⁻] 0.10 mol dm⁻³ → rate 2.0 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹
  • Exp 2: [RX] 0.020, [OH⁻] 0.10 mol dm⁻³ → rate 4.0 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹
  • Exp 3: [RX] 0.010, [OH⁻] 0.20 mol dm⁻³ → rate 2.0 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹
Mission 3

Why the routes differ

  • Tertiary: three alkyl groups push electrons towards C⁺, stabilising the carbocation.
  • They also crowd the back of the carbon, blocking attack. → SN1.
  • Primary: the carbocation would be too unstable, and the back is open. → SN2.
  • Secondary: both mechanisms can occur.
Mission 3

SN2 turns the molecule inside out

  • Background: not required by your spec, but it shows how SN2 works.
  • OH⁻ bonds at the back as Br leaves at the front.
  • The other three groups flip over, like an umbrella in a gale.
  • This is inversion of configuration.
  • SN1 is different: OH⁻ can attack the planar C⁺ from either side equally.
Mission 3

Energy profiles

  • Background: not required by your spec, but it pictures the two routes.
  • SN2: one hump. The top is the transition state. No intermediate.
  • SN1: two humps. The carbocation intermediate sits in the dip between them.
  • The dip lies above the reactants and below both humps.
  • The first hump (the slow step) is the higher one.
Mission 3

Why iodoalkanes react fastest

  • In both mechanisms the C–X bond breaks.
  • C–Cl 346 · C–Br 290 · C–I 228 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • Weaker bond → breaks faster: RI > RBr > RCl, same R and conditions.
  • In SN1, C–X breaking is the slow step.
Quick check
?Which statement about the mechanisms of nucleophilic substitution of halogenoalkanes is correct?
Mission check

?Two isomers of C5H11Cl are each hydrolysed by OH⁻(aq). Doubling [OH⁻] doubles the rate for 1-chloropentane, but leaves the rate unchanged for 2-chloro-2-methylbutane. Which statement is correct?

The tertiary carbon forms a stable carbocation and is crowded at the back → SN1. The primary carbon's back is open and its carbocation unstable → SN2. That's why the two C4H9Br isomers take different routes.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Why does hydroxide turn a bromoalkane into an alcohol in water, but into a gas in ethanol?

Make a guess: Why does hydroxide turn a bromoalkane into an alcohol in water, but into a gas in ethanol?

Mission 4

One ion, two jobs

  • OH⁻ has lone pairs on its O. It can donate one to two different places.
  • To a δ+ carbon: OH⁻ acts as a nucleophile → substitution (see Mission 1).
  • To an H on the carbon next to the C–Br carbon: OH⁻ acts as a base (it accepts H⁺) → elimination.
  • Same reagent, same compound. Conditions decide which job wins.
Quick check
?2-bromopropane is heated under reflux with potassium hydroxide dissolved in ethanol, and propene forms. What is the role of the hydroxide ion?
Mission 4

Elimination

  • Elimination: a small molecule is removed from two neighbouring carbons, leaving a C=C.
  • Here HBr is removed: H from one carbon, Br from the next.
  • CH3CHBrCH3 + KOH → CH2=CHCH3 + KBr + H2O
  • 2-bromopropane → propene, a gas.
Mission 4

Conditions decide

  • Both reactions happen at the same time (concurrently). Conditions change the proportions.
  • Aqueous KOH, warm → mainly substitution → alcohol.
  • Ethanolic KOH, hot (reflux) → mainly elimination → alkene.
  • Elimination is also favoured by tertiary halogenoalkanes; substitution by primary.
  • So 2-bromopropane with KOH gives both propan-2-ol and propene.
Quick check
?A chemist wants as much propene, and as little propan-2-ol, as possible from 2-bromopropane. Which conditions should be used?
Mission 4

Symmetrical or not?

  • H can be removed from any carbon next to the C–X carbon.
  • Symmetrical: 2-bromopropane. Both neighbours are CH3, so one alkene: propene.
  • Unsymmetrical: 2-bromobutane, CH3CHBrCH2CH3.
  • H from C1 → but-1-ene. H from C3 → but-2-ene, as E and Z isomers.
  • So unsymmetrical halogenoalkanes give a mixture of alkenes.
Quick check
?2-bromo-2-methylbutane, (CH3)2CBrCH2CH3, is heated with ethanolic potassium hydroxide. How many different alkenes can form?
Mission 4

In the lab

  • Dissolve KOH in ethanol. Add the halogenoalkane, e.g. 2-bromopropane.
  • Heat in a water bath or with an electric heater: no naked flame (ethanol is flammable).
  • Collect the gas over water in a test tube.
  • Shake the gas with bromine water: orange → colourless. The gas has a C=C.
  • With 1-bromobutane, the gas is but-1-ene.
Mission check

?Chlorocyclohexane is warmed with aqueous sodium hydroxide in one tube, and heated under reflux with sodium hydroxide in ethanol in another. The main product in the second tube is cyclohexene. Which statement explains the second result?

Same reagent, two jobs. In water, warm, OH⁻ mostly attacks carbon (alcohol). In ethanol, hot, it mostly removes H⁺ (alkene, often a gas).

Mission 5

Mission 5: Spray-can gases that are safe to breathe thinned the ozone layer. How can a little gas destroy so much ozone?

Make a guess: Spray-can gases that are safe to breathe thinned the ozone layer. How can a little gas destroy so much ozone?

Mission 5

Ozone: the UV shield

  • Ozone, O3, forms naturally in the upper atmosphere (stratosphere).
  • UV splits O2: O2 → 2O. Then O + O2 → O3.
  • Ozone absorbs UV radiation: O3 → O2 + O. The UV is used up, not passed on.
  • Less ozone → more harmful UV reaches the surface → more skin cancer, cataracts and crop damage.
Mission 5

CFCs: why chemists loved them

  • CFCs: chlorofluorocarbons, e.g. CCl2F2 (also written CF2Cl2) and CCl3F.
  • Non-toxic, non-flammable, very unreactive, easily liquefied.
  • Used as refrigerants, aerosol propellants, solvents and for blowing foam.
Mission 5

Chlorine atoms destroy ozone

  • CFCs are so unreactive that they last for decades and drift up to the ozone layer.
  • There, strong UV breaks them, releasing chlorine atoms.
  • Each chlorine atom destroys an ozone molecule and is then set free again.
  • So each chlorine atom destroys many thousands of ozone molecules: it acts as a catalyst.
  • Result: a thinner ozone layer, and more harmful UV reaches the Earth's surface.
Mission 5

Evidence, a ban and replacements

  • The Montreal Protocol (1987) and its later tightening phased out CFCs.
  • Replacements contain no chlorine: HFCs (e.g. CH2FCF3) and hydrocarbons (e.g. 2-methylpropane in fridges).
  • The ozone layer is now slowly recovering.
Quick check
?HFC-134a, CH2FCF3, replaced CFCs in fridges. Why doesn't it damage the ozone layer?
Mission check

?Old fridges leaked a CFC, CCl3F. Years later it reaches the ozone layer. What happens, and why is the effect so large?

Light frees a chlorine atom; the atom is a catalyst, set free again every cycle. That is how a little CFC destroys so much ozone.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Nucleophiles donate an electron pair to the δ+ carbon; the halogen leaves as X⁻ (heterolytic fission).
  • Curly arrows: from lone pair to C; from C–X bond to X.
  • Rate: C–I fastest, C–Cl slowest: bond enthalpy, not polarity.
  • CN⁻ gives nitriles (chain + 1 C); excess NH3 gives primary amines.

Mission 2

  • Acid protonates –OH; water leaves; then lose H⁺ (alkene) or add a halide (halogenoalkane).

Mission 3

  • SN2 (primary): one step, OH⁻ attacks opposite X, rate = k[RX][OH⁻].
  • SN1 (tertiary): slow C–X break to a carbocation, then fast attack; rate = k[RX].

Mission 4

  • OH⁻ is a nucleophile (substitution → alcohol) or a base (elimination → alkene).
  • Ethanolic KOH, hot → elimination of HX; unsymmetrical halogenoalkanes give a mixture of alkenes.

Mission 5

  • Ozone absorbs UV. UV breaks the C–Cl bond in CFCs, releasing chlorine atoms.
  • Each Cl atom is regenerated, so it catalyses the breakdown of many ozone molecules.
