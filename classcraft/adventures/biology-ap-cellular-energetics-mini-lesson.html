Mission 1

Mission 1: How can a protein make a reaction millions of times faster, yet come out of it unchanged?

Make a guess: How can a protein make a reaction millions of times faster, yet come out of it unchanged?

Mission 1

Enzymes are catalysts

  • An enzyme is a protein that acts as a biological catalyst.
  • A catalyst increases the rate of a reaction.
  • It is not used up: it leaves the reaction unchanged, so one molecule catalyzes the same reaction again and again.
  • The reaction can happen without the enzyme, but at body temperature it would be far too slow.
  • Enzymes facilitate reactions in cells; their structure and function help regulate biological processes.
  • Say: "speeds up a reaction" — not: "makes a reaction possible".
Quick check
?Which statement best describes the role of an enzyme in a chemical reaction in a cell?
Mission 1

Metabolism: a web of reactions

  • Metabolism: all the chemical reactions in an organism — a network of linked pathways.
  • Almost every step is catalyzed by its own enzyme. So an organism needs thousands of different enzymes.
  • Control the enzyme → control the step. That is how cells regulate metabolism.
Mission 1

Lowering the energy barrier

  • To react, bonds in the substrate must first be broken. That needs energy: the activation energy.
  • An enzyme gives the reaction a route with a lower activation energy.
  • At the same temperature, more substrate molecules now have enough energy to react. So the rate rises.
  • The energy of the substrate and of the products is unchanged. So is the energy released overall.
  • The enzyme supplies no energy.
  • Say: "the enzyme lowers the activation energy" — not: "the enzyme provides the activation energy".
Mission 1

Worked example: reading an energy diagram

Illustrative values, read from an energy diagram:

LevelWithout enzymeWith enzyme
Substrate5050
Peak11075
Products2020
  • Energies in kJ/mol; AP calls the substrate level "reactants".
  • Activation energy = peak − substrate level. Not the peak value read from the axis.
  • Without enzyme: 110 − 50 = 60. With enzyme: 75 − 50 = 25.
  • Energy released = substrate − products = 50 − 20 = 30, with or without the enzyme.
  • Percent decrease in activation energy = (60 − 25) ÷ 60 × 100 = 58%. Divide by the original value.
Quick check
?The graph shows the energy of the molecules during a reaction with and without an enzyme. By what percentage does the enzyme decrease the activation energy of the reaction?
Mission 1

The active site

  • Enzymes are globular proteins: one or more polypeptide chains folded into a compact 3D shape (tertiary structure).
  • The active site is a small pocket or groove where the substrate binds.
  • The substrate's shape is complementary to the active site; its charged groups are attracted to opposite charges there.
  • Substrate + active site → enzyme–substrate complex → products released; enzyme free again.
  • The substrate's shape and charge must be compatible with the active site: the enzyme–substrate complex model.
  • Say: "complementary shape" — not: "the same shape".
Mission 1

Specificity

  • Each enzyme catalyzes one reaction, or a few very similar ones: specificity.
  • Reason: only a molecule whose shape and charges suit the active site can bind.
  • Starch and cellulose are both chains of glucose. Amylase hydrolyzes starch but not cellulose.
  • Size alone does not decide: many small molecules do not bind.
  • Say: "the active site is complementary to one substrate" — not: "any molecule small enough fits".
Quick check
?In the human small intestine, the enzyme lactase hydrolyzes the disaccharide lactose, and the enzyme sucrase hydrolyzes the disaccharide sucrose. In people who make little lactase, much of the lactose they eat is not hydrolyzed in the small intestine, and their sucrase does not hydrolyze it. Which statement best explains why sucrase does not hydrolyze lactose?
Mission check

?In a hypothetical bacterium, enzyme E catalyzes the conversion of compound A to compound B. The conversion of A to B releases energy. A mutation stops the bacterium from making enzyme E. No other enzyme in the bacterium acts on compound A. Which prediction about compound B in the mutant bacterium is best supported?

An enzyme changes the route, not the destination: a lower barrier, the same start and finish, and the enzyme itself unchanged.

Mission 2

Measuring the rate

  • Rate = amount of product formed (or substrate used) per unit time.
  • Measure product appearing (e.g. oxygen from catalase) or substrate disappearing (e.g. starch, with iodine).
  • A product–time curve is steep at first, then flattens as substrate is used up.
  • Initial rate: the gradient at time zero, before the substrate runs down. The fairest way to compare conditions.
  • Timing a fixed end point (a color change, a disc rising)? Rate is proportional to 1 ÷ time.
Mission 2

Worked example: initial rate from a tangent

Illustrative data: oxygen released by catalase, plotted against time.

1. Draw a straight line touching the curve at t = 0: the tangent.

2. Pick two points far apart on the tangent: (0 s, 0) and (40 s, 36).

3. Gradient = change in y ÷ change in x = 36 ÷ 40.

  • Initial rate = 0.90 mL/s.
  • Same method at any point on a curve: tangent there, then gradient.
  • Fixed end point: a disc took 25 s to rise. Rate = 1 ÷ 25 = 0.040 s⁻¹.
Quick check
?Catalase was added to hydrogen peroxide and the volume of oxygen collected was recorded: 0 s, 0 mL; 20 s, 12 mL; 40 s, 20 mL; 60 s, 24 mL (illustrative data). What is the best estimate of the initial rate of reaction from these data?
Mission 2

Temperature

  • Warmer → molecules have more kinetic energy → they move faster.
  • More frequent collisions between substrate and active sites, and more with enough energy → more enzyme–substrate complexes per second → faster rate.
  • Above the optimum, vibrations break hydrogen and ionic bonds that hold the tertiary structure.
  • The active site changes shape; the substrate no longer fits. The enzyme is denatured.
  • In the cold, enzymes are slow, not denatured.
  • Optima differ: many human enzymes ≈ 37 °C; DNA polymerase from a hot-spring bacterium ≈ 75 °C.
  • Say: "the enzyme is denatured" — not: "the enzyme is killed".
Quick check
?A hypothetical enzyme has its highest rate of reaction at 40 °C. At 45 °C, its rate is lower. Which statement best explains the lower rate at 45 °C?
Mission 2

pH

  • pH measures the concentration of hydrogen ions (H⁺).
  • H⁺ ions change the charges on R groups (e.g. –COO⁻ + H⁺ → –COOH).
  • Ionic and hydrogen bonds change → the active site's shape and charges change → substrate binds less well.
  • Far from the optimum the enzyme is denatured.
  • Optima differ: pepsin (stomach) ≈ pH 2; salivary amylase ≈ pH 7; trypsin (small intestine) ≈ pH 8.
  • Say: "pH changes the charges on R groups" — not: "pH slows the molecules down".
Quick check
?A hypothetical species of freshwater alga lives in a lake with a pH of 8.5. One of its enzymes, which works in the water surrounding the alga's cells, has an optimal pH of 8.5. Pollution lowers the lake's pH to 7.0. Which prediction about the activity of this enzyme is best supported?
Mission 2

Substrate and enzyme concentration

  • More substrate: more collisions with active sites → faster rate.
  • At high substrate concentration, every active site is occupied almost all the time: the enzyme is saturated. The rate levels off; enzyme concentration now limits it.
  • More enzyme: more active sites → rate rises in proportion, as long as substrate is in excess.
  • A rate–concentration plateau (saturation) is not the same as a product–time curve flattening (substrate used up).
  • Say: "the active sites are saturated" — not: "the enzyme is used up".
Mission 2

Chemicals, reversibility and products

  • Chemicals can denature enzymes too, e.g. urea disrupts the hydrogen bonds that hold the folded shape.
  • In some cases denaturation is reversible. Ribonuclease unfolded by urea plus a chemical that breaks its disulfide bonds refolded and regained its activity when both were removed (Anfinsen, 1961).
  • Many enzyme-catalyzed reactions run in both directions.
  • The relative concentrations of substrates and products set the net direction and how efficiently the reaction proceeds.
  • Product building up slows the net forward reaction; product being removed keeps it going.
Quick check
?The enzyme carbonic anhydrase, found in red blood cells, catalyzes the reversible reaction CO2 + H2O ⇌ H2CO3. As blood passes through the lungs, CO2 diffuses out of the blood into the air in the lungs, so the concentration of CO2 in the red blood cells falls. Which prediction about the reaction catalyzed by carbonic anhydrase in these red blood cells is best supported?
Mission check

?A hypothetical bacterium makes an enzyme that loses its activity when heated to 50 °C. A student claims that the denaturation of this enzyme at 50 °C is reversible. Which observation, if made, would best support the student's claim?

Heat speeds enzymes by speeding collisions, until it disrupts the bonds that shape the active site. Sometimes the shape can return; often it cannot.

Mission 3

Metabolic pathways

  • A metabolic pathway is a chain of enzyme-catalyzed steps.
  • The product of one step is the substrate of the next: A → B → C → D, with enzymes 1, 2 and 3.
  • The product of each step is the reactant for the next. Energy pathways are sequential, so energy is transferred in small, controlled steps.
Quick check
?In a hypothetical bacterium, compound S is made from compound P by a pathway of three enzyme-catalyzed steps: P → Q → R → S. Enzyme 1 converts P to Q, enzyme 2 converts Q to R, and enzyme 3 converts R to S. A mutation makes enzyme 2 non-functional. Which prediction about the mutant bacterium is best supported?
Mission 3

Competitive inhibitors

  • Similar in shape (and charge) to the substrate.
  • Binds reversibly to the active site. While it is there, the substrate cannot bind.
  • Inhibitor and substrate compete: whichever collides with a free active site binds.
  • More substrate → substrate wins more often → rate climbs back toward the uninhibited maximum.
  • More inhibitor → more active sites blocked → lower rate.
Mission 3

Non-competitive inhibitors

  • Bind at a site away from the active site.
  • This is an allosteric site.
  • Binding changes the enzyme's tertiary structure → the active site changes shape → substrate cannot bind, or is not converted.
  • No competition: extra substrate does not restore the rate. The maximum rate is lowered.
  • Say: "changes the shape of the active site" — not: "blocks the active site".
  • Say: "binds away from the active site" — not: "non-competitive means irreversible".

Mission check

?Methanol is not very toxic itself, but the enzyme alcohol dehydrogenase converts it into toxic products. Ethanol is also a substrate of alcohol dehydrogenase. Doctors have treated methanol poisoning by giving ethanol. Why does this reduce the harm?

One enzyme, one step: a molecule that occupies or reshapes that enzyme's active site slows that reaction and leaves the rest of metabolism running.

Mission 4

Hydrolysis releases energy

  • ATP + H2O → ADP + Pi (inorganic phosphate).
  • Energy is released: enough for many single tasks in the cell.
  • ATP hydrolysis is an energy-releasing process.
  • Breaking any bond takes energy. Energy is released overall because ADP and Pi are more stable than ATP.
  • Say: "energy is released when ATP is hydrolyzed" — not: "energy is stored in a high-energy bond".
Mission 4

Coupling: putting the energy to work

  • ATP hydrolysis is coupled to an energy-requiring process: one enzyme links them, so they happen together.
  • Energy-releasing processes can be coupled with energy-requiring ones. ATP hydrolysis driving an ion pump against its gradient is one example.
  • Why not use glucose directly? Its energy is released in many enzyme-controlled steps; one ATP releases a small, usable amount in one step, right where the task needs it.
  • Say: "active transport uses ATP" — not: "diffusion and osmosis use ATP".
Quick check
?Which of the following is an example of an energy-releasing process coupled with an energy-requiring process in a cell?
Mission check

?The model compares the energy of the reactants and products of reaction 1, which joins molecules A and B, of reaction 2, the hydrolysis of ATP, and of the two reactions coupled together in a cell. Which statement about the coupled reaction is supported by the model?

Coupling works because ATP hydrolysis releases more energy than the joined reaction needs.

Mission 5

Mission 5: A living cell is far more ordered than its surroundings. How does it stay that way without breaking physics?

Make a guess: A living cell is far more ordered than its surroundings. How does it stay that way without breaking physics?

Mission 5

Every living thing needs energy in

  • All living systems require an input of energy.
  • Plants and other photosynthesizers capture light energy. Other organisms take in chemical energy in food.
  • Cells spend energy to build molecules, move substances and keep their order.
  • Resting cells and dormant seeds still use energy, just more slowly.
  • Say: "plants capture light energy" — not: "plants make their own energy".
Quick check
?Which statement about the energy requirements of living cells is correct?
Mission 5

Order and the two laws

  • Cells are highly ordered: membranes, concentration gradients, macromolecules.
  • First law of thermodynamics: energy is never created or destroyed, only transformed.
  • Second law: every energy transformation increases the disorder (entropy) of the universe. Some energy always leaves as heat.
  • A cell builds order inside by increasing disorder outside: it releases heat and small molecules such as CO2 and H2O.
  • Total disorder still rises. Life obeys both laws.
  • Say: "energy is transformed" — not: "energy is used up" or "created".
Quick check
?A seed grows into a large plant whose cells contain highly ordered structures built from simple molecules. A student claims that this growth violates the second law of thermodynamics, which states that energy transformations increase the disorder (entropy) of the universe. Which reasoning best refutes the student's claim?

  • To keep its order and grow, energy input must exceed energy loss.
  • Heat released leaves the body. It cannot be turned back into usable chemical energy.
  • Heat absorbed from the sun warms the body but cannot power its processes.
  • If input falls below loss, the animal breaks down stores (fat, glycogen): it loses mass as CO2 and water.
Quick check
?The table shows the energy budget of a young hypothetical mammal over one day. Assume that all of the energy in the food eaten that does not leave the body is stored in new body tissue. What percentage of the energy in the food eaten is stored in new body tissue?
Mission 5

Coupling: paying for uphill reactions

  • Energy-releasing processes can be coupled with energy-requiring ones.
  • Example: ATP hydrolysis (releases energy) coupled to pumping ions against their gradient (requires energy).
  • Energy passes from the releasing reaction to the requiring one. Never the reverse.
  • The coupled pair releases energy overall, so it can proceed.
  • Heat cannot drive the requiring reaction. Enzymes lower activation energy but supply no energy.
Quick check
?A student draws four diagrams to represent how a cell drives the energy-requiring reaction A + B → AB. In each diagram, an arrow shows a transfer of energy. Which diagram correctly represents energy coupling in the cell?
Mission 5

Lose the energy flow, lose the order

  • Order is not permanent. Membranes, gradients and molecules need constant energy to maintain.
  • Cells hold only a small amount of ATP; they must keep making it.
  • Stop the flow and order collapses: a significant loss of order or energy flow results in death.
  • Example: cyanide blocks the electron transport chain. ATP production stops; cells die within minutes.
  • Example: a plant kept in darkness respires its stored sugars, loses dry mass, then dies.

Mission 5

Pathways go step by step

  • Energy-related pathways are sequential: many small steps, each catalyzed by its own enzyme.
  • The product of one step is the reactant for the next: P → Q → R → S.
  • Small steps give a controlled transfer: part of the energy is captured in usable form (ATP), not all released at once as heat.
  • Burning glucose in a flame releases the same total energy in one step.
  • Block one enzyme: its reactant builds up; everything after it falls.
Quick check

Mission check

?A student puts germinating pea seeds into one insulated flask and the same mass of pea seeds killed by boiling into a second insulated flask. Both sets of seeds were first rinsed in a disinfectant that kills microorganisms. A thermometer in each flask reads 20 °C at the start, and the room stays at 20 °C. Which result after 48 hours is best predicted?

Living things pay for their order with a constant energy input, and the heat they give off keeps the universe's disorder rising.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Enzymes are protein catalysts: they lower the activation energy, leave the energy released unchanged, and are not used up.
  • The active site, held in shape by the whole tertiary structure, is complementary in shape and charge to one substrate: hence the enzyme–substrate complex and specificity.

Mission 2

  • Temperature and substrate concentration raise the rate by increasing collisions, until denaturation (temperature) or saturation (substrate) takes over; pH acts on the charges that hold the active site's shape.
  • Some denaturation is reversible; relative substrate and product concentrations set the net direction of a reaction.

Mission 3

  • In a pathway the product of one step is the substrate of the next; acting on one enzyme controls one pathway.
  • Competitive inhibitors bind the active site and are outcompeted by more substrate; non-competitive inhibitors bind elsewhere, change the active site and lower the maximum rate.

Mission 4

  • An energy-releasing process such as ATP hydrolysis can be coupled with an energy-requiring one, so the pair proceeds overall.

Mission 5

  • Living systems need a continual energy input; input must exceed loss to keep order. They build order inside by releasing heat and disorder outside, so they obey both laws of thermodynamics.
  • Energy-releasing processes drive energy-requiring ones by coupling; pathways run in small sequential steps; losing the energy flow means losing order, and death.
