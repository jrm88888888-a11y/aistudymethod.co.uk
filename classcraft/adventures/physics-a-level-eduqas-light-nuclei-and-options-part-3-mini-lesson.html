Mission 1

Mission 1: Experiments in the early 1800s convinced physicists light is a wave. Which experiment made them think again?

Make a guess: Experiments in the early 1800s convinced physicists light is a wave. Which experiment made them think again?

Mission 1

Demonstration: electroscope and zinc plate

  • Clean zinc plate on a gold-leaf electroscope, charged negatively: the leaf rises (repelled).
  • Shine ultraviolet on the zinc: the leaf falls. Electrons are leaving the zinc.
  • Shine a very bright visible lamp instead: no change. Below the threshold frequency.
  • Charge it positively and shine UV: no change. Emitted electrons are pulled back.
  • Put a sheet of glass (absorbs UV) in the beam: the leaf stops falling.
Quick check
?A clean zinc plate is isolated and initially uncharged, and it is not connected to any circuit or battery. In a vacuum, ultraviolet light with a frequency above the threshold frequency for zinc shines on the plate. Which claim is correct?
Mission 1

Vacuum photocell: measuring Ek max

  • Light of one frequency on the photocathode (emitter) of an evacuated photocell; electrons cross to the anode (collector).
  • Make the anode negative with a variable supply; read the current on a sensitive ammeter (a nanoammeter).
  • Raise the reverse p.d. until the current is just zero: the stopping potential Vs.
  • Ek max = eVs. In eV, Ek max is simply Vs: 1.35 V → 1.35 eV.
  • In J: × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ → 1.35 eV = 2.16 × 10⁻¹⁹ J.
  • Repeat with filters or LEDs of different frequencies.
Mission 1

The graph of Ek max against f

  • Ek max = hf − φ is a straight line: y = mx + c.
  • Gradient = h, the same for every metal.
  • Intercept on the f axis = f0, the threshold frequency.
  • Extrapolate back: intercept on the Ek max axis = −φ.
  • Different metals: parallel lines, shifted along the f axis.
  • Brighter light: same line. Intensity is not in the equation.
Quick check
?Monochromatic light falls on the emitting plate of a photocell. The potential of the collecting electrode relative to the emitting plate is varied. The photocurrent is zero at −1.2 V and below; it rises as the potential increases and levels off at 3.0 μA above about +2 V. The intensity of the light is then doubled, with its frequency unchanged. Which describes the new current–potential graph?
Quick check
?A student uses a vacuum photocell. With light of frequency 6.0 × 10¹⁴ Hz the stopping potential is 0.52 V; with light of frequency 8.0 × 10¹⁴ Hz it is 1.35 V. What is the threshold frequency of the photocathode? (h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C)
Mission check

?The zinc on a negatively charged electroscope is replaced by a clean plate of a metal that emits electrons only for light of wavelength shorter than 540 nm. A bright red lamp (650 nm) shines on it, then a dim blue lamp (450 nm). What does the leaf do?

Whether electrons leave depends on the energy of each photon, set by its frequency; brightness changes only how many leave. A sharp threshold like this fits photons, not waves.

Mission 2

Mission 2: An LED stays dark until the voltage reaches a set value, and that value depends on its colour. Why?

Make a guess: An LED stays dark until the voltage reaches a set value, and that value depends on its colour. Why?

Mission 2

From electron energy to photon energy

  • An electron accelerated through a p.d. V gains energy eV.
  • If all of it goes into one photon: eV = hf = hc/λ.
  • That photon has the highest frequency and shortest wavelength possible.
  • Example: 2.0 V → 2.0 eV → λ = hc/eV = (6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ × 3.00 × 10⁸)/(1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 2.0) = 622 nm (orange-red).
Mission 2

Planck constant from LEDs

  • An LED emits light of (nearly) one wavelength λ, given by the maker or measured with a grating.
  • It begins to emit only when the p.d. across it reaches a threshold value V0.
  • Model: each electron crossing the LED gives one photon: eV0 = hc/λ.
  • Circuit: variable supply, protective resistor in series, voltmeter across the LED, ammeter (or watch for the first glow through a tube).
  • Repeat for several colours. Plot V0 against 1/λ: straight line, gradient = hc/e, so h = e × gradient/c.
  • No semiconductor theory needed.
Mission 2

Worked example

Red LED, λ = 630 nm, threshold p.d. V0 = 1.96 V.

  • eV0 = hc/λ → h = eV0λ/c
  • h = (1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 1.96 × 630 × 10⁻⁹)/(3.00 × 10⁸) = 6.59 × 10⁻³⁴ J s.
  • Close to 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s. Judging the exact moment the LED "starts" is the main uncertainty: use the graph of several LEDs.
Quick check
?A student is given the frequency f of each LED's light, not its wavelength. She plots threshold p.d. V0 against f. The gradient of the straight line is G. Which expression gives the Planck constant? (e = electron charge, c = speed of light)

  • Shorter-wavelength ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma can ionise most atoms (ionisation energies mostly ≈ 4–25 eV).
Quick check
?A heat camera detects photons each with an energy of 3.0 × 10⁻²⁰ J. To which region of the electromagnetic spectrum do they belong?
Mission check

?An LED of wavelength λ starts to emit at a threshold p.d. V0. A second LED starts to emit at 2V0. What is its wavelength?

eV0 = hc/λ: the higher the threshold voltage, the shorter the wavelength. Each colour's photons need a different energy.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Electrons fired at a thin carbon film make rings, just as light does. How can particles diffract?

Make a guess: Electrons fired at a thin carbon film make rings, just as light does. How can particles diffract?

Mission 3

Wave or particle?

  • Light: diffraction and interference → wave. Photoelectric effect → photons (particles).
  • Electrons: definite mass and charge, single impacts → particle. Electron diffraction → wave.
  • Wave–particle duality: light and matter both need both models. The experiment decides which shows.
Mission 3

The de Broglie wavelength

  • Every moving particle has a wavelength.
  • p = h/λ, for particles of matter and for photons.
  • More momentum → shorter wavelength.
  • h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, so λ is tiny unless p is tiny.
Mission 3

Worked example

  • An electron is accelerated from rest through 150 V. Find λ.
  • Kinetic energy: Eₖ = eV = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 150 = 2.40 × 10⁻¹⁷ J.
  • Momentum: Eₖ = p²/2m, so p = √(2mEₖ) = √(2 × 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ × 2.40 × 10⁻¹⁷) = 6.61 × 10⁻²⁴ kg m s⁻¹.
  • λ = h/p = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ ÷ 6.61 × 10⁻²⁴ = 1.00 × 10⁻¹⁰ m.
  • About the spacing of atoms in a crystal.
Quick check
?An electron is accelerated from rest through a potential difference of 2.0 kV. Take h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C, me = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg and c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹, and treat the electron as non-relativistic. What is the de Broglie wavelength of the electron?
Quick check
?Predict first. In an electron diffraction tube, electrons accelerated through a potential difference of a few kilovolts pass through a thin film of graphite and strike a fluorescent screen, where bright concentric rings are seen. How does this experiment provide evidence for the wave nature of matter?
Mission 3

Electron diffraction

  • Electron gun: a hot filament releases electrons; a few kV accelerates them.
  • They pass through a thin film of polycrystalline graphite, then hit a fluorescent screen.
  • The screen shows bright concentric rings: a diffraction pattern.
  • Electron λ (about 10⁻¹¹ m) is a few times smaller than the spacing between carbon atoms (about 10⁻¹⁰ m): close enough for the atomic layers to diffract them.
  • Many tiny crystals at random angles → rings, not spots.
  • Ring angles fit λ = h/p. Only a wave model explains this.
Mission 3

More momentum, less diffraction

  • Raise the accelerating potential difference → electrons faster → more momentum.
  • More momentum → shorter λ → smaller diffraction angles → smaller rings.
  • Lower potential difference → longer λ → larger rings.
  • From eV = p²/2m: λ ∝ 1/√V. Four times the p.d. halves λ.
Mission 3

When does the wave nature show?

  • Diffraction is noticeable only when λ is comparable to the gap or object size.
  • A cricket ball at 30 m s⁻¹: λ ≈ 10⁻³⁴ m. No gap is that small, so no diffraction is seen.
Mission 3

Photons have momentum too

  • A photon also has momentum: p = h/λ (and E = pc).
  • Light absorbed by a surface: each photon gives it momentum h/λ.
  • Light reflected straight back: each photon's momentum reverses, giving 2h/λ.
  • Force = momentum delivered per second. Beam of power P: F = P/c absorbed, F = 2P/c reflected.
  • Radiation pressure (intensity I): I/c absorbed, 2I/c reflected.
Mission 3

Worked example: a solar sail

  • Sunlight near the Earth: I ≈ 1.36 × 10³ W m⁻². Sail: 100 m², perfectly reflecting, facing the Sun.
  • Pressure = 2I/c = 2 × 1.36 × 10³ ÷ 3.00 × 10⁸ = 9.07 × 10⁻⁶ Pa.
  • Force = pressure × area = 9.07 × 10⁻⁶ × 100 = 9.1 × 10⁻⁴ N.
  • A black (absorbing) sail would get half this.
Quick check
?A laser beam of power 2.0 W falls normally on a surface that absorbs all of it. What force does the beam exert on the surface? Take c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m s⁻¹.
Mission check

?A beam of electrons and a beam of protons are each accelerated from rest through 100 V and aimed at the same crystal, whose rows of atoms are 2.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ m apart. Which beam shows clear diffraction?

Electrons have a wavelength λ = h/p. They diffract when it is close to the size of the gap, as with the spacing of carbon atoms.

Mission 4

Mission 4: A laser beam is a flood of light waves all marching in perfect step. How does a laser make them?

Make a guess: A laser beam is a flood of light waves all marching in perfect step. How does a laser make them?

Mission 4

Absorption and spontaneous emission

  • Two energy levels, E1 (lower) and E2 (upper). N1 and N2: numbers of atoms in each.
  • Absorption: a photon with hf = E2 − E1 is absorbed; an electron moves up to E2.
  • Spontaneous emission: an excited electron drops back to E1 on its own, at a random time, giving a photon with hf = E2 − E1.
  • Spontaneous photons have random directions and phases: ordinary lamp light.
Mission 4

Stimulated emission

  • A photon with hf = E2 − E1 passes an atom that is already excited to E2.
  • It triggers the electron to drop to E1, emitting a second photon.
  • The new photon is a copy: same frequency, same phase, same direction (and polarisation).
  • The first photon is not absorbed: one photon in, two identical photons out.
  • Each copy can trigger more copies: a chain. All in step, so the light is coherent.
Quick check
?An atom has only two energy levels, 1.8 eV apart, and its electron is in the upper one. A photon of energy 2.1 eV passes it. What happens?
Mission 4

Population inversion

  • Normally N1 > N2: most atoms are in the lower level.
  • Then a passing photon is more likely to meet an unexcited atom and be absorbed than to stimulate emission. Light is weakened.
  • To amplify light, stimulated emission must win: need N2 > N1.
  • Population inversion: more atoms in the upper level of the transition than in the lower level.
  • Laser = Light Amplification by Stimulated Emission of Radiation.
Mission 4

Metastable state and pumping

  • Most excited states last about 10⁻⁸ s before spontaneous emission empties them.
  • A metastable state is an excited state that lasts far longer, around 10⁻³ s.
  • Atoms pile up in the metastable state instead of dropping back at once.
  • With enough pumping, it can then hold more atoms than the level below: a population inversion.
  • Energy must be supplied continually to lift atoms up: pumping (with light, or an electric current or discharge).
Quick check
?In a medium, 6.0 × 10¹⁸ atoms are in the lower level of a transition and 4.0 × 10¹⁸ in the upper level. A beam of photons with hf = E2 − E1 passes through. What happens to the beam?
Mission 4

Structure of a laser

  • Amplifying medium (gas, crystal or semiconductor) with a population inversion, kept up by a pump.
  • A mirror at each end, facing each other.
  • One mirror reflects (almost) 100%. The other is partially transmitting: it lets out a few per cent.
  • Photons travelling along the axis bounce back and forth, stimulating more emission on every pass. Photons in other directions leave through the sides.
  • The fraction let out through the partial mirror is the laser beam: coherent, monochromatic, narrow.
Quick check
?A laser's partially transmitting mirror is replaced by a clear window that reflects nothing. The other mirror is unchanged. What happens?
Mission check

?Along the axis of a ruby laser, a photon of energy 1.79 eV passes three chromium ions in turn. Each ion has an electron in a metastable level 1.79 eV above its ground level, and each is stimulated to emit once. What travels on past the third ion?

The copy is made by stimulated emission: an excited atom, nudged by a matching photon, emits an identical photon. Mirrors and a population inversion keep the copying going.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Why can't a laser work with just two energy levels, and why are four better than three?

Make a guess: Why can't a laser work with just two energy levels, and why are four better than three?

Mission 5

Why two levels fail

  • Pump a two-level system with photons of energy E2 − E1.
  • For one atom, a pump photon is as likely to stimulate emission (atom at E2) as to be absorbed (atom at E1).
  • So while N2 < N1, absorption wins and N2 rises. As N2 approaches N1, stimulated emission catches up; at N2 = N1 the two balance.
  • Best possible: N2 = N1. Never N2 > N1, so no population inversion.
  • So a laser needs more than two levels: pump into one level, lase from another.
Mission 5

Pumping

  • Pumping: supplying energy to lift atoms into an upper level.
  • Purpose: to create, and keep up, the population inversion. Lasing drains it all the time.
  • Methods: optical (flash lamp, or another laser), electrical (discharge through a gas, or a current through a semiconductor).
  • All the energy in the beam comes from the pump. Real lasers turn only part of the pump energy into beam energy.
Quick check
?A medium contains 1000 atoms, each with only two energy levels, E1 and E2. It is pumped with very intense light of photon energy E2 − E1. What is the largest number of atoms that can be in level E2?
Mission 5

Three-level system

  • Level 1: ground state. Level 3: pump level, short-lived. Level 2: metastable.
  • Pump atoms 1 → 3. They drop quickly 3 → 2 (no light emitted).
  • They pile up in level 2. Lasing: 2 → 1.
  • Snag: the lower laser level is the ground state, which starts full.
  • So more than half of all the atoms must be pumped out of it before N2 > N1: intense pumping needed (e.g. the ruby laser, which is usually pulsed).
Mission 5

Four-level system

  • Level 0 ground; pump 0 → 3; fast drop 3 → 2 (metastable, the upper laser level).
  • Lasing: 2 → 1. Then a fast drop 1 → 0.
  • Level 1 empties quickly, so it is almost always nearly empty.
  • So N2 > N1 with only a small fraction of atoms excited.
  • Much less pumping needed; continuous operation is easy (e.g. Nd:YAG lasers).
Quick check
?A laser medium contains 1.0 × 10²⁰ atoms. In a four-level scheme, the lower laser level holds only about 1 × 10¹⁴ atoms at any moment, because it empties so fast. How many atoms must be in the upper laser level for a population inversion in a three-level scheme, and in this four-level scheme?
Mission 5

Semiconductor lasers

  • Pumped directly by an electric current through a tiny semiconductor chip.
  • Small: a fraction of a millimetre.
  • Cheap: mass-produced like other chips.
  • Far more efficient than other types: a large share of the electrical energy becomes light.
  • Uses: reading CDs, DVDs and Blu-ray discs; telecommunications (signals down optical fibres); laser pointers; barcode scanners.
Quick check
?A semiconductor laser turns about 30% of its electrical input into light; a helium–neon gas laser, about 0.1%. Each must give a beam of power 1.0 mW. What electrical input power does each need?
Mission check

?In a laser, atoms are pumped from the ground state to a short-lived level. They drop quickly to a metastable level. The laser transition is from the metastable level to a level 0.25 eV above the ground state, and atoms in that level drop to the ground state within nanoseconds. Which statement is correct?

With two levels, pumping pushes atoms down as fast as up, so N2 never beats N1. Extra levels separate pumping from lasing.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Two atoms of the same element can have different masses. What is different inside them?

Make a guess: Two atoms of the same element can have different masses. What is different inside them?

Mission 6

Inside the atom

  • A tiny, positively charged nucleus, with electrons around it.
  • Nucleus: protons (charge +e) and neutrons (no charge). Together: nucleons.
  • Nucleus about 10⁻¹⁵ to 10⁻¹⁴ m across; atom about 10⁻¹⁰ m: 10 000 to 100 000 times larger.
  • A proton or neutron has about 1840 times an electron's mass. Nearly all the atom's mass is in the nucleus.
Mission 6

Nuclear notation

  • ᴬZ X. Example: ⁵⁶26Fe.
  • Z = proton number (atomic number): 26 protons.
  • A = nucleon number (mass number): 56 nucleons.
  • Neutrons: N = A − Z = 56 − 26 = 30.
  • Z fixes the element. Every iron nucleus has 26 protons.
Quick check
?A nucleus of potassium-40 is written in nuclear notation as ⁴⁰19K. Which statement about this nucleus is correct?
Quick check
?Predict first. Carbon-12 (¹²6C) and carbon-14 (¹⁴6C) are isotopes of carbon. Which statement about these two nuclides is correct?
Mission 6

Isotopes

  • Isotopes: same proton number, different neutron number.
  • Same element. Same number of electrons, so the same chemistry.
  • Different mass: extra neutrons add mass.
  • Carbon: ¹²6C (6 neutrons), ¹³6C (7), ¹⁴6C (8).
  • Some isotopes are unstable: carbon-14 is radioactive; carbon-12 is not.
Mission check

?Which pair are isotopes of the same element?

Same element means the same number of protons. Atoms of one element differ in mass because their nuclei hold different numbers of neutrons.

Mission 7

Mission 7: Can a nucleus decay three times and end up as the same element it started as?

Make a guess: Can a nucleus decay three times and end up as the same element it started as?

Mission 7

Unstable nuclei

  • Some nuclei are unstable. They decay: they emit radiation and become more stable.
  • Decay is spontaneous: nothing triggers it. Heat, pressure and chemistry do not change it.
  • The emitted particle or photon is the radiation: α, β or γ.
  • The type of decay is set by the isotope: carbon-14 decays by β⁻; carbon-11 by β⁺.
Mission 7

Meet the radiations

  • α particle: a helium-4 nucleus. 2 protons + 2 neutrons. Charge +2e. Symbol ⁴2He or ⁴2α.
  • β⁻ particle: a fast electron from the nucleus. Charge −e. Symbol ⁰₋1e or ⁰₋1β.
  • β⁺ particle: a fast positron, the electron's antiparticle. Charge +e. Symbol ⁰₊1e or ⁰₊1β.
  • γ ray: a high-energy photon. No charge, no mass.
Mission 7

Balancing decay equations

  • Top numbers (nucleon number, A) add up the same on both sides.
  • Bottom numbers (proton number or charge, Z) add up the same on both sides.
  • An electron counts as ⁰₋1e: no nucleons, charge −1.
Mission 7

Worked example: α and β⁻

  • α decay: ²³⁸92U → ²³⁴90Th + ⁴2He
  • A: 238 = 234 + 4 ✓. Z: 92 = 90 + 2 ✓.
  • New element: Z falls by 2 (uranium → thorium). A falls by 4.
  • β⁻ decay: ¹⁴6C → ¹⁴7N + ⁰₋1e + ν̄
  • ν̄ is an antineutrino: uncharged, almost no mass, emitted with the β⁻ particle.
  • A: 14 = 14 + 0 ✓. Z: 6 = 7 + (−1) ✓.
  • Inside: a neutron → proton. Z rises by 1; A unchanged.
Quick check
?A nucleus has nucleon number A and proton number Z. It undergoes an alpha decay, and the nucleus formed then undergoes a beta-minus decay. Which expression gives the number of neutrons in the final nucleus?
Mission 7

β⁺ and γ decay

  • β⁺ decay: ¹⁸9F → ¹⁸8O + ⁰₊1e + ν
  • Inside: a proton → neutron. Z falls by 1; A unchanged.
  • γ decay: after α or β decay the new nucleus is often left excited. It drops to a lower energy state by emitting a γ photon.
  • ⁶⁰28Ni* → ⁶⁰28Ni + γ. A and Z do not change. Same nuclide, less energy.
Mission 7

Neutrinos

  • β⁻ decay emits an antineutrino ν̄; β⁺ decay emits a neutrino ν.
  • Both are uncharged, have tiny mass, and very rarely interact with matter.
Quick check
?Sodium-22 (²²11Na) decays by β⁺ emission. The daughter nucleus is left in an excited state and then emits a γ photon. What is the final nucleus?

?A steel works checks the thickness of steel sheet, about 5 mm thick, with a radioactive source on one side of the sheet and a detector on the other. Which type of source is most suitable, and why?
Quick check
?Predict first. With no radioactive source near it, a detector still records a count rate. Which statement about this background count rate is correct?
Mission 7

Background radiation

  • A detector counts even with no source present: the background count.
  • Natural: radon gas from rocks and soil, rocks and building materials, cosmic rays, food and drink (e.g. potassium-40).
  • Artificial (smaller): medical uses, past weapons tests, the nuclear industry.
  • Correction: measure the background (long count, no source). Subtract it from every reading.
  • Corrected count rate = measured count rate − background count rate.
Mission check

?Thorium-232 (²³²90Th) decays through a chain of α and β⁻ decays to stable lead-208 (²⁰⁸82Pb). How many α decays and how many β⁻ decays are there in the chain?

An α decay lowers Z by 2; two β⁻ decays raise it by 2. So α, β⁻, β⁻ returns a nucleus to the same element: an isotope with 4 fewer nucleons.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Negatively charged zinc on an electroscope discharges under UV, not under bright visible light: a threshold frequency.
  • Photocell: Ek max = eVs. Graph of Ek max against f: gradient h, f-intercept f0, Ek-intercept −φ.

Mission 2

  • One electron's energy eV into one photon: eV = hc/λ.
  • LEDs: eV0 = hc/λ; plot V0 against 1/λ, h = e × gradient/c.
  • Photon energies: visible ≈ 2–3 eV; X-rays ≈ keV; gamma ≈ MeV.

Mission 3

  • Particles have a de Broglie wavelength λ = h/p; electron diffraction is the evidence.
  • p = h/λ for particles and photons; light exerts a pressure I/c (absorbed) or 2I/c (reflected).
  • More momentum → shorter λ → less diffraction (smaller rings).

Mission 4

  • Stimulated emission: a photon with hf = E2 − E1 triggers an excited atom to emit an identical photon (same f, phase, direction): coherent light.
  • Amplification needs a population inversion (N2 > N1), made possible by a long-lived metastable state and pumping; mirrors (one partially transmitting) feed the light back through the medium.

Mission 5

  • Two levels: N2 can at best equal N1, so no inversion. Pumping (optical or electrical) creates and maintains the inversion.
  • Three-level: lower laser level is the ground state, so over half the atoms must be pumped. Four-level: lower laser level empties fast, so inversion is easy. Semiconductor lasers: small, cheap, efficient; CDs/DVDs, telecommunications.

Mission 6

  • Nucleus: protons + neutrons (nucleons). ᴬZ X: Z protons, A nucleons, A − Z neutrons.
  • Isotopes: same Z, different numbers of neutrons; same chemistry, different mass.

Mission 7

  • α: ⁴2He, A − 4, Z − 2. β⁻: neutron → proton, Z + 1. γ: no change to A or Z.
  • β⁺: proton → neutron, Z − 1. β⁻ emits an antineutrino, β⁺ a neutrino.
  • α: most ionising, least penetrating; γ: least ionising, most penetrating.
  • Subtract the background from every count.
