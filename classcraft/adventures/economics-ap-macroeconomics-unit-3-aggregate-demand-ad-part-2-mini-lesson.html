Mission 1

Mission 1: Why can prices and unemployment rise at the same time?

Make a guess: Why can prices and unemployment rise at the same time?

Mission 1

Where AD meets AS

  • Macroeconomic equilibrium (short run): where AD = SRAS.
  • It sets two things at once: the price level and real output (real GDP).
  • Above it: planned output > planned spending. Unsold stock piles up, so firms cut output and prices.
  • Below it: spending > output. Stocks run down, so firms raise output and prices.
  • A change in the price level → a movement along AD or SRAS.
  • A change in anything else (confidence, interest rates, costs) → a shift of the curve.
  • Say: "an increase in the quantity of real output supplied" for a movement along SRAS — not: "SRAS increases".
Mission 1

Long-run equilibrium

  • LRAS is vertical at full-employment output, Yf.
  • Long-run equilibrium: AD and SRAS cross on LRAS.
  • There, unemployment is at its natural rate: no cyclical unemployment, but some frictional and structural unemployment.
  • Inflation is stable: no pressure from too much or too little spending.
  • In the short run, equilibrium can sit left or right of LRAS. Then the economy has an output gap.

Mission 1

Demand shocks

  • Demand shock: a sudden, unexpected shift of AD.
  • Negative: AD shifts left. Examples: a collapse in confidence, a credit crunch, a world recession cutting exports.
    • Price level falls (or rises more slowly), real output falls, unemployment rises.
  • Positive: AD shifts right. Examples: an export boom, a burst of confidence, a sudden rise in government spending.
    • Price level rises, real output rises, unemployment falls.
  • Key pattern: after a demand shock, prices and output move in the same direction.
  • A rightward AD shift that raises the price level causes demand-pull inflation.
  • Real world: in 2008–09 banks cut lending and confidence collapsed. Real GDP fell in 2009 in the US, the UK, the euro area and Japan.
  • US unemployment reached 10.0% in October 2009, and the average level of US consumer prices was 0.4% lower in 2009 than in 2008 (BLS).

Mission 1

Supply shocks

  • Supply shock: a sudden, unexpected shift of SRAS, usually from a change in costs.
  • Negative: SRAS shifts left. Examples: a jump in oil or gas prices, a poor harvest, a jump in import prices.
    • Price level rises, real output falls, unemployment rises.
    • Rising prices and rising unemployment: stagflation.
  • Positive: SRAS shifts right (e.g. a fall in world commodity prices).
    • Price level falls, real output rises, unemployment falls.
  • Key pattern: after a supply shock, prices and output move in opposite directions.
  • A leftward SRAS shift that raises the price level causes cost-push inflation.
  • Real world: in 1973–74 oil exporters cut supply and the oil price roughly quadrupled. Many rich countries had rising inflation and rising unemployment.
  • US CPI inflation was 12.3% over 1974, and unemployment reached 9.0% in May 1975 (BLS).

  • Takeaway: a negative supply shock brings stagflation: higher prices and lower output at the same time.
Mission 1

Output gaps

  • Output gap: the difference between actual real output and potential (full-employment) output.
  • Recessionary gap (negative output gap): output below Yf. Inflationary gap (positive output gap): output above Yf.
  • The unemployment caused by a shortfall in AD is cyclical (demand-deficient) unemployment.
  • Output gap (%) = (actual − potential) ÷ potential × 100.
  • Say: "output below potential is a negative gap", even if output is still growing.

Mission 1

Back to the long run: automatic adjustment

  • Without policy, flexible wages and prices restore full employment in the long run.
  • Recessionary gap: unemployment is above the natural rate → nominal wages fall → costs fall → SRAS shifts right → back to LRAS at a lower price level.
  • Inflationary gap: unemployment is below the natural rate → nominal wages rise → SRAS shifts left → back to LRAS at a higher price level.
  • In the long run, output returns to full employment and unemployment to its natural rate.
  • How fast and how far this works.

Mission 2

Mission 2: What makes prices across a whole economy keep going up?

Make a guess: What makes prices across a whole economy keep going up?

Mission 2

Two routes to inflation

  • Inflation: a sustained rise in the general price level.
  • In the AD/AS model, the price level rises in two ways:
    • AD shifts right: spending pulls prices up → demand-pull inflation
    • SRAS shifts left: costs push prices up → cost-push inflation
  • One shift gives a one-off rise in the price level. Ongoing inflation needs the shift to keep happening.
  • Say: "inflation is a rising price level" — not: "inflation means prices are high".
Mission 2

Demand-pull inflation

  • Demand-pull: AD grows faster than the economy's capacity to produce.
  • "Too much money chasing too few goods."
  • What can push AD right: lower interest rates, easy credit, rising house prices (wealth), high confidence, an export boom, more government spending, tax cuts.
  • With spare capacity, extra AD mostly raises real output.
  • Near full capacity, firms cannot expand much, so extra AD mostly raises the price level.
  • AD1 → AD2 to the right: PL1 → PL2 and real GDP rises. Beyond Yf, this opens an inflationary gap.
  • Real world: in the late 1960s US government spending rose for the Vietnam War and new social programs. Unemployment fell to 3.5% in 1969, and CPI inflation rose from 1.6% (1965) to 5.5% (1969).
Quick check
?Interest rates are cut and house prices climb, so households borrow and spend more. The economy is already close to full capacity. Which best describes what happens in the short run?
Mission 2

Cost-push inflation

  • Cost-push: firms' costs rise, so they supply less at each price level. SRAS shifts left.
  • Sources of higher costs:
    • wages rising faster than productivity
    • energy and raw materials (commodity prices)
    • imported inputs (a weaker currency or more expensive world prices)
    • indirect taxes on inputs or goods
  • Result: the price level rises and real output falls. Unemployment rises: stagflation (see Mission 1).
  • SRAS1 → SRAS2 to the left: PL1 → PL2 and real GDP falls below Yf.
  • Real world: after the 1973 oil embargo, the oil price rose from 2.90 to 11.65 dollars a barrel. US CPI inflation was 12.3% over 1974.
  • Say: "costs shift SRAS left" — not: "costs shift AD".

Mission 3

Mission 3: If a slump ends by itself, why do some economists say we should not wait?

Make a guess: If a slump ends by itself, why do some economists say we should not wait?

Mission 3

A recessionary gap closes itself (in theory)

  • Start: AD has fallen. Output is below full employment; unemployment is above its natural rate.
  • With many people looking for work, nominal wages fall (and other input prices fall).
  • Lower costs → SRAS shifts right.
  • The price level falls, so more real output is bought (a movement down along AD).
  • This continues until SRAS meets AD on LRAS.
  • Long run: output back at full employment, unemployment back at its natural rate, price level lower.
  • Adjustment can be slow. Until it happens, output is lost and unemployment stays high, so governments and central banks may act instead.
  • Say: "SRAS shifts right as nominal wages fall" — not: "LRAS shifts".
Mission 3

An inflationary gap closes itself

  • Start: AD has risen. Output is above full employment; unemployment is below its natural rate.
  • Labor is scarce, and workers expect prices to keep rising: they win higher nominal wages.
  • Higher costs → SRAS shifts left.
  • The price level rises again; real output falls back.
  • Long run: output back at full employment, unemployment at its natural rate, price level higher than before the boom.
  • So a lasting rise in AD has no lasting effect on real output: only the price level is higher.
Quick check

Mission 4: Can an economy keep growing just by people spending more?

Make a guess: Can an economy keep growing just by people spending more?

Mission 4

What counts as growth

  • Economic growth: an increase in real GDP (real output) over time.
  • Rate of growth = % change in real GDP from one year to the next.
  • Nominal GDP is measured at current prices, so it rises with inflation. Real GDP removes price changes.
  • Total real GDP shows the size of the economy. Real GDP per capita (per person) is closer to average living standards.
  • Economic growth is measured as the growth rate of real GDP per capita.
  • Productivity: output per worker (or per hour worked).
  • Say: "real GDP rose by 2%" — not: "GDP rose by 2%", when you mean output.
Mission 4

Worked example: growth rates

  • Illustrative data.
Year 1Year 2
Nominal GDP500 bn540 bn
Real GDP (year 1 prices)500 bn515 bn
Population50 m51 m
  • Real growth = (515 − 500) / 500 × 100 = 3.0%.
  • Real GDP per capita: 500 bn / 50 m = 10,000 → 515 bn / 51 m = 10,098.
  • Growth per capita = (10,098 − 10,000) / 10,000 × 100 = 0.98%.
  • Population grew too, so output per person grew much less than total output.
  • Small rates compound. Real GDP per capita doubles in about 35 years at 2% a year, about 10 years at 7%, about 8 years at 9%.
  • After 35 years, 2% a year makes output per person about 2 times as big; 7% a year makes it about 10.7 times as big.
Quick check
?Illustrative data: a country's real GDP rises from 800 bn to 820 bn in a year. Its population rises from 40 million to 41 million. What is the growth rate of real GDP per capita?
Mission 4

Actual and potential growth on a PPC

  • Actual growth: a rise in the output the economy actually produces (real GDP).
  • Potential growth: a rise in the economy's productive capacity: the most it could produce with all resources fully and efficiently used.
  • On a PPC:
    • point A inside the curve: unemployed resources (spare capacity)
    • A → B, onto the curve: actual growth, using spare capacity
    • PPC1 → PPC2, shifting outward: potential growth
  • Once the economy is on its PPC, output can only keep rising if the PPC shifts out.
  • Long-run growth is shown by an outward shift of the PPC.
  • Say: "potential growth: productive capacity increases" — not: "the economy produces more".
Mission 4

Long-run growth: LRAS shifts right

  • Long-run growth: productive capacity rises, so LRAS shifts right: LRAS1 → LRAS2.
  • Full-employment output rises: Yf1 → Yf2. This is the same event as the PPC shifting out.
  • With AD unchanged, real output rises and the price level falls.
  • LRAS sits at full-employment output; a rightward LRAS shift shows economic growth.
  • Long-run growth is what lets output keep rising year after year without rising inflation.

Mission 4

Causes of growth

  • Short run (actual growth): a rise in AD, when there is spare capacity: C, I, G or (X − M).
  • Long run (potential growth): more or better factors of production.
    • Quantity: a bigger labor force (migration, more people in work); more capital (investment).
    • Quality: education and training (human capital); new technology.
    • Institutions: property rights, rule of law, stable government; good infrastructure.
  • Investment does both: it raises AD now and capacity later.
  • Supply-side policies that shift LRAS: education and training, infrastructure, support for research, tax incentives for investment, policies that bring more people into work.
  • Aggregate production function: real GDP depends on labor, physical capital, human capital, technology and natural resources.
  • Labor productivity (output per employed worker) rises with more physical capital per worker, more human capital per worker and better technology.
  • Crowding out: government borrowing raises the real interest rate. Short run: interest-sensitive spending (investment) falls. Long run: less capital accumulation, so slower growth.
Mission 4

Worked example: productivity

  • Illustrative data.
  • Real GDP = 1,000 bn; workers = 25 m.
  • Labor productivity = 1,000 bn / 25 m = 40,000 per worker.
  • Next year, more capital per worker lifts productivity 3%: 40,000 × 1.03 = 41,200.
  • Same 25 m workers: real GDP = 25 m × 41,200 = 1,030 bn (+3%).
  • If the number of workers also rises 1% to 25.25 m: real GDP = 25.25 m × 41,200 = 1,040.3 bn.
  • Growth comes from more workers or more output per worker. Only productivity growth raises output per worker.
Mission check

?An economy is at full capacity: unemployment is at its natural rate and firms are working at full capacity. The government wants output to keep rising over the next ten years. Which policy is most likely to achieve this?

Spending can only use the capacity an economy has. Lasting growth needs that capacity to grow.

Mission 5: When a slump hits, can a government spend its way out of it?

Make a guess: When a slump hits, can a government spend its way out of it?

Mission 5

What fiscal policy is

  • Fiscal policy: the use of government spending and taxation to influence the economy.
  • AP: government purchases, taxes and transfer payments, used to influence AD, real GDP, employment and the price level.
  • Main aim: steady AD, so output stays close to potential: growth, jobs, low inflation.
  • Budget deficit: spending > tax revenue in a year. Budget surplus: tax revenue > spending.
  • National debt: the total stock of past borrowing still owed.
  • Illustrative data: debt 1,000 bn; deficit of 50 bn this year → debt 1,050 bn. A surplus of 10 bn next year → debt 1,040 bn.
  • Say: "the deficit is a yearly flow; the debt is the stock" — not: "deficit and debt mean the same".
Mission 5

Where the money goes and where it comes from

  • Current spending: day-to-day items: public-sector wages, medicines, school supplies.
  • Capital spending: investment that lasts: roads, hospitals, broadband.
  • Transfer payments: pensions and benefits; nothing is produced in return, so they are not part of G in GDP.
  • Revenue: taxes (on income, profits, spending), plus charges for state-provided goods and services and sales of state assets.
  • AP: government purchases (G) count in AD directly; transfers change disposable income and so affect C.
Mission 5

Expansionary and contractionary fiscal policy

  • Expansionary: raise G and/or cut taxes → AD shifts right.
  • Used to close a recessionary gap: real GDP and employment rise; PL rises.
  • Contractionary: cut G and/or raise taxes → AD shifts left.
  • Used to close an inflationary gap: inflationary pressure eases.
  • Multiplier: the final shift in AD is larger than the first change in G or taxes.

Mission 5

Worked example: spending vs tax multipliers

  • Recessionary gap: real GDP is $200 billion below full-employment output. MPC = 0.8, MPS = 0.2. Assume the price level is constant (the maximum effect).
  • Spending multiplier = 1 ÷ 0.2 = 5. Needed rise in G = 200 ÷ 5 = $40 billion.
  • Tax multiplier = −0.8 ÷ 0.2 = −4. Needed tax cut = 200 ÷ 4 = $50 billion.
  • A tax cut needs to be bigger: households save part of it before spending.
  • Inflationary gap: do the reverse: cut G or raise taxes.
  • Say: "the spending multiplier is larger than the tax multiplier (in absolute value)".
Quick check
?There is an inflationary gap of $90 billion. The MPC is 0.75. Using taxes only, and assuming the price level is constant, what should the government do?
Mission 5

Time lags and other limits

  • Automatic stabilizers: in a recession, tax revenue falls and benefit spending rises with no new decision, so AD falls less. In a boom, the reverse.
  • Discretionary policy: the government chooses to change spending or tax rates.
  • Time lags: spotting the problem, deciding, passing it into law, then waiting for it to work.
  • AP: lags come from the time needed for decision and implementation.
  • A stimulus that arrives late may push up demand when recovery has already begun.
Mission 5

Strengths and a real case

  • Strengths: fiscal policy can target sectors or regions (e.g. house building, low-income households).
  • It can work when interest rates are already near zero and monetary policy has little room left: a deep recession.
  • US 2009: the American Recovery and Reinvestment Act combined spending, tax cuts and transfers, estimated at about $787 billion when passed (CBO).
  • Economists disagree on how much such measures added to output.
  • Estimates of the multiplier vary.
Mission 5

Fiscal and monetary policy together

  • Combined expansionary policy: higher G or lower taxes and an expansionary monetary policy (the Fed lowers interest rates).
  • AD shifts right by more: real GDP and employment rise, PL rises.
  • Effect on the interest rate is indeterminate: fiscal expansion tends to raise it; monetary expansion lowers it.
  • Combined contractionary policy: AD shifts left; real GDP and PL fall.
Quick check
?A government's budget deficit falls from 50 billion last year to 30 billion this year. What happened to the national debt this year, other things equal?

Mission 6

Mission 6: Why does a government's borrowing jump in a recession even when nobody votes for it?

Make a guess: Why does a government's borrowing jump in a recession even when nobody votes for it?

Mission 6

Two ways the budget changes

  • Discretionary fiscal policy: a deliberate decision to change tax rates or government spending. E.g. a budget that cuts income tax or funds new roads.
  • Automatic stabilizers: changes in tax revenue and transfer spending that happen on their own as the economy moves through the trade cycle (also called the business cycle). No new decision is needed.
  • Tax rates and benefit rules stay the same. What changes is how much income, spending and unemployment there is.
  • AP: automatic stabilizers are taxes and transfers that change with real GDP and moderate recessions and expansions, e.g. progressive income taxes and unemployment insurance (unemployment benefits).
Mission 6

How stabilizers work

In a recession:

  • Incomes fall → income tax paid falls. Spending falls → sales taxes (VAT) collected fall.
  • With a progressive tax, tax falls proportionally more than income.
  • Unemployment rises → unemployment benefit payments rise.
  • So disposable income falls by less than income. Consumption, and AD, fall by less.

Illustrative data:

  • Income tax: 0% on the first 10,000 · 20% from 10,000 to 50,000 · 40% above 50,000.
  • Income falls from 60,000 to 40,000 (−33%). Tax falls from 12,000 to 6,000 (−50%).
  • In a boom, the reverse: tax rises faster than income and benefit spending falls. This slows the rise in AD.
  • Say: "automatic stabilizers reduce the size of fluctuations" — not: "they stop recessions".

Mission 6

Stabilizers and the deficit

  • Lower tax revenue + higher benefit spending = a bigger deficit in a recession, with no new decision.
  • In a boom the deficit shrinks (or a surplus grows) automatically.
  • AP example: in fiscal year 2009 the US federal deficit reached about 10% of GDP. Part was automatic (falling tax receipts, rising unemployment insurance payments). Part was discretionary: Congress passed a stimulus (the American Recovery and Reinvestment Act) of about $800 billion.
Mission 6

Automatic vs discretionary: strengths and limits

Automatic stabilizers:

  • Fast: no time lag for recognizing the problem or passing a law.
  • Work in both directions: they cushion recessions and cool booms.
  • But partial: they reduce fluctuations; they do not remove them.

Discretionary policy:

  • Can be larger and targeted (e.g. at infrastructure or at low-income households).
  • But faces time lags: recognizing the problem, deciding, then putting it into effect. It may arrive after recovery has begun.
  • Can face political pressure: tax cuts and spending rises are popular; reversing them in a boom is not.
  • AP: discretionary fiscal policy needs action by Congress and the President, which takes time; automatic stabilizers act without new legislation.
Mission check

?Country A has a progressive income tax and pays unemployment benefits. Country B charges every adult the same fixed amount of tax and pays no unemployment benefits. Both suffer the same fall in exports. Neither government takes any new decision. Which statement is correct?

Automatic stabilizers cushion AD in a downturn, and the price is a larger cyclical deficit.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Short-run equilibrium: AD = SRAS. Long-run equilibrium: on LRAS, at full employment and the natural rate of unemployment.
  • Demand shocks move the price level and real output the same way; supply shocks move them opposite ways (stagflation).
  • Output below Yf is a recessionary gap; above it, an inflationary gap.

Mission 2

  • Demand-pull: AD shifts right faster than capacity; prices and real output rise together.
  • Cost-push: higher costs shift SRAS left; prices rise while output falls.

Mission 3

  • Without policy, flexible wages shift SRAS until the economy is back on LRAS: output at full employment, unemployment at its natural rate.

Mission 4

  • Economic growth is an increase in real GDP.
  • Growth is measured as the growth rate of real GDP per capita.
  • Actual growth uses spare capacity (AD); potential growth raises capacity (PPC shifts out, LRAS shifts right).
  • Long-run growth comes from more or better factors: labor, capital, human capital, technology, institutions.

Mission 5

  • Fiscal policy uses government spending and taxes (and transfers) to influence AD, and some measures also raise AS.
  • Expansionary policy shifts AD right to close a negative (recessionary) gap; contractionary policy shifts AD left to close a positive (inflationary) gap.
  • Spending multiplier 1 ÷ MPS is larger than the tax multiplier −MPC ÷ MPS.
  • Deficit = yearly flow; debt = stock. Lags, debt and spare capacity limit effectiveness.

Mission 6

  • Discretionary policy = a deliberate decision. Automatic stabilizers = tax and transfers that change with the cycle, with no decision.
  • In a recession, tax revenue falls and benefit spending rises: AD falls by less, the deficit rises.
