Mission 1

Three parts: TaNaKh

  • Tenakh is an acronym: Torah + Nevi'im + Ketuvim. 24 books in the Jewish count, mostly in Hebrew.
  • Torah (teaching, instruction): Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, Deuteronomy. The heart of the Tenakh.
  • Nevi'im (Prophets): Joshua to Kings (history); Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel and the Twelve.
  • Ketuvim (Writings): Psalms, Proverbs, Job, the five Megillot (scrolls, e.g. Esther, Ruth), Daniel, Ezra–Nehemiah, Chronicles.
  • "Torah" can also mean all Jewish teaching, written and oral.
  • Your spec spells it Tanakh.
  • Your spec writes G-d, a form many Jews use out of respect.
Mission 1

Given at Sinai

  • The Torah's account: after the Exodus, Israel camps at Mount Sinai (Exodus 19). God makes a covenant with the people.
  • God speaks the Ten Commandments (Exodus 20); Moses receives the rest of the teaching.
  • The people promise to do all God has said (Exodus 24:7); the rabbis read the Hebrew order, "we will do" before "we will hear", as accepting before fully understanding.
  • Moses writes the Torah and gives it to the priests and elders (Deuteronomy 31:9).
  • Revelation is to a whole people, not one individual: the root of Jewish identity.
  • 613 mitzvot (commandments) are counted in the Torah.

Mission 1Diversity

Word of God or inspired word?

  • Orthodox: Torah min ha-shamayim ("Torah from heaven"). God gave the whole Torah to Moses; every letter is divine, complete, unchangeable. Maimonides' 8th Principle.
  • "Ye shall not add unto the word which I command you, neither shall ye diminish from it" (Deuteronomy 4:2, JPS 1917).
  • Reform and Liberal: the Torah is inspired by God but written by humans over centuries; it records Israel's encounter with God. Ethical teaching binds; ritual laws may change.
  • Modern scholarship (e.g. the theory of several sources behind the Torah) shaped this view.
  • Masorti (Conservative): divine in origin, passed on and shaped by humans; law still binding but developing.
  • All treat the Torah as holy and central.

Mission 2

A text that needs explaining

  • The written Torah gives commands without details:
  • "thou shalt not do any manner of work" on the Sabbath (Exodus 20:10, JPS 1917): what counts as work? (The Mishnah later lists 39 categories of work, such as kindling a fire, writing and building: Shabbat 7:2.)
  • "thou shalt bind them for a sign upon thy hand" (Deuteronomy 6:8, JPS 1917): how? (the answer: tefillin).
  • Slaughter "as I have commanded thee" (Deuteronomy 12:21, JPS 1917): where was that commanded? Nowhere in writing.
  • Rabbinic Judaism teaches that God gave Moses an oral Torah with the written one: explanations, methods and laws passed on by word of mouth.
  • Together: Torah she-bikhtav (written) and Torah she-be'al peh (oral).
Mission 2Read the source

The chain of transmission

> "Write thou these words, for after the tenor of these words I have made a covenant with thee and with Israel." (Exodus 34:27, JPS 1917)

  • The Hebrew for "after the tenor of" is literally "by the mouth of": the Talmud reads this as a hint of an oral covenant too (Gittin 60b).
  • Pirkei Avot 1:1 (paraphrase): Moses received Torah from Sinai and handed it to Joshua; Joshua to the elders; the elders to the prophets; the prophets to the Men of the Great Assembly.
  • Then pairs of sages, down to Hillel and Shammai (1st century BCE): order gives authority.

Mission 2

The Mishnah

  • Why written down: after the Temple's destruction (70 CE) and the failed Bar Kokhba revolt (132–135), sages feared the oral teaching would be lost.
  • Edited c. 200 CE by Rabbi Judah ha-Nasi in the land of Israel, in Hebrew.
  • Content: six orders (Shas): Zera'im (seeds, farming), Mo'ed (festivals), Nashim (women, marriage), Nezikin (damages, civil law), Kodashim (holy things), Tohorot (purity); 63 tractates.
  • Style: short, arranged by topic not by verse; records disputes and minority opinions by name.
  • The teachers of the Mishnah are the Tannaim.
  • Importance: the first authoritative code of the oral Torah; studied as the base text of the Talmud.
Mission 2

The Gemara

  • Gemara: the discussion of the Mishnah by later sages, the Amoraim (c. 200–500 CE), mostly in Aramaic.
  • Style: a debate on paper: question, answer, objection, proof from scripture, digressions. Often no final ruling.
  • Content: halakhah (law) and aggadah (stories, ethics, theology, history).
  • Talmud = Mishnah + Gemara.
  • A printed page: Mishnah and Gemara in the centre; Rashi's commentary on the inner side; Tosafot on the outer side.
  • Importance: it teaches how to reason about law, not only what the law is.

Mission 2

Two Talmuds

  • Jerusalem Talmud (Yerushalmi): from the academies of Galilee (e.g. Tiberias); completed c. 400 CE; Western Aramaic; shorter and less edited; covers the farming laws of the land of Israel.
  • Babylonian Talmud (Bavli): from the academies of Sura and Pumbedita; main editing c. 500 CE (Rav Ashi and Ravina), with later polishing; Eastern Aramaic; much longer, fuller aggadah.
  • The Bavli became the Talmud: Babylonia's academies led the Jewish world for centuries; later authorities ruled it decisive where the two disagree.
  • Origins and transmission: oral discussion over three centuries, edited, then copied by hand; first fully printed in Venice (1520–23).

Mission 2

From Talmud to Shulkhan Arukh

  • Halakhah ("the way to walk"): Jewish law, the practical result of the oral Torah.
  • Geonim (c. 600–1040): heads of the Babylonian academies answered questions by letter (responsa).
  • Maimonides, Mishneh Torah (c. 1180): a complete code in clear Hebrew.
  • Jacob ben Asher, Arba'ah Turim (14th century): code in four sections.
  • Joseph Karo, Shulkhan Arukh ("set table"; printed 1565): the standard code, following Sephardi practice. Moses Isserles added Ashkenazi customs (the Mappah, "tablecloth").
  • Today: rabbis still write responsa on new questions, such as electricity on Shabbat or medical ethics.
  • Importance today: for Orthodox Jews, halakhah from written and oral Torah shapes every part of daily life.

Mission 2Diversity

Whose authority?

  • Orthodox: the oral Torah is divine revelation given at Sinai with the written Torah; the Talmud is binding as rabbis apply it. Many study a page a day (daf yomi), finishing the Talmud every seven and a half years.
  • Masorti (Conservative): authoritative, but developed over history; law can change by rabbinic process.
  • Reform and Liberal: human wisdom from inspired teachers; valuable, not binding; individual informed choice.
  • Karaites (from the 8th century): reject the oral Torah altogether, following the written text only.
  • Challenges: historians trace the Talmud's growth over centuries; the Talmud itself records disagreement. Orthodox reply: disagreement belongs inside one revealed tradition.
Mission 2Evaluate

Is the oral Torah divine revelation?

  • Claim: The oral Torah has the same divine authority as the written Torah.
  • For: the written Torah cannot be kept without it (tefillin, Shabbat); the chain of transmission (Avot 1:1); Exodus 34:27.
  • For: Orthodox Jews hold both were given at Sinai.
  • Against: historians see it as developing over centuries; Karaites reject it; the Talmud records many human disagreements.
  • Against: Reform Jews treat it as human wisdom, not binding.
  • Judge it by: Does "divine" require dictation, or can inspired interpretation count (Bava Metzia 59b)? Which community's view?

Mission 3

A tractate about character

  • Pirkei Avot ("Chapters of the Fathers"; also "Ethics of the Fathers"): a tractate of the Mishnah (c. 200 CE).
  • Unlike the rest of the Mishnah, it has almost no law: it collects sayings of the sages about wisdom, study, character and how to treat others.
  • It sits in the order Nezikin ("damages"): civil and criminal law, courts and judges.
  • Why there? Many explain: judges and those who apply law need good character. The Talmud says whoever wants to be pious should keep the laws of Nezikin; another sage says, "the matters of Avot" (Bava Kamma 30a, paraphrase).
  • Many communities study a chapter of Avot on Shabbat afternoons in the summer.
Mission 3Read the source

Mission 3

What the world stands on

  • Simeon the Righteous (1:2): the world stands on three things: Torah (study), avodah (worship; once the Temple service) and gemilut chasadim (acts of loving-kindness).
  • Rabban Simeon ben Gamaliel (1:18): the world endures on three things: justice, truth and peace, citing "execute the judgment of truth and peace in your gates" (Zechariah 8:16, JPS 1917).
  • So the chapter begins with study and worship and ends with justice in society.
  • Ethics is not an extra: it holds the world up.

Mission 3

Sayings of the sages

All paraphrases from Avot 1:

  • Antigonus (1:3): do not serve God like servants working for a reward.
  • Joshua ben Perachiah (1:6): get yourself a teacher, gain a friend, and judge everyone favourably.
  • Hillel (1:12): be a disciple of Aaron: love peace, pursue peace, love people and bring them near to Torah.
  • Hillel (1:14): If I am not for myself, who will be for me? If I am only for myself, what am I? If not now, when?
  • Shammai (1:15): make study a fixed habit; say little and do much; greet everyone cheerfully.
  • Simeon (1:17): not study but action is the main thing.
Mission 3

Law, ethics and the nature of Torah

  • Torah is more than the written text: Avot 1 assumes an oral Torah passed through teachers, so interpretation is part of revelation.
  • Authority by transmission: the chain names each link; the order shows authority passing from prophets to sages (see Mission 2).
  • Law: "a fence around the Torah" grounds rabbinic rules that protect commandments (e.g. not handling tools on Shabbat).
  • Ethics: most sayings concern character: humility, generosity, fair judgement, peace.
  • Interpretation: study must lead to action (1:17); motive matters (1:3).
  • So Torah, as Avot presents it, joins law and ethics in one way of life.
Mission 3

Gemilut chasadim

  • Gemilut chasadim: acts of loving-kindness, one of the three pillars (Avot 1:2).
  • Greater than charity (Sukkah 49b, paraphrase): charity uses money, kindness uses body and money; charity is for the poor, kindness for rich and poor; charity is for the living, kindness for the living and the dead.
  • Examples: visiting the sick (bikur cholim), hospitality, comforting mourners, burying the dead, free loans.
  • Imitating God: God clothed Adam, visited Abraham, comforted Isaac, buried Moses: Jews are to do the same (Sotah 14a, paraphrase).

Is ethics, not law, the heart of Torah?

  • Claim: Pirkei Avot shows that ethics, not law, is the heart of Torah.
  • For: most sayings are about character; action and kindness hold the world up (1:2, 1:17, 1:18).

Mission 4

A covenant: a binding bond

  • A covenant (Hebrew berit) is a solemn, binding agreement between two parties, sealed by an oath or a sign.
  • In the Torah, God makes covenants with people: Noah, Abraham, then all Israel at Sinai.
  • Abraham (first called Abram) leaves Haran for Canaan at God's call (Genesis 12).
  • Jews call him Avraham Avinu, "Abraham our father": the first patriarch, father of the Jewish people.

Land, offspring, a chosen people

  • Faith: "he believed in the LORD; and He counted it to him for righteousness" (Genesis 15:6, JPS 1917).

Mission 4

Two signs: cut animals and circumcision

  • Hebrew says one "cuts" a covenant (karat berit).
  • Genesis 15: Abram cuts a heifer, a she-goat and a ram in half; at night "a smoking furnace, and a flaming torch" pass "between these pieces" (Genesis 15:17, JPS 1917). Only God passes through: God alone takes the oath.
  • Ancient parallel (often cited; compare Jeremiah 34:18): passing between cut animals invited the same fate if the oath were broken.
  • Genesis 17: God, as El Shaddai ("God Almighty"), renames Abram Abraham ("father of a multitude") and Sarai Sarah.
  • The sign is circumcision: "it shall be a token of a covenant betwixt Me and you" (Genesis 17:11, JPS 1917), on the eighth day (17:12).
  • Genesis 17 adds a demand: "walk before Me, and be thou wholehearted" (17:1). Genesis 15 is a promise; Genesis 17 asks for a sign kept by every generation.
Mission 4

The shape of a covenant

  • Scholars compare biblical covenants with Ancient Near Eastern treaties (Hittite, 2nd millennium BCE; Neo-Assyrian, 7th century BCE).
  • Parity treaty: between equals. Suzerainty treaty: a great king (suzerain) binds a lesser king (vassal).
  • Usual parts: speaker named (preamble) · history of past kindness · requirements (stipulations) · witnesses (gods) · curses and blessings.
  • George E. Mendenhall (1916–2016), Law and Covenant in Israel and the Ancient Near East (1955), applied the suzerainty model to Sinai.
  • The Abrahamic covenant fits a royal grant better: a king rewards a loyal servant with land and a lasting house, with few conditions (Moshe Weinfeld, 1925–2009, article on "covenant of grant", 1970).
  • So Genesis 15 is close to unconditional promise; Genesis 17 adds a requirement (circumcision). No witnesses or curses: God swears by himself (compare 22:16).
Mission 4

Who wrote it, when, and why?

  • Traditional Jewish view: the Torah was given to Moses by God (Torah min ha-shamayim); Genesis records real events in Abraham's life (dated by many to c. 1800 BCE).
  • Documentary hypothesis (Julius Wellhausen, 1844–1918, Prolegomena, 1883): Genesis weaves sources. Genesis 15 is usually assigned to older narrative sources (J, with some E); Genesis 17 to P (the Priestly source, often dated to the exile, 6th century BCE).
  • Theological purpose: for exiles in Babylon, an "everlasting covenant" with a sign kept anywhere (circumcision) and a promise of land offered hope of return.
  • Philip R. Davies (1945–2018), In Search of 'Ancient Israel' (1992): "biblical Israel" is largely a literary construct of the Persian period; the patriarchs are literary figures, not recoverable history.
  • Bernhard W. Anderson (1916–2007), The Living World of the Old Testament: reads the patriarchal stories as faith traditions with a historical setting.
  • Dan Cohn-Sherbok (b. 1945), Judaism: History, Belief and Practice (2003): sets traditional and critical accounts side by side.

Mission 5

The Ten Commandments

  • Hebrew: Aseret ha-Dibrot, "the ten words" or "ten statements" (compare 34:28, "the ten words").
  • Two stone tablets, traditionally five statements on each: the first five (God, no idols, God's name, Shabbat, honouring parents) are linked with duties to God, the second five (murder, adultery, theft, false witness, coveting) with duties to people.
  • Jewish count: the first statement is "I am the LORD thy God, who brought thee out of the land of Egypt" (Exodus 20:2, JPS 1917). Belief in God comes first.
  • Most Christians count differently: none counts "I am the LORD thy God" as a separate statement. Catholics and Lutherans take it with "no other gods" and "no idols" as the first, and split coveting in two; most Protestants and the Orthodox make "no other gods" the first and "no idols" the second.
  • They are 10 of the 613 mitzvot. Medieval rabbis (e.g. Saadia Gaon) saw all 613 as grouped under the ten.
  • The rabbis removed the daily reading of the Ten Commandments in the synagogue so that no one would claim only these ten came from Sinai (Talmud, Berakhot 12a).

Mission 5

Written, broken, renewed

  • Recorded: "Moses wrote all the words of the LORD" (Exodus 24:4, JPS 1917), built an altar and 12 pillars for the 12 tribes.
  • Ratified: he read the "book of the covenant"; the people said "will we do, and obey" (24:7: na'aseh ve-nishma).
  • Sign of blood: "Behold the blood of the covenant, which the LORD hath made with you" (Exodus 24:8, JPS 1917): half the blood on the altar, half on the people. Both parties bound.
  • Failure: the golden calf (Exodus 32). Moses breaks the tablets (32:19).
  • Renewed: "Hew thee two tables of stone like unto the first" (34:1); God tells Moses, "Write thou these words" (34:27); the tablets bear "the words of the covenant, the ten words" (34:28).

Mission 5

The shape of a treaty

  • George E. Mendenhall (1916–2016), Law and Covenant in Israel and the Ancient Near East (1955): Sinai follows the Hittite suzerainty treaty.
  • Speaker (preamble): "I am the LORD thy God" (20:2a).
  • History of past kindness: "who brought thee out of the land of Egypt" (20:2b).
  • Requirements: the commandments (20:3–17).
  • Deposit and reading: the tablets in the ark; the book read aloud (24:7).
  • Witnesses: none named in Exodus 20 (no gods can witness); some see the 12 pillars (24:4) in this role; Deuteronomy calls "heaven and earth to witness" (Deuteronomy 30:19, JPS 1917).
  • Curses and blessings: brief in Exodus 20 (20:5–6, 20:12); full lists in Leviticus 26 and Deuteronomy 28.
  • Parity vs suzerainty: Sinai is between unequal parties: a suzerainty pattern, unlike the near-unconditional grant to Abraham (see Mission 4).
  • Critics: Dennis J. McCarthy (Treaty and Covenant, 1963) found the full treaty pattern in Deuteronomy, not in Exodus 19–24; others (e.g. Moshe Weinfeld) link Deuteronomy to Neo-Assyrian treaties of the 7th century BCE.
Mission 5

Date, authorship, purpose

  • Traditional view: God gave the Torah to Moses at Sinai (Maimonides' 8th Principle). Orthodox Judaism holds this.
  • Critical scholarship: Exodus 19–24 combines sources: older narrative strands (J and E), Priestly framing, and the Covenant Code (Exodus 20:22–23:33), perhaps 8th century BCE or older.
  • Purpose: for later Israel, and for exiles, Sinai explains why Israel lost the land (covenant broken) and how to live as God's people anywhere (law).
  • Philip R. Davies (1945–2018), In Search of 'Ancient Israel' (1992): Sinai is a foundation story written much later, in the Persian period, to shape a community's identity.
  • Bernhard W. Anderson (1916–2007), The Living World of the Old Testament: the covenant is the centre of Israel's faith, whatever the date of the texts.
  • Conservative and Reform Judaism often accept critical findings yet treat Sinai as real revelation (see Mission 1).
Mission 5Diversity

Sinai today

  • Orthodox: the covenant is binding law (halakhah): all applicable mitzvot, as interpreted by the oral Torah. Revelation at Sinai is historical.
  • Conservative (Masorti): halakhah binding, but it develops; Sinai as an ongoing revelation through interpretation.
  • Reform / Liberal: the moral law (e.g. the Ten Commandments) binds; ritual laws are chosen where they bring meaning. Classical Reform's Pittsburgh Platform (1885) accepted "only the moral laws" as binding; later Reform statements (1999) value more ritual practice.
  • Shavuot celebrates the giving of the Torah; the Ten Commandments are read in the synagogue, and the congregation stands.
Mission 5Evaluate

Is the Sinai covenant mainly a legal contract?

  • Claim: The Sinai covenant is above all a legal agreement.
  • For: It has stated requirements, a written record and sanctions, like a legal agreement.
  • For: Its parts match a suzerainty treaty (Mendenhall).
  • For: It is the source of halakhah: Jewish law still applies it in courts (beit din) and daily life.
  • Against: It begins with rescue and love ("eagles' wings"), not a contract; obedience answers grace.
  • Against: Prophets later describe the covenant as a marriage (Hosea 2:21–22 in Jewish numbering).
  • Against: Some say covenant could become a tool of religious control (law enforced by priests); others answer that the people freely consent (24:7).
  • Judgement criteria: What does "legal" mean: binding, or impersonal? Do Orthodox and Reform Jews experience it the same way?
  • Also ask: does the treaty form fit Exodus, or only Deuteronomy?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The Tenakh = Torah, Nevi'im, Ketuvim; the Torah tells of its own giving at Sinai through Moses, within a covenant.
  • Orthodox Jews hold the Torah is the complete, unchangeable Word of God (Torah min ha-shamayim); Reform and Liberal Jews see it as inspired but humanly written.

Mission 2

  • The oral Torah explains the written Torah; Pirkei Avot 1:1 traces its chain from Moses to the Men of the Great Assembly.
  • Mishnah (Hebrew, c. 200 CE, six orders) + Gemara (Aramaic discussion) = Talmud; the Babylonian Talmud became more authoritative than the Jerusalem Talmud.
  • Halakhah developed through the Geonim, Maimonides and the Tur to the Shulkhan Arukh (1565); Orthodox, Masorti and Reform Jews give the oral Torah different authority.

Mission 3

  • Pirkei Avot, a Mishnah tractate in Nezikin, opens with the chain of transmission and collects the sages' ethical sayings.
  • The world stands on Torah, worship and gemilut chasadim (1:2), and endures on justice, truth and peace (1:18).

Mission 4

  • In Genesis 12, 15 and 17 God promises Abraham land, offspring and a blessing for all families of the earth; Abraham responds with trust and the sign of circumcision.
  • The covenant resembles a royal grant more than a suzerainty treaty; Genesis 15 and 17 are often assigned to different sources with different purposes.

Mission 5

  • In the Exodus account, God, who had rescued Israel, offers a covenant at Sinai: "if" Israel keeps it, they will be a treasured, holy nation; the people agree (Exodus 19:8).
  • The Ten Commandments begin, in Jewish counting, with "I am the LORD thy God".
  • Moses wrote the covenant down, the people agreed again (24:7), and it was renewed after the golden calf (Exodus 24; 32; 34).
  • Sinai is the defining covenant of law, unlike the near-unconditional promise to Abraham; scholars compare its form with suzerainty treaties.
