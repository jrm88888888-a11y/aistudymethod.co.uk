Mission 1

What is a biome?

  • A biome is "a community of plants and animals living together in a certain kind of climate". Biomes are large-scale ecosystems.
  • Climate (temperature and rainfall) does the most to decide which biome grows where.
Mission 1

The world map of biomes

  • Biomes form rough bands across the map, by latitude.
  • Near the equator: tropical forest. Either side: tropical grassland, then hot desert.
  • Further from the equator: temperate grassland and temperate forest, then boreal forest, then tundra and polar ice.
Mission 1

Why the bands? Rising and sinking air

  • The equator gets more direct sunlight; the poles get less.
  • Near the equator warm air rises in the ITCZ (Inter-Tropical Convergence Zone), "forming thunderstorms": heavy rain all year feeds rainforest.
  • The air sinks over the subtropics (about 30°), "creating high-pressure regions over … deserts like the Sahara". Sinking air warms and dries, so clouds cannot form.
  • Near the poles there is "little or no solar energy in the winter", and the sun is at a low angle: cold biomes.
Quick check
?Why are many of the world's hot deserts found around 30° north and south?
Mission 1

Tropical rainforest

  • Where: between the Tropics of Cancer and Capricorn (e.g. Amazon, Congo).
  • Climate: hot all year (at Manaus, Brazil, every month averages 27–28 °C); 2,000–10,000 mm of rain a year.
  • Plants: giant trees up to 75 m form the canopy; vines, ferns and palms below.
  • Soil: leaf litter rots fast in the heat and wet, "sending nutrients back into the soil".
Mission 1

Hot deserts

  • Where: around 30° N and S under sinking air (e.g. Sahara).
  • Rain: about 250 mm a year; most get under 300 mm.
  • Temperature: days average 38 °C; nights fall to about −3.9 °C, because dry air lets heat escape.
  • Plants: cacti store water in their stems; some plants lie dormant until rain comes.
Mission 1

Grasslands: tropical and temperate

  • Rain: about 500–900 mm a year: too little for dense forest.
  • Tropical grassland (savanna): warm all year with a wet and a dry season; between rainforest and desert.
  • Temperate grassland: cold winters, warm summers: prairies (North America), pampas (South America), steppes (Central Eurasia), veldt (South Africa).
  • Plants: grasses die back to their roots each year.
Mission 1

Temperate and boreal forests

  • Temperate deciduous forest (eastern USA, Europe, China, Japan): 750–1,500 mm of rain; average 10 °C; four seasons; leaves fall in winter.
  • Boreal (coniferous) forest: about 50–60° N; winters very cold, summers around 10 °C; 300–900 mm of rain.
  • Conifers are evergreen, with needles that survive cold and dry conditions.
Mission 1

Tundra and polar regions

  • Tundra: south of the Arctic ice caps, across North America, Europe and Siberia.
  • Below 0 °C for 6 to 10 months; only 150–250 mm of rain a year.
  • Permafrost (permanently frozen ground) and a short growing season mean almost no trees: mosses, lichens, grasses and shrubs.
  • Polar regions: little or no winter sun; some areas are so dry they are polar deserts.

Mission 2

Where are tropical rainforests?

  • Between the Tropic of Cancer and the Tropic of Capricorn, mostly close to the equator.
  • The largest areas: the Amazon (South America), the Congo Basin (Africa) and South-East Asia.
  • Describe the pattern: a broken belt either side of the equator, in lowlands with high rainfall all year.
Mission 2

The climate: hot and wet all year

  • Manaus, Brazil (1970–2000):
    • every month 27–28 °C: an annual range of only 1 °C
    • 2,323 mm of rain a year; even the driest month (August) has 68 mm
  • Rainforests typically get 2,000 mm or more of rain a year.
Mission 2

Why: the equatorial climate

  • Latitude: near the equator, the sun is overhead or nearly overhead all year, so heating is strong every month.
  • Hot air rises, creating low pressure; the trade winds meet and rise in the ITCZ, forming thunderstorms.
  • Convectional rainfall: the sun heats the ground → warm, moist air rises → cools → condenses → towering clouds → afternoon storms.
Mission 2

A day in an equatorial climate

  • Singapore, 1° north (1991–2020):
    • monthly mean temperature 26.8–28.6 °C
    • 2,113 mm of rain a year, on 171 days
  • Storms come mostly in the afternoon, when solar heating is strongest; clouds tower 9–12 km high.

Mission 2

Layers of the forest

  • Emergents: the tallest trees, standing above the rest.
  • Canopy: a dense roof of leaves that blocks most sunlight from reaching the ground. Woody vines climb trees to reach the light.
  • Under canopy: smaller trees, vines, ferns and palms in dim light.
  • Shrub layer and forest floor: dark, with few plants, covered in leaf litter.
Mission 2

Soils: latosols

  • Latosols are deep: warm, wet conditions weather the rock far down.
  • They are red, from iron oxides.
  • They are poor in nutrients: heavy rain washes nutrients down and out (leaching).
  • Only a thin top layer is rich, where leaf litter rots.
Mission 2

The nutrient cycle (Gersmehl model)

  • Nutrients are held in three stores: biomass (living things), litter (dead leaves) and soil.
  • Flows link them: leaf fall, decomposition, uptake by roots, plus leaching and runoff (out) and rain and weathered rock (in).
  • In a rainforest, the biomass store is largest. Litter rots fast in warm, wet conditions, and roots take the nutrients straight back up.
  • So the litter and soil stores are small.

Mission 2

The water cycle

  • The canopy intercepts rain; much evaporates back into the air.
  • Trees take up water and release it through their leaves (transpiration).
  • This moist air makes more rain: air that has passed over forest produces at least twice as much rain as air that has passed over little vegetation.
  • So the forest helps make its own rain: a regional-scale process.
Mission 2

The carbon cycle

  • Trees take in CO₂ by photosynthesis and store carbon in wood, roots and soil.
  • Amazonian trees take in and store more carbon than any other ecosystem on Earth.
  • When forest is burned or cleared, carbon returns to the air: south-eastern Amazonia now gives out more carbon than it takes in. A global-scale process.
Mission 2

Food webs

  • Producers: plants use sunlight to make food.
  • Consumers: e.g. sloths eat leaves and fruit; insects, birds and monkeys eat leaves, fruit and each other; snakes and eagles eat the animals.
  • Decomposers: fungi and bacteria break down dead matter, returning nutrients to the soil, and the cycle restarts.
Mission 2

Interdependence

  • Interdependence: the parts of an ecosystem depend on each other; change one and the others change.
  • Climate → plants: heat and rain all year let plants grow all year.
  • Plants → soil: leaf fall feeds the thin fertile layer; roots hold the soil.
  • Plants → water → climate: transpiration makes more rain.
  • Animals ↔ plants: animals eat fruit and spread seeds; plants give food and shelter.
  • People: forest peoples depend on it for food and materials; clearing breaks the links.
Mission 2

Biodiversity

  • Biodiversity: the variety of living things in an area.
  • About 60% of all vascular plant species are found in tropical forests.
  • Forests are home to 80% of known amphibian species, 75% of birds and 68% of mammals.
  • Amazonia alone has roughly 16,000 tree species.
Mission 2

Ecosystem services

  • Ecosystem services: benefits people get from ecosystems.
  • Water-cycle regulation: forests recycle moisture and help make rain.
  • Flood mitigation: forests reduce flooding from frequent, small storms, but cannot stop large catastrophic floods.
  • Carbon storage, medicines, food and timber.
Quick check
?Which statement best describes biodiversity?
Mission check

?Farmers clear a patch of rainforest and burn the trees. Crops grow well for two or three years, then fail. What best explains this?

In the Gersmehl model the rainforest's nutrients sit mainly in biomass; litter rots fast and is taken straight back up. Clear the trees and heavy rain leaches the thin, poor soil, so crops soon fail.

Mission 3: Coral reefs grow in clear seas with few nutrients, yet they teem with life. How?

Mission 3

Where coral reefs are found

  • Reef-building corals live in tropical and semi-tropical seas.
  • Most are in shallow waters between about 35°N and 35°S.
  • They need light, so most reef-building species live where light reaches, to about 30 m deep.
  • Large examples: the Great Barrier Reef (Australia), the Mesoamerican Reef (Caribbean), the Coral Triangle (South-east Asia).
Mission 3

The climate a reef needs

  • Warm water: corals cannot survive below 18 °C. Many grow best at 23–29 °C.
  • Salty water: 32–42 parts per thousand.
  • Clear water, so sunlight gets through.
  • So reefs grow in warm tropical seas, away from muddy river mouths.
  • Contrast with the rainforest: the reef's "climate" is mostly the sea: its temperature, saltiness and clarity.
Mission 3

What a reef is made of

  • A coral is a colony of tiny animals called polyps.
  • Hard corals build a skeleton of calcium carbonate (CaCO₃). Layer on layer, skeletons build the reef.
  • Reefs grow slowly: about 0.3–2 cm a year for massive corals, up to 10 cm a year for branching ones.
  • Three types: fringing (next to the shore), barrier (separated from land by a lagoon) and atoll (a ring around a lagoon).
  • Reefs cover only about 1% of the ocean but give habitat to at least 25% of marine life.
Quick check
?Which place is most likely to have a healthy coral reef?
Mission 3

A partnership inside the coral

  • Inside most reef-building corals live tiny algae called zooxanthellae.
  • This is mutualism: both partners gain.
  • The coral gives the algae shelter, plus the carbon dioxide and water they need for photosynthesis.
  • The algae give the coral sugars, oxygen and help with removing waste.
  • Up to 90% of the food the algae make passes to the coral.
Mission 3

The reef nutrient cycle

  • Tropical seas are nutrient-poor.
  • The reef survives by tight recycling: nutrients pass back and forth between coral and algae instead of being lost.
  • Polyps also catch zooplankton and small fish with stinging tentacles, mostly at night. This brings in extra nutrients.
  • Waste from fish and other animals is broken down by bacteria and reused by algae.
  • NOAA calls it a "paradox": reefs need nutrient-poor water, yet are "among the most productive and diverse marine environments on earth".
Quick check
?Why can coral reefs be so productive in water with few nutrients?
Mission 3

The coral reef food web

  • Producers: zooxanthellae, seaweed (algae) and phytoplankton make food from sunlight.
  • Primary consumers: zooplankton; grazing fish that eat seaweed; corals, which get sugars from their algae.
  • Secondary consumers: corals also eat zooplankton and small fish; small fish eat zooplankton.
  • Top predators: large fish and sharks.
  • Coral eaters: crown-of-thorns starfish eat coral; extra nutrients from farm runoff "can exacerbate outbreaks".
  • If grazing fish are removed, seaweed can spread; seaweed "competes with coral for space".
Quick check
?Fishing boats remove most of the grazing fish from a reef. What is most likely to happen next?
Mission 3

Reefs and the water cycle

  • Warm tropical seas lose a lot of water by evaporation.
  • Moist air rises, cools and forms clouds; rain falls on land and sea.
  • Rivers carry water back to the sea, with mud and nutrients from farms and towns.
  • After big storms, flood plumes of river water can reach reefs far offshore.
  • Fresh, muddy water lowers saltiness and blocks light, which corals need. So land and sea are linked through the water cycle.
Mission 3

Reefs and the carbon cycle

  • Zooxanthellae take in CO₂ for photosynthesis.
  • Corals lock carbon into calcium carbonate skeletons. Over long timescales, reef rock can become limestone: a long-term carbon store.
  • But building a skeleton also releases some CO₂ into the water, so a living reef does not simply soak up CO₂ from the air.
  • The ocean absorbs about 30% of the CO₂ released into the atmosphere.
  • Extra CO₂ makes seawater more acidic, so there are "fewer carbonate ions available for calcifying organisms" such as corals.
  • So rising CO₂ in the global atmosphere affects how well reefs can build.
Quick check
?How does extra carbon dioxide in the atmosphere affect coral reefs through the carbon cycle?
Mission check

?After a cyclone, a muddy river plume covers part of a reef for several weeks. The plume carries extra nutrients. Why might the corals still starve?

A reef thrives in nutrient-poor water because its corals and their algae recycle nutrients tightly and make food from sunlight. Take away the light and the whole system goes hungry.

Mission 4

What is an ecosystem?

  • An ecosystem is a community of living things and their environment, working together.
  • Biotic parts are living: plants, animals, fungi, bacteria.
  • Abiotic parts are non-living: sunlight, water, temperature, oxygen, nutrients, mud.
  • A small-scale ecosystem can be one pond or one hedgerow.
Mission 4

Our pond: Hampstead Heath

  • Hampstead Heath, north London: a large park with woodland, meadows, hedgerows and more than 30 lakes and ponds.
  • The Heath records 180 bird species and 18 species of dragonfly and damselfly.
  • Our example: the Model Boating Pond, with a small island used by mallard, coot, mute swan, tufted duck, moorhen and great crested grebe.
Mission 4

Producers, consumers, decomposers

  • Only the birds are recorded at this pond; the other species here are typical of ponds like it.
  • Producers make food by photosynthesis: algae, pondweed, reeds.
  • Primary consumers eat producers: water fleas, pond snails, tadpoles; coots also graze plants.
  • Secondary and tertiary consumers eat animals: dragonfly nymphs, small fish, then fish-eating birds such as the great crested grebe.
  • Decomposers break down dead matter and waste: bacteria and fungi.
Mission 4

Food chains

  • A food chain shows who eats whom; arrows point to the eater.
  • A typical pond chain: algae → water flea → dragonfly nymph → small fish → great crested grebe.
  • At each step energy is lost, so there are fewer animals higher up.
Quick check
?In the chain algae → water flea → dragonfly nymph → small fish → grebe, which is the primary consumer?
Mission 4

Food webs

  • Real animals eat more than one thing, so chains join into a food web.
  • Small fish eat water fleas and dragonfly nymphs; ducks eat plants and snails.
  • A web shows how a change in one species can spread to others.
Mission 4

Nutrient cycling

  • Plants take nutrients (e.g. nitrates, phosphates) from water and mud.
  • Animals get nutrients by eating plants or other animals.
  • Dead leaves, dead animals and droppings sink to the mud; decomposers break them down.
  • Nutrients are released back into the water for plants to reuse: a cycle.
Mission 4

People and the pond

  • The Heath's ponds are busy: swims at its lido and three bathing ponds rose from 200,000 (2020/21) to just under 620,000 (2023/24).
  • At the Model Boating Pond, people regularly crossed a fenced causeway to the island, disturbing birds.
  • Disturbance can stop birds feeding, resting and breeding, lowering biodiversity.
  • During bird flu, visitors are asked not to feed wild birds by hand.
Mission 4

Managing for wildlife

  • In 2026 the causeway was removed, creating 0.17 ha of undisturbed habitat.
  • 100 m² of new reedbed was added: a UK priority habitat.
  • Reeds change flows and cycles:
  • Water: wetland plants slow water movement and improve water quality.
  • Pollution: reeds trap pollutants, improving air quality around the pond.
  • Carbon: reeds absorb CO₂ and store it in stems, roots and soil.
Quick check
?How does the new reedbed change the pond's flows and cycles?
Mission check

?In a hedgerow: hawthorn leaves → caterpillars → blue tits → sparrowhawks. A farmer sprays insecticide that kills most of the caterpillars. What is most likely to happen to the sparrowhawks?

In any small-scale ecosystem, producers, consumers and decomposers are linked by food webs and nutrient cycles, so changing one part spreads to others.

People can harm a pond (disturbance, pollution) or help it: at the Model Boating Pond, removing the causeway and adding reeds made space for birds and changed water, pollution and carbon flows.

Mission 5: Forests, reefs and rivers feed, shelter and protect us for free. So why do we keep cutting them down?

Mission 5

Goods and services

  • The biosphere is the living layer of the Earth: plants, animals and the soils and waters they live in.
  • Ecosystem services are "the benefits people obtain from ecosystems".
  • Goods: things we take — food, water, timber, fuel, medicine.
  • Services: things ecosystems do for us — protect coasts, regulate climate, clean water, attract tourists.
Mission 5

The goods

  • Food: crops, fish, wild fruit and game.
  • Medicine: "Many drugs are now being developed from compounds discovered in coral reef animals and plants".
  • Building materials: timber, bamboo, thatch.
  • Fuel: wood and charcoal. In 2024 only 22% of people in sub-Saharan Africa had clean cooking, against 75% worldwide; most of the rest cook with fuels such as wood.
Quick check
?Which of these is a good, not a service, from an ecosystem?
Mission 5

The services

  • Natural protection: mangroves "stabilize the coastline, reducing erosion from storm surges, currents, waves, and tides".
  • Climate regulation: about half the CO₂ people emit each year stays in the air; "the rest is absorbed by Earth's land ecosystems and oceans".
  • Tourism: Australia's Great Barrier Reef is reported to contribute $9 billion a year to the economy (a study quoted by the park authority).
  • Health: medicines from reefs and forests, clean water and clean air.
Mission 5

From local use to commercial exploitation

  • Local people have always used ecosystems for subsistence: food, fuel and shelter for their own families.
  • Now ecosystems are exploited commercially: cleared or mined on a large scale to sell goods in global markets.
  • Example (minerals): Indonesia's share of world nickel mine production rose from about 30% in 2019 to about 67% in 2025 (calculated from USGS figures). Nickel is used in electric-vehicle batteries.
Mission 5

Food: mechanised farming

  • Mechanised farming: machines and large fields replace hand labour, so huge areas can be farmed by few people.
  • In South America the area of soybeans "more than doubled from 26.4 Mha to 55.1 Mha" between 2000 and 2019.
  • "Across the continent, 9% of forest loss was converted to soybean by 2016", nearly half of it in Brazil's Cerrado savanna.
  • Modification: natural forest and grassland replaced by one crop (a monoculture).
Mission 5

Food: commercial fishing

  • Large boats with big nets catch fish to sell worldwide.
  • In FAO's latest count, wild catch was 92 million tonnes; fish farming (aquaculture) produced 103 million tonnes.
  • In 2023, 62.4% of assessed fish stocks were "biologically sustainable".
  • Modification: fewer large fish, changed food webs, damage to the seabed from some gear.
Quick check
?In 2023, 62.4% of assessed fish stocks were biologically sustainable. What share were not?
Mission 5

Energy: deforestation and mining

  • Deforestation: forests are cleared for fuelwood, charcoal, farms and mines.
  • World forest area fell from about 4.34 billion hectares in 1990 to 4.14 billion in 2025: a net loss of about 203 million hectares, or 4.7% (calculated from FAO data).
  • Mining for energy: coal and uranium are dug from open pits or deep mines. Open pits remove the whole ecosystem above the rock.
Mission 5

Water: reservoirs and transfer schemes

  • A reservoir is an artificial lake made by building a dam across a valley. The valley behind it is flooded.
  • A water transfer scheme moves water from a wet area to where it is needed, through rivers, pipes or tunnels.
  • Kielder Water, Northumberland (1982), holds about 200 billion litres.
  • Its water can be released into the Tyne, Derwent and Tees to supply homes and industry in north-east England: a reservoir and a transfer scheme.
Quick check
?Which is an example of a water transfer scheme?
Mission 5

Using goods can cost services

  • Between 2000 and 2020, the biggest single cause of mangrove loss worldwide was aquaculture (fish and shrimp ponds): 27% of losses.
  • The pond owner gains a good (shrimp to sell). The coast loses a service (protection from waves).
  • The people who gain are often not the people who lose.
Mission check

?A government plans to clear a mangrove forest on a low coast to build shrimp ponds for export. Which outcome is most likely?

Ecosystems give goods that can be sold (shrimp, timber) and services that are usually free (protection, climate regulation). Clearing for goods is why ecosystems keep being cut down: the losses fall on people who are not paid for them.

Mission 6

The Welsh coast: an energy environment

  • Wind: strong, steady winds over the Irish Sea off North Wales.
  • Tides: the Severn estuary has an "enormous tidal range".
  • Offshore wind farms already line the North Wales coast: North Hoyle, Rhyl Flats and Gwynt y Môr.
  • The same coast holds protected mudflats, saltmarsh, birds and fish.
Mission 6

Offshore wind: Gwynt y Môr

  • 576 MW wind farm in the Irish Sea, 13 km from the North Wales coast at its nearest point.
  • 160 turbines, each 150 m tall to the blade tip; working since June 2015.
  • A new farm, Awel y Môr, plans up to 50 turbines about 10 km offshore, west of Gwynt y Môr.
Mission 6

Tidal power: barrages and lagoons

  • A tidal barrage is a dam across an estuary; water flows through turbines as the tide rises and falls.
  • A tidal lagoon is a sea wall enclosing part of a bay, with turbines in the wall.
  • Swansea Bay lagoon: a six-mile horseshoe sea wall, about 500 GWh of electricity a year; given planning consent in 2015.
  • In 2018 the government refused to fund it: £1.3 billion to build for about 0.15% of UK electricity.
Quick check
?What is the main difference between a tidal barrage and a tidal lagoon?
Mission 6

The Severn barrage plan

  • A 2010 government study looked at five possible Severn schemes.
  • The biggest, a barrage from Cardiff to Weston, could install 8,640 MW and make 15.6 TWh a year.
  • Estimated cost: £34.3 billion.
  • The study judged it "high cost and high risk" compared with other low-carbon power.
Mission 6

Impact on the estuary's ecosystem

  • The estuary is internationally protected for its mudflats, saltmarsh, migratory fish and over-wintering birds.
  • A barrage would hold back the tide, so the shore would no longer be exposed as widely at low tide.
  • Cardiff–Weston would lose 118–163 km² of intertidal habitat: 40–50% of it.
  • Birds: significant declines predicted in 30 species.
  • Fish: likely local extinction of Atlantic salmon and twaite shad; 3 of the 4 UK rivers where twaite shad breed flow into the Severn.
Quick check
?Why would losing intertidal mudflat cut wading-bird numbers?
Mission 6

Impacts of offshore wind on wildlife

  • Birds: the most-reported harms are collisions with blades and displacement from feeding areas.
  • Marine mammals: piling noise during construction disturbs harbour porpoises. At one North Sea wind farm, porpoises had a 50% chance of responding within 7.4 km at first, falling to 1.3 km by the end of piling.
  • Positive effects: turbine bases act as artificial reefs, attracting fish; trawling bans near turbines protect the seabed.
Mission 6

Reducing the harm

  • Any Severn scheme would first need new habitats created to replace those lost.
  • Offshore wind projects that harm protected marine sites must provide compensation.
  • A lagoon has a smaller footprint: the study found a Bridgwater Bay lagoon had "lower environmental impacts than barrage options".
Quick check
?Which offshore wind impact on wildlife can be positive?
Mission 6

Weighing it up

  • Offshore wind: proven and cheaper; wildlife harm is real but local and partly offset by reef effects.
  • Tidal barrage: predictable power over a 120-year design life, but huge cost and major loss of estuary habitat.
  • Tidal lagoon: smaller impact, but costly for its output.
  • Exam move: name the environment (Welsh coast), the use (wind, tidal), and one impact on ecosystems and one on biodiversity.
Mission check

?A country plans a barrage across a large estuary with wide mudflats, wintering birds and salmon rivers. Using the Severn study, what is the most serious risk to the estuary ecosystem?

The Severn barrage has never been built because it was judged high cost and high risk: about £34 billion, and the loss of up to half the estuary's intertidal habitat, with declines in 30 bird species and possible loss of salmon and twaite shad.

Mission 7

Mission 7: The world's biggest rainforest is still being cleared. Who is doing it, and why does it matter beyond Brazil?

Make a guess: The world's biggest rainforest is still being cleared. Who is doing it, and why does it matter beyond Brazil?

Specification: what this mission covers

Eduqas GCSE Geography A (C111)

  • 5.4.2 impacts of human activity incl. food production at a range of scales on the TRF
  • 5.4.2 local scale: habitat loss changes nutrient cycles, soil structure, soil erosion
  • 5.4.2 regional scale: monoculture and intensive farming affect water cycles and regional climate
  • 5.4.2 biodiversity: reduced range, isolated populations, extinctions
Mission 7

The Amazon rainforest

  • The largest tropical rainforest on Earth. Most of it is in Brazil; it also covers parts of eight other South American countries and territories.
  • About 16,000 tree species.
  • Amazon trees store more carbon than any other ecosystem on Earth.
  • This mission uses the Brazilian Amazon as the case study.
Mission 7

Changing rates of deforestation

  • From 2004 to 2012 the rate of clearing in the Brazilian Amazon fell by 84%.
  • 2012: 4,571 km² cleared, the lowest on record until 2026.
  • The rate then rose again: 5,731 km² in August 2024–July 2025.
  • August 2025–July 2026: an estimated 3,335 km², the lowest since records began in 1988.
Quick check
?Using the figures just taught: By what percentage did clearing fall from 2024–25 (5,731 km²) to 2025–26 (3,335 km²)?
Mission 7

Cause: farming

  • Across the tropics, 90–99% of deforestation in 2011–15 was driven by agriculture.
  • In the Amazon, most cleared land first becomes cattle pasture (commercial farming).
  • Soy then spreads, mostly onto pastures first cleared for cattle.
  • Much soy is exported: in 2025 China imported 82.3 million tonnes of soybeans from Brazil, 74% of its soybean imports.
Mission 7

Cause: logging

  • Selective logging takes valuable trees and leaves the rest standing.
  • 1999–2002, in Brazil's top five timber states, 12,075–19,823 km² were logged each year: 60–123% of the area deforested.
  • Logging roads open up the forest to farmers and settlers.
Mission 7

Cause: roads, mining and energy

  • Roads: nearly 95% of deforestation happened within 5.5 km of a road or 1 km of a river. Over 190,000 km of the roads are unofficial.
  • Mining: small-scale gold mining (garimpo) in Brazil grew from about 218 km² (1985) to about 2,627 km² (2022). More than 91% is in the Amazon, and at least 77% of 2022 sites showed signs of illegality.
  • Energy (HEP): the Belo Monte dam on the Xingu River flooded land and forced people in Altamira to move.
Quick check
?Which cause most often opens the forest up for the others?
Mission 7

Impact: soils and nutrients

  • Most nutrients in a rainforest are stored in plants, not the soil. Rainforest soils are thin and poor.
  • Clearing breaks the nutrient cycle: no more leaf litter feeds the soil.
  • Heavy rain hits bare ground: soil erosion washes topsoil away and leaching removes nutrients.
  • Pasture and crops can fail within a few years, so more forest is cleared.
Mission 7

Impact: rainfall and regional climate

  • Trees pump water vapour into the air (transpiration), which falls again as rain downwind.
  • Over more than 60% of tropical land, air that has passed over forest gives at least twice as much rain as air that has passed over little vegetation.
  • Replacing forest with monoculture crops or pasture means less moisture is recycled.
  • If clearing trends continued, Amazon rainfall could fall by 12% (wet season) and 21% (dry season) by 2050.
Mission 7

Impact: climate change

  • Trees store carbon. Clearing and burning release it as carbon dioxide.
  • Aircraft measurements in 2010–2018 found south-eastern Amazonia had become a net carbon source.
  • The causes: more deforestation and longer, drier dry seasons, bringing more fires.
Mission 7

Impact: biodiversity

  • Habitat loss shrinks each species' range.
  • Fragmentation: in 1999–2002, deforestation and logging created about 32,000 km and 38,000 km of new forest edge each year.
  • Small, separate fragments leave isolated populations that cannot mix or move.
  • Species found only in cleared areas can die out locally, or globally: extinction.
Quick check
?Which impact of clearing works at a regional scale, across much of the Amazon basin?
Mission check

?In September–October 2015, forest and peatland fires across maritime south-east Asia, mostly in Indonesia, released more CO₂ per day than all the fossil fuels burned in the EU. Which conclusion best fits?

In the Amazon, farming (cattle, then soy) is the main cause, helped by roads, logging, mining and HEP. The impacts reach far beyond Brazil: rainfall, carbon and biodiversity.

Mission 8

Mission 8: How can a farm far inland, or a fishing boat, push coral species towards extinction?

Make a guess: How can a farm far inland, or a fishing boat, push coral species towards extinction?

Specification: what this mission covers

Eduqas GCSE Geography A (C111)

  • 5.4.2 impacts of human activity, including food production, at a range of scales on the TRF and the same other biome (here: tropical coral reefs)
  • 5.4.2 biodiversity impacts, reduced range and habitat, isolated populations, local and global extinctions
Mission 8

The coral reef ecosystem

  • Reef-building corals are small animals that build skeletons of calcium carbonate. Together they build reefs.
  • The reef's structure is habitat: shelter and food for fish and many other species.
  • The Great Barrier Reef covers 344,400 km² and supports 77,000 full-time jobs.
Mission 8

Food production 1: fishing

  • Overfishing: taking fish faster than they can breed shrinks the populations that live on the reef. Worldwide, in 2023, 62.4% of marine fishery stocks assessed by the FAO were at biologically sustainable levels, so about 37.6% were not.
  • Blast fishing: explosives "detonated to startle fish out of hiding places". More than 40 countries are affected.
  • Cyanide fishing: poison sprayed onto reefs to stun live fish; more than 15 countries have reported it.
  • Anchors "dropped from fishing vessels onto reefs can break and destroy coral colonies".
Mission 8

Food production 2: farm runoff

  • Rain washes soil, fertiliser and pesticides off farmland into rivers, and rivers carry them to the sea.
  • Runoff "may contain sediments, nutrients, chemicals, insecticides, oil, and debris".
  • Extra nutrients promote "the rapid growth of algae and other organisms that can smother corals".
  • On the Great Barrier Reef, UNESCO names "continued declining water quality from land-based sources" as a major threat.
Quick check
?How can fertiliser spread on a farm 50 km inland harm a coral reef?
Mission 8

Impacts at a range of scales

  • Local: blast fishing, anchors, and divers who "trample fragile corals".
  • Regional: farm runoff from whole river basins. GBRMPA names "crown-of-thorns starfish outbreaks, poor water quality and some fishing activities" as threats, but expects decline "due largely to climate change".
  • Global: warming oceans. From early 2023 to mid-2025, bleaching-level heat stress hit 84% of the world's reef area.
  • Global CO₂ also makes seawater more acidic, leaving fewer carbonate ions for coral skeletons.
Quick check
?Which human impact on coral reefs works at the global scale?
Mission 8

Biodiversity: less habitat, smaller range

  • When corals die, the reef's structure breaks down: habitat is lost for the species that live there.
  • Northern Great Barrier Reef: hard coral cover fell from 39.8% (2024) to 30.0% (2025), the largest annual fall recorded there since monitoring began.
  • AIMS: "the mass coral bleaching event was the primary source of coral mortality".
  • Reduced range: a species survives over a smaller area than before.
Mission 8

Isolated populations and extinction

  • Isolated populations: healthy patches of reef become separated by dead reef. Fewer young can reach each patch, so each population is smaller and more at risk.
  • Local extinction: a species disappears from one place but survives elsewhere. Global extinction: it disappears everywhere.
  • The IUCN reassessed 892 reef-building coral species: 44% are at risk of extinction.
  • "Climate change is the main threat"; others include "pollution, agricultural runoff, disease and unsustainable fishing".
Quick check
?A coral species has vanished from one reef but still lives on reefs elsewhere. What has happened?
Mission 8

Exam move: build a chain

  • Link each step: activity → process → effect on the reef → biodiversity impact.
  • Example: fertiliser → nutrient runoff → algae smother corals → habitat lost → smaller, isolated populations → local extinction.
  • Name the scale (local, regional, global) and use a figure, such as 84% of reef area under heat stress in 2023–25.
Mission check

?On a tropical island, new farms upstream spread fertiliser, and some fishers use explosives on the reef. Which chain best explains the threat to coral species there?

Food production reaches reefs by two routes: runoff from farmland and fishing on the reef. Both cut habitat; on top of global warming, this leaves smaller, isolated populations. The IUCN finds 44% of reef-building coral species at risk of extinction.

Mission 9

Mission 9: Some villagers in Guatemala make a living by cutting down rainforest trees. Can logging help save a forest?

Make a guess: Some villagers in Guatemala make a living by cutting down rainforest trees. Can logging help save a forest?

Specification: what this mission covers

Eduqas GCSE Geography A (C111)

  • 5.4.3 sustainable strategies to manage habitat and biodiversity in tropical rainforests
  • 5.4.3 zoning within National Parks and creation of wildlife corridors
Mission 9

What makes management sustainable?

  • Sustainable management meets people's needs today without destroying the forest for the future.
  • Test any plan with three questions:
  • Environment: does forest cover and wildlife stay?
  • People: do local people earn a living from it?
  • Long term: will it still work in 20 years, with rules that are enforced?
Mission 9

The toolkit: using the timber

  • Selective logging: cut only some mature trees, leave the rest standing.
  • Replanting: replace trees that are felled; native species keep habitat for wildlife.
  • Certification: the Forest Stewardship Council (FSC) sets standards for responsible forest management; independent bodies check forests against them.
  • International hardwood agreements: the International Tropical Timber Agreement has governed trade in tropical timber since the 1980s; today's version, of 2006, came into force in 2011.
Mission 9

The toolkit: protecting and paying

  • Conservation: protected areas such as national parks ban or limit clearing.
  • Education: teaching local people and buyers why the forest matters, and how to use it without clearing it.
  • Ecotourism: small-scale tourism that pays local people to keep the forest standing.
  • Debt reduction (debt-for-nature swaps): part of a country's debt is cancelled in return for spending on conservation. The first was signed by Bolivia and Conservation International in 1987.
Quick check
?A forest company wants to keep earning from timber but do the least damage to the forest. Which strategy fits best?
Mission 9

The case: the Maya Biosphere Reserve, Guatemala

  • Petén, northern Guatemala: the largest expanse of continuous rainforest in Central America.
  • Guatemala's protected areas cover 32% of its land, yet only Nicaragua has lost forest faster in Latin America and the Caribbean since 2000.
  • The reserve is shared out in zones, each with different rules.
Mission 9

Zoning

  • Core zone: strictly protected; no logging. Its national parks include Tikal and Mirador–Río Azul.
  • Multiple-use zone: forest used under rules. Rights to timber and other forest products were given to 12 community groups and 2 timber companies.
  • Buffer zone: along the southern edge; farming and settlement, with some clearing allowed "under certain circumstances".
  • UNESCO's model: core = strict protection; buffer = activities compatible with conservation; transition = where communities live and work.
Mission 9

Local scale: Uaxactún's community forest

  • A village of about 800 people that manages 835 km² of forest in the multiple-use zone.
  • Selective logging since 2000; certified by the FSC since 2001.
  • Residents agreed hunting rules: no hunting at waterholes, no selling meat to outsiders.
  • Result: camera-trap surveys estimated 25 species of large ground animals in Uaxactún's forest and 24 in the strictly protected Mirador–Río Azul area next door.
  • But heavily hunted species, such as the great curassow, were scarcer near the village.
Mission 9

Wildlife corridors

  • A wildlife corridor is a strip of habitat that links protected areas, so animals can move, feed and breed between them.
  • The Mesoamerican Biological Corridor aims to link the ecosystems of South, Central and North America.
  • The reserve's strictly protected north joins Mexico's Calakmul Biosphere Reserve across the border.
  • Why it matters: jaguars have been lost from over 77% of their historic range in Mesoamerica; keeping populations connected is now a conservation priority.
Mission 9

Does it work?

  • Success: Tikal and Mirador–Río Azul national parks stayed over 99% stable forest, 2000–2017.
  • Mixed: some concessions manage forests well; others "have had limited success or failed completely".
  • Failure: the buffer zone lost 1.4% of its forest a year; by 2017 only 17% was undisturbed forest.
  • Pressure on the core: Laguna del Tigre National Park, beside the buffer zone, was heavily cleared despite its protection.
Quick check
?Laguna del Tigre National Park, beside the buffer zone, was heavily cleared, while Mirador–Río Azul National Park, in the far north, stayed almost untouched. Both are in the same strictly protected core zone. What does this suggest?
Mission check

?A government wants to cut deforestation in a rainforest region where many villagers depend on the forest for income. Which plan is most likely to be sustainable?

In the Maya Biosphere Reserve, a certified community forest at Uaxactún kept as many large animal species as the strictly protected forest next door. Where rules were not enforced, as in the buffer zone, the forest kept shrinking.

Mission 10

Mission 10: A community on Kenya's coast earns money by not cutting down its mangroves. Who pays, and why?

Make a guess: A community on Kenya's coast earns money by not cutting down its mangroves. Who pays, and why?

Specification: what this mission covers

Eduqas GCSE Geography A (C111)

  • 5.4.3 sustainable strategies to manage habitat and biodiversity in the chosen contrasting ecosystem (coral reef)
  • 5.4.3 zoning within National Parks and creation of wildlife corridors
Mission 10

Where coral reefs grow

  • Reef-building corals live in tropical and semi-tropical seas.
  • They cannot survive water below 18 °C.
  • The water must be clear, so sunlight reaches the algae living in the coral, and salty (32–42 parts per thousand).
  • Reefs cover only about 1% of the ocean, yet give habitat to at least 25% of marine life.
Mission 10

Where mangroves grow

  • Mangroves: salt-tolerant trees on muddy tropical and subtropical coasts, between low and high tide.
  • The world had 14.8 million hectares of mangroves in 2020.
  • Nearly 44% is in South and South-east Asia; Indonesia alone has 21%.
Mission 10

Why they matter

  • Coral reefs protect shorelines "by reducing the impact from waves, storms, and floods". They support fishing and tourism, and many medicines are being developed from reef animals and plants.
  • Mangroves store large amounts of carbon in their soils, protect coasts from storms and erosion, and support rich wildlife.
  • They also shelter young fish that later live on reefs.
Quick check
?Which is a benefit of mangroves to people on the coast?
Mission 10

Threats to reefs: heat

  • Coral bleaching: under stress, corals expel the algae (zooxanthellae) living in their tissues and turn white.
  • The leading cause is warmer water from climate change; runoff and pollution, strong sunlight and very low tides add stress.
  • From early 2023 to mid-2025, bleaching-level heat stress hit 84% of the world's coral reef area.
  • Bleached coral is not dead, but many corals die if the stress lasts.
Mission 10

Threats from people

  • Farming: fertiliser and soil wash into the sea, clouding the water and feeding algae. Poor water quality is a named threat to the Great Barrier Reef.
  • Deforestation: mangroves are cleared for fish and shrimp ponds, rice and oil palm. Forest cleared inland sends mud down rivers onto reefs.
  • Industrialisation: ports, dredging, factories and coastal building pollute water and bury habitat.
  • Tourism: hotels on the shore, sewage, boat anchors, and divers touching coral.
Quick check
?A new resort dredges a channel for boats, and its sewage is piped into the lagoon. Which threat is this?
Mission 10

Gazi Bay, Kenya: Mikoko Pamoja

  • "The world's first community-led blue carbon project": local people protect and replant mangroves.
  • The carbon the trees store is sold as carbon credits to buyers who want to balance out their own emissions.
  • 1,081 households take part.
  • The money "has enabled community development such as provision of schoolbooks, construction of school buildings and the provision of clean drinking water".
Mission 10

Zoning: the Great Barrier Reef

  • The Great Barrier Reef Marine Park, Australia, covers 344,400 km², with about 3,000 reefs.
  • Zoning divides it into areas where each activity is allowed, banned or needs a permit.
  • About 115,000 km² are "no-take" or "no-entry" zones: about a third of the park.
  • Fishing is banned in no-take zones, so fish can breed and spread to nearby areas.
Mission 10

Wildlife corridors

  • A wildlife corridor links protected habitats so animals can move between them.
  • On tropical coasts, many reef fish grow up in mangroves, then move to the reef.
  • In the Caribbean, where reefs are linked to mangroves, the biomass of several commercially important reef fish is "more than doubled".
  • So protecting mangroves, seagrass and reefs together, as one connected system, works like a corridor.
Mission 10

Is the management sustainable?

  • Zoning: fishers lose some areas, but fish stocks and tourism can gain.
  • Mikoko Pamoja: local people gain, so they protect the forest. Limits: one small project, and income depends on buyers of credits.
  • Water quality must be managed on farms on land, outside the marine park.
  • Scale: no local plan can stop global ocean warming, the main threat to reefs.
Quick check
?Which threat to the Great Barrier Reef can its managers not control by zoning?
Mission check

?A fishing village on another tropical coast wants to protect its mangroves but needs income. Which scheme follows the Gazi Bay model?

In Gazi Bay, buyers of carbon credits pay for the carbon that protected mangroves store. The village gains income for schools and clean water, so it has a reason to keep the trees.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Biomes are large-scale ecosystems; their distribution follows climate: rainforest at the equator, deserts at about 30°, temperate and boreal forest further out, tundra and ice near the poles.

Mission 2

  • Characteristics: between the Tropics; hot (27–28 °C) and wet (over 2,000 mm) all year from overhead sun, low pressure and convectional rain; layers from emergents to forest floor; deep, red, leached latosols.
  • How it works: nutrients stored mainly in biomass and recycled fast; forests make their own rain and store carbon; everything is interdependent.
  • Biodiversity is the highest on land.

Mission 3

  • Coral reefs: warm (above 18 °C), salty, clear, shallow tropical seas, mostly 35°N–35°S.
  • Corals and zooxanthellae share food and nutrients (mutualism), so reefs are productive in nutrient-poor water.
  • The food web runs from algae and plankton through corals and fish to sharks; removing one link changes the rest.
  • Water and carbon cycles link reefs to the land (flood plumes) and to the global atmosphere (CO₂ and acidity).

Mission 4

  • Pond ecosystem: producers (algae, pondweed, reeds) → consumers (water fleas, snails, dragonfly nymphs, fish, grebes); decomposers recycle nutrients from dead matter back to the water.
  • Human activity at Hampstead Heath: disturbance and heavy use; management (2026 causeway removal, new reedbed) boosts biodiversity and changes flows and cycles.

Mission 5

  • Goods (food, medicine, building materials, fuel, water) are taken; services (protection, climate regulation, tourism, health) are provided.
  • Commercial exploitation modifies ecosystems: mechanised farming and fishing (food), deforestation and mining (energy, minerals), reservoirs and transfers (water).

Mission 6

  • The Welsh coast is used for offshore wind (Gwynt y Môr: 576 MW, 160 turbines) and has been proposed for tidal power (Swansea Bay lagoon; the Severn barrage).
  • Impacts: a barrage would destroy 40–50% of the Severn's intertidal habitat, harming birds and migratory fish; offshore wind causes bird collisions and porpoise disturbance, but turbine bases can act as reefs.

Mission 7

  • Amazon clearing fell sharply after 2004, rose again after 2012, and in 2025–26 reached its lowest estimate since 1988.
  • Causes: farming (cattle, soy), logging, roads, mining, HEP.
  • Impacts: soil erosion, less rainfall, carbon release and biodiversity loss.

Mission 8

  • Food production harms reefs through overfishing, destructive fishing and farm runoff; warming and acidification act at the global scale.
  • Biodiversity impacts: lost habitat and range, isolated populations, then local and possibly global extinction.

Mission 9

  • Zoning (strict core, managed multiple-use, buffer) works where people benefit and rules are enforced: Uaxactún kept its wildlife; the buffer zone lost most of its forest.
  • Corridors such as the Mesoamerican Biological Corridor link protected areas so wide-ranging animals like the jaguar can move between them.

Mission 10

  • Coral reefs (clear, warm, shallow tropical seas) and mangroves (tropical muddy coasts) protect coasts and support fish, but face warming, farming, deforestation, industry and tourism.
  • Management works best when local people gain (Mikoko Pamoja), but no local plan can stop global warming. Zoning controls where each activity is allowed. Wildlife corridors protect linked habitats together.
