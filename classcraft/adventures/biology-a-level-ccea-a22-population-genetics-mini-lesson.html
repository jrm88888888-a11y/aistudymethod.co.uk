Mission 1

Mission 1: A harmful allele can hide in healthy carriers. How can you work out how many people carry it?

Make a guess: A harmful allele can hide in healthy carriers. How can you work out how many people carry it?

Mission 1

Populations and gene pools

  • Population: organisms of one species, in the same place at the same time, that can interbreed.
  • Gene pool: all the genes, and all their alleles, in a population.
  • Allele frequency: the fraction of all copies of a gene that are one allele.
  • Two alleles: frequency of A = p, frequency of a = q.
  • Each diploid has 2 copies of each gene. 500 individuals → 1000 copies.
  • Say: "allele frequency counts copies" — not: "the fraction of individuals that carry it".
Mission 1Worked example

Count the copies

A population of 400 snails: 196 AA, 168 Aa, 36 aa.

  • Step 1: total copies = 2 × 400 = 800.
  • Step 2: copies of A = (2 × 196) + 168 = 392 + 168 = 560.
  • Step 3: p = 560 ÷ 800 = 0.70.
  • Step 4: q = 1 − 0.70 = 0.30. Check: (2 × 36 + 168) ÷ 800 = 240 ÷ 800 = 0.30.
  • AA individuals give 2 copies; Aa give 1. Never count individuals.

Mission 1

The Hardy–Weinberg equations

  • p + q = 1 (all copies are A or a).
  • p² + 2pq + q² = 1 (all individuals are AA, Aa or aa).
  • p² = AA. 2pq = Aa. q² = aa.
  • Why 2pq? Aa forms two ways: A egg + a sperm, or a egg + A sperm.
  • Dominant phenotype = p² + 2pq = 1 − q². Recessive phenotype = q².
  • Prediction: allele frequencies stay the same from generation to generation.
  • The principle: genetic equilibrium is kept, and at fertilisation alleles combine randomly.
Mission 1Worked example

Find the hidden carriers

Real figure: in the UK, about 1 in 2,500 babies has cystic fibrosis, a recessive condition.

  • Step 1: affected = aa = q². So q² = 1 ÷ 2,500 = 0.0004.
  • Step 2: q = √0.0004 = 0.02.
  • Step 3: p = 1 − 0.02 = 0.98.
  • Step 4: carriers = Aa = 2pq = 2 × 0.98 × 0.02 = 0.039.
  • So about 1 in 25 people is a carrier: about 98 carriers for every affected person.
  • Start from q², the only phenotype that shows one genotype.
Quick check
?In a large population, 16% of individuals show the phenotype of the homozygous recessive genotype for a gene with two alleles. Assuming Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium, what is the frequency of the homozygous dominant genotype?
Mission 1

When the prediction holds

The allele frequencies stay constant only if:

  • the population is large (no chance changes: genetic drift),
  • mating is random,
  • there are no new mutations,
  • there is no migration in or out,
  • there is no natural selection: every genotype survives and reproduces equally.
  • The population is outbreeding. Disturbing factors: mutation, non-random fertilisation, migration, selection.
  • Say: "equilibrium: frequencies stay the same" — not: "equilibrium: p = q = 0.5".
Mission 1

Read the data: MN blood groups

Real data: MN blood group genotypes in 6,129 people in the USA (a classic dataset). M and N are codominant, so each blood type shows its genotype.

MMMNNN
Observed178730391303
Expected (p², 2pq, q² × 6129)178430451300
  • p(M) = (2 × 1787 + 3039) ÷ (2 × 6129) = 6613 ÷ 12 258 = 0.5395. q(N) = 0.4605.
  • Expected MM = 0.5395² × 6129 ≈ 1784. Expected MN = 2 × 0.5395 × 0.4605 × 6129 ≈ 3045.
  • Keep four decimal places in p and q, or expected counts drift.
  • Observed and expected are very close.
  • A χ² test on these numbers gives a value far below any critical value.

Mission 1

When frequencies change

  • Evolution: a change in allele frequencies in a population over generations.
  • Selection, drift, migration and mutation change allele frequencies.
  • Say: "populations evolve" — not: "individuals evolve".
Mission 1

Recessive alleles hide

  • A harmful recessive allele is selected against only in aa individuals.
  • Most copies sit in healthy Aa carriers (cystic fibrosis: about 98 carriers per affected person).
  • So selection lowers q slowly, and ever more slowly as q falls.
  • Being dominant does not make an allele spread.
Quick check

A new disease then appears, and all rr individuals die before reproducing, while RR and Rr individuals survive and reproduce equally well. Which prediction about the frequency of r over the next several generations is best supported?
Mission check

?In a large, randomly mating population of a hypothetical moth species, pale wings are caused by a recessive allele. 9% of the moths have pale wings. Assuming Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium, what proportion of the moths are heterozygous?

Count the ones you can see (q²), take the square root, and the hidden carriers appear: 2pq.

Mission 2

What is a gene mutation?

  • A gene mutation is a change in the base sequence of DNA.
  • Many arise as errors during DNA replication that are not corrected.
  • The changed DNA is transcribed into changed mRNA. That may change the polypeptide.
  • Larger changes, to whole chromosomes, are chromosome mutations (see Mission 4).
Mission 2

Three ways to change a sequence

  • (1) Substitution: one base replaced by another. Same length.
  • (2) Deletion: a base removed. One shorter.
  • Compare the sequences base by base, and count their lengths.
Quick check
?Part of the base sequence of a gene is GAT TCA GGC ATT before a mutation and GAT TCG GCA TT after it. Which type of gene mutation has occurred?
Mission 2

A substitution: three possible outcomes

  • A substitution changes one codon only. The reading frame is untouched.
  • Outcome 1: the new codon codes for the same amino acid. No change. The code is degenerate.
  • Outcome 2: the new codon codes for a different amino acid. One amino acid changes.
  • Outcome 3: the new codon is a stop codon. The polypeptide is cut short.
  • You may also see: silent (1), missense (2), nonsense (3).
Mission 2

Worked example: four mutations, one gene

  • Code table: AUG Met · GCA, GCU Ala · UGG Trp · AAG Lys · GAG Glu · CUU Leu · CAU His · GGA Gly · AGC Ser · UGA stop.
  • Normal mRNA: AUG GCA UGG AAG CUU → Met–Ala–Trp–Lys–Leu.
  • (a) GCA → GCU: Met–Ala–Trp–Lys–Leu. No change.
  • (b) AAG → GAG: Met–Ala–Trp–Glu–Leu. One amino acid changed.
  • (c) UGG → UGA: Met–Ala–stop. Two amino acids only.
  • (d) Delete the G of codon 2: AUG CAU GGA AGC UU… → Met–His–Gly–Ser… Every codon after the deletion changes.
Quick check
?In mRNA the codons GCU, GCC, GCA and GCG all code for alanine. A base substitution in a gene changes the codon GCU in its mRNA to GCC. What is the effect on the polypeptide?
Mission 2

Deletions and insertions: the frame shifts

  • The ribosome reads in threes from the start codon. There are no gaps or commas.
  • Delete one base and every later base moves up one place: a frameshift.
  • Every downstream codon is regrouped. Codons before the mutation are unchanged.
  • A new stop codon usually appears early. The polypeptide almost always stops working.
  • Say: "frameshift: all downstream triplets change" — not: "one base, one amino acid".
Quick check
?In a hypothetical bacterium, strain A has a deletion of one nucleotide in codon 10 of a gene that has 300 codons. Strain B has a deletion of the three adjacent nucleotides that make up codon 10 of the same gene. Which prediction about the polypeptides translated from the two genes is best supported?
Mission 2

A real point mutation: sickle cell

  • Gene: β-globin, one of the polypeptides of haemoglobin.
  • DNA coding strand: GAG → GTG. mRNA: GAG → GUG.
  • Amino acid 6: glutamic acid (charged) → valine (non-polar).
  • At low oxygen, these haemoglobin molecules stick together into long fibres.
  • Red blood cells become sickle-shaped, rigid, and block capillaries.
  • One base out of hundreds. Two copies of this allele: sickle cell anaemia.
Quick check
?In the allele that causes sickle cell anaemia, the mRNA codon for amino acid 6 of the beta chain of haemoglobin is GUG instead of GAG. GAG codes for glutamic acid and GUG codes for valine. What is the effect of this mutation on the protein?
Mission 2

Harmful, neutral or beneficial?

  • Harmful: the protein no longer works, or works worse.
  • Neutral: no detectable effect on the organism. Silent mutations are usually neutral.
  • Beneficial: the new protein gives an advantage.
  • The environment decides: a mutation that lets a bacterium survive an antibiotic helps only where that antibiotic is present.
  • Say: "silent: same amino acid sequence" — not: "silent: no effect on the phenotype". That is neutral.
Mission check

?A gene codes for a polypeptide of 400 amino acids. A single base substitution changes the fifth codon of its mRNA from UAC to UAA. In the code table, UAC codes for tyrosine and UAA is a stop codon. What is the most likely effect?

One letter can do nothing, change one amino acid, or stop the whole chain. It depends on which codon it creates.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Can a living thing ever mutate because it needs to?

Make a guess: Can a living thing ever mutate because it needs to?

Mission 3

Copying is almost perfect

  • Every cell division copies billions of bases.
  • DNA polymerase sometimes pairs the wrong base. Proofreading and repair fix almost all errors.
  • The few that escape become permanent: spontaneous mutations.
  • Errors in DNA repair add more.
  • So mutations happen even with no harmful agent present. Each person carries dozens of new mutations neither parent had.
Mission 3

Random, not directed

  • A mutation can happen anywhere in the genome.
  • No natural mechanism makes a chosen change to a chosen base to produce a useful trait.
  • A mutagen raises the rate. It does not pick the gene.
  • The environment selects among variants that already exist. It does not create them.
  • Say: "a random mutation that happened to give resistance" — not: "the bacteria mutated to become resistant".
Mission 3

Read the data: chance or need?

  • 1943: Luria and Delbrück asked whether bacteria become resistant to a virus because they meet it.
  • Set-up: grow 20 separate small cultures of E. coli from a few cells each; also take 10 samples from one big culture. Spread each on plates with virus. Count resistant colonies.
  • Real data (Luria and Delbrück, 1943), resistant colonies per plate:

  • Separate cultures vary wildly. A mutation early in growth leaves many resistant descendants; a late one, few; often none.

Mission 3

The only source of new alleles

  • A new allele can arise only by mutation.
  • Meiosis and fertilisation reshuffle existing alleles into new combinations. They make no new alleles.
  • Most mutations are harmful or neutral to the individual. A few are beneficial.
  • Gene and chromosome mutations are both sources of genetic variation.
Quick check
?Why is gene mutation described as the original source of all genetic variation?
Mission check

?When a new insecticide was first used on a crop, it killed nearly all individuals of a hypothetical pest insect. After several years of use, most of the pest population was resistant. Which statement best explains how resistance became common?

Mutation supplies variation by chance; the environment only chooses among what is already there.

Mission 4

Mission 4: One extra chromosome can change a whole body. So how do some plants thrive with doubled sets?

Make a guess: One extra chromosome can change a whole body. So how do some plants thrive with doubled sets?

Mission 4

Gene or chromosome mutation?

  • Gene mutation: a change in the bases of one gene (see Mission 2).
  • Chromosome mutation: a change in the number or structure of whole chromosomes.
  • One chromosome carries hundreds of genes. Gain or lose one, and hundreds of genes change dose at once.
  • Development depends on genes being in the right balance.
  • A whole extra set keeps every gene in proportion. Many plants live well with one.
Mission 4

Non-disjunction in meiosis

  • Normal meiosis: each pair of homologous chromosomes separates (meiosis I); then each pair of chromatids separates (meiosis II).
  • Non-disjunction: a pair fails to separate. Both go to the same pole.
  • (1) In meiosis I: all four gametes are abnormal. Two have one extra chromosome (n + 1); two have one missing (n − 1).
  • (2) In meiosis II: two gametes are normal, one is n + 1, one is n − 1.
  • Fertilisation by a normal gamete gives a zygote with 2n + 1 or 2n − 1. Every cell inherits it by mitosis.
Mission 4

One extra, or a whole extra set?

  • Aneuploidy: one chromosome extra or missing. Trisomy (2n + 1), monosomy (2n − 1).
  • Polyploidy: whole extra sets. Triploid (3n), tetraploid (4n).
  • Aneuploidy unbalances gene doses. In animals it usually causes serious developmental effects or death.
  • Polyploidy keeps every gene in the same ratio. Many plants tolerate it well; many crops are polyploid.
  • Say: "trisomy is aneuploidy" — not: "triploidy is aneuploidy". Triploidy is a whole extra set: polyploidy.
Mission 4

Worked example: counting chromosomes

  • Human body cells: 2n = 46. Normal gametes: n = 23.
  • Non-disjunction of one pair gives gametes with 24 or 22.
  • Zygote: 24 + 23 = 47 (one chromosome present three times).
  • Zygote: 22 + 23 = 45 (one chromosome present once).
  • Error in meiosis I: gametes 24, 24, 22, 22. Error in meiosis II: 24, 22, 23, 23.
Mission 4

Down syndrome

  • Down syndrome: three copies of chromosome 21 (trisomy 21). 47 chromosomes in each cell.
  • Most cases: non-disjunction of chromosome 21 in meiosis, usually in the egg-forming cells of the mother.
  • The chance rises with maternal age. One explanation: eggs pause part-way through meiosis I for decades, and the links holding chromosomes together weaken.
  • Effects vary widely between people; they include some degree of learning disability and a raised chance of heart defects.
  • The genes on chromosome 21 are normal. The problem is their dose.

Mission 4

Errors in mitosis, broken chromosomes

  • Non-disjunction in mitosis affects only the descendants of that one cell. A body-cell error is not inherited.
  • Structure can change too, when chromosomes break and rejoin wrongly:
  • Deletion (segment lost) · duplication (segment repeated) · inversion (segment reversed) · translocation (segment moved to another chromosome).
  • Deletions and duplications change gene dose. Breaks can disrupt genes at the break points.
  • These changes can cause genetic disorders, often with developmental effects.
Quick check
?In a hypothetical animal, a segment carrying several genes breaks off one copy of chromosome 4 and is lost, while the rest of that chromosome remains in the cell. Which statement best explains why this change can lead to a genetic disorder?
Mission check

?A species has 2n = 8. In one cell undergoing meiosis, one pair of homologous chromosomes fails to separate in meiosis I; meiosis II is normal. How many chromosomes are in each of the four gametes?

A normal gamete (4) fertilising these gives 9 or 7: one chromosome too many or too few, which unbalances hundreds of genes. A doubled set keeps them balanced.

Mission 5

Mission 5: If no animal can change its own genes, how can a whole population change over time?

Make a guess: If no animal can change its own genes, how can a whole population change over time?

Mission 5

Where variation comes from

  • Individuals in a population differ in phenotype.
  • Causes: genes, environment, and both together.
  • Mutation is the original source of all new alleles. It is random: it does not happen because an allele is needed.
  • Meiosis and random fertilisation make new combinations of existing alleles. They make no new alleles.
  • Say: "a mutation arose by chance" — not: "the organism mutated to survive".
Quick check
?A population of a plant species has two alleles of a flower-colour gene. A third allele, never seen before, appears in one seedling. Which process could have produced it?
Mission 5

More young than the habitat can support

  • Populations overproduce: far more offspring than can survive.
  • Resources are limited: food, water, nest sites, territory, light.
  • So members of the same species compete (intraspecific competition).
  • Selection pressures decide who survives and breeds:
    • biotic: predation, disease, competition;
    • abiotic: e.g. extreme cold, drought.
Mission 5

Natural selection, step by step

  • (1) Variation: individuals differ in a heritable trait.
  • (2) Selection pressure: e.g. a predator that finds pale beetles more easily on dark bark.
  • (3) Differential survival and reproduction: individuals with the advantageous phenotype are more likely to survive and leave more offspring.
  • (4) Inheritance: they pass on the advantageous allele. Over many generations its frequency rises.
  • No individual changed. The proportions in the population changed.
  • Selection does not create useful adaptations. It edits inherited features: some alleles become more frequent, others less.
Mission 5

Fitness

  • Fitness is about reproductive success: surviving offspring that go on to breed.
  • Not size, strength or speed. Those count only if they lead to more offspring.
  • CCEA defines fitness as the features that adapt an organism to its environment. Their effect is seen in survival and reproduction.
  • A phenotype that raises fitness in one environment may lower it in another.
  • Say: "leaves more surviving offspring" — not: "is the fittest because strongest".
Quick check
?In a population of red deer, stag X is the largest and strongest male but leaves two offspring that survive to breed. Stag Y is smaller and weaker but leaves six offspring that survive to breed. Which stag has the higher fitness, and why?
Mission 5

Only heritable traits evolve

  • Selection changes a population only if the trait is heritable: coded in the base sequence of DNA and passed on in gametes.
  • Acquired characteristics are not inherited: trained muscles, an injury, a learned skill.
Quick check
?A woman develops large, strong arm muscles through years of rowing training. Will her children inherit larger arm muscles as a result of her training?
Mission 5

Evolution: a change in allele frequency

  • Evolution = a change in the allele frequencies of a population over time.
  • Over long periods, adaptation and selection (with isolation) produce new species and the diversity of life.
Mission 5

Worked example: allele frequency

Illustrative data: a gene with alleles D and d, 100 individuals (200 alleles).

GenotypeDDDddd
Generation 143264
Generation 10164836
  • Count alleles, not individuals. Each DD has two D; each Dd has one.
  • Generation 1: D = 2 × 4 + 32 = 40 of 200. Frequency of D = 0.20.
  • Generation 10: D = 2 × 16 + 48 = 80 of 200. Frequency of D = 0.40.
  • The allele frequency changed: the population evolved.
  • Trap: 36% of generation 1 carry D, but D's frequency is 0.20.
Quick check
?A population of 50 plants contains 8 RR, 24 Rr and 18 rr. What is the frequency of allele R?
Mission check

?A student builds a flowchart model of how natural selection increased the mean beak depth of a population of a hypothetical seed-eating bird species over several generations of drought. Which flowchart best represents this process?

No bird changed its genes. The population changed because some birds left more offspring than others.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Why doesn't selection always push a population in the same direction?

Make a guess: Why doesn't selection always push a population in the same direction?

Mission 6

Three shapes of selection

  • Directional: one extreme is favoured. The mean shifts.
  • Stabilising: the intermediate (modal) phenotype is favoured; both extremes are selected against. The mean stays; the range narrows.
  • All of them change allele frequencies. Stabilising selection removes alleles for extreme phenotypes.
  • Say: "stabilising selection still changes allele frequencies" — not: "no change in the mean, so no evolution".
Mission 6

Named examples

  • Directional: antibiotic resistance in bacteria (see Mission 7).
  • Stabilising: human birth mass. In a large study of births at a London hospital, mortality was lowest at intermediate birth masses and higher for very light and very heavy babies (Karn and Penrose, 1951).
Quick check
?In a study of human births in the mid-twentieth century, babies of intermediate birth mass had the highest survival, while both very light and very heavy babies had higher mortality. Which type of selection does this show, and why?
Quick check
?Which statement about directional and stabilising selection is correct?
Mission 6

Polymorphic populations

  • Polymorphism: two or more distinct, inherited forms (morphs) of one species living together.
  • Morph frequencies are easy to count, so they show selection in action.
  • Peppered moth: the dark form (carbonaria) became common in soot-darkened industrial areas in the 1800s; after clean-air laws it declined again. Bird predation on moths that stand out is the main documented cause.
  • That is directional selection, first one way, then back.
  • Banded snails (Cepaea): shell colour and banding frequencies differ between habitats, linked to predation by thrushes.
Mission 6

When the heterozygote wins

  • Example: the sickle-cell allele where malaria is common.
    • Homozygous for sickle-cell allele: sickle-cell disease, often severe.
    • Homozygous for the normal allele: more likely to die of severe malaria.
    • Heterozygote: more resistant to severe malaria, and no severe sickle-cell disease.
  • So selection keeps both alleles in the population.
  • This is selection maintaining a polymorphism (a balanced polymorphism).
  • Where malaria is absent, the heterozygote has no advantage, and the sickle-cell allele is selected against.
Quick check
?In some regions where malaria is common, the allele for sickle-cell haemoglobin occurs at a relatively high frequency, although people homozygous for this allele have sickle-cell disease, which can be severe. People heterozygous for the allele are more resistant to severe malaria than people homozygous for the normal haemoglobin allele. Which statement best explains why the sickle-cell allele remains common in these regions?
Mission check

?In a hypothetical songbird, clutch size (number of eggs) is heritable. Parents with very large clutches cannot feed all their chicks, and many starve; parents with very small clutches raise few young. Over many generations, the mean clutch size stays at 4 eggs, but nests with 2 or 6 eggs become rarer. Which statement is correct?

The mean stayed put, yet the population still evolved. Selection can hold the middle as well as push an extreme.

Mission 7

Mission 7: Nobody designs a drug-resistant germ, so how do whole populations of them appear so quickly?

Make a guess: Nobody designs a drug-resistant germ, so how do whole populations of them appear so quickly?

Mission 7

How a resistant population arises

  • (1) A random mutation gives a few bacteria an allele for resistance, before any antibiotic is used. Bacteria can also gain resistance genes on plasmids from other bacteria.
  • (2) The antibiotic kills susceptible bacteria. Resistant ones survive.
  • (3) Survivors reproduce fast (binary fission) and pass on the resistance allele.
  • (4) Over generations the frequency of the allele rises: the population is now mostly resistant.
  • One extreme is favoured: directional selection (see Mission 6).
  • Say: "the antibiotic selected resistant bacteria" — not: "the bacteria became resistant".
Mission 7

Read the data: was resistance there first?

Lederberg and Lederberg (1952): replica plating with the bacterium E. coli.

  • (1) Bacteria spread on a master plate with no antibiotic. Each cell grows into a colony.
  • (2) A sterile velvet pad picks up cells from every colony and presses them onto plates with the antibiotic, keeping their positions.
  • (3) A few colonies grew on the antibiotic plates, at the same positions on every replica.
  • Cells taken from those positions on the master plate, never exposed to the drug, already included resistant cells.
  • So resistance arose before exposure. The drug selected it; it did not cause it.
Quick check
?In a replica-plating test, colonies grow on the antibiotic plate only at the positions of colonies 4 and 11 on the master plate, which has no antibiotic. Cells are then taken from colony 4 on the master plate and placed in a broth containing the antibiotic. What result is expected, and why?
Mission 7

Many drugs, many pests

  • Resistance to a drug usually depends on a specific gene or mutation. Strains can accumulate several: multiresistant bacteria, e.g. MRSA.
  • Every use of an antibiotic is a selection pressure. More use, more selection.
Quick check
?A hospital ward uses one particular antibiotic heavily. Over several years, an increasing proportion of the bacteria isolated from patients on the ward are found to be resistant to it. Which statement correctly explains this change?
Mission 7

Read the data: owls and snow

Tawny owls (Strix aluco) in Finland, studied 1981–2008 (Karell and colleagues, 2011).

  • Two colour forms: grey and brown. Colour is strongly inherited (about 80% heritable).
  • In winters with deep snow, brown owls survived less well than grey owls.
  • Over the study, snowy winters became less common as the climate warmed.
  • The proportion of brown owls rose.
  • Reading: less snow → brown owls' fitness rose → alleles for brown plumage rose in frequency. Evolution driven by climate change.
Quick check
?In Finland, tawny owls (Strix aluco) occur as a grey morph and a brown morph, and the colour is inherited. Brown owls survived snowy winters less well than grey owls. Between 1981 and 2008 winters became milder with less snow, and the proportion of brown owls rose markedly. What best explains the change?
Mission check

?A patient is given an antibiotic. Within a few days, the bacteria in the patient's infection change from almost all susceptible to almost all resistant. Which statement best explains how this happened so quickly?

Germs, insects, owls: the same rule. The allele was there first; the environment decided who left offspring.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • p + q = 1 and p² + 2pq + q² = 1: from the recessive phenotype (q²) you can find every allele and genotype frequency.
  • Allele frequencies stay constant only in a large, randomly mating population with no mutation, migration or selection; a change in allele frequencies is evolution.

Mission 2

  • Substitutions change one codon: same amino acid (degenerate code), one different amino acid, or an early stop. Deletions and insertions not in multiples of three shift the frame and change every downstream codon.

Mission 3

  • Mutations arise spontaneously from copying and repair errors, at random: the environment selects among them but does not direct them.
  • Mutation is the only source of new alleles; selection then changes their frequency.

Mission 4

  • Non-disjunction in meiosis gives gametes with n + 1 or n − 1; fertilisation then gives 2n + 1 or 2n − 1 in every cell.
  • Down syndrome is trisomy 21, usually from non-disjunction in the mother's meiosis; its chance rises with maternal age.
  • Aneuploidy unbalances gene dose; polyploidy adds whole sets and can create a new plant species in one generation.

Mission 5

  • Random mutation supplies new alleles; selection pressures make individuals with advantageous heritable phenotypes leave more offspring, so those alleles rise in frequency.
  • Evolution is a change in allele frequencies; fitness is reproductive success.

Mission 6

  • Directional selection favours one extreme and shifts the mean; stabilising selection favours the intermediate and narrows the range; both change allele frequencies.
  • Heterozygote advantage keeps both alleles in a population.

Mission 7

  • Resistance alleles arise by random mutation (or gene transfer) before exposure; the drug or pesticide kills susceptible individuals, so resistant ones reproduce and the allele's frequency rises.
