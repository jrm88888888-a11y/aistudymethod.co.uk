Mission 1

Why imbalances arise

  • Cyclical deficit: in a boom, incomes rise and spending on imports rises. Firms also sell more at home and less abroad. It shrinks in a downturn.
  • Structural deficit: it persists even at normal levels of output, because of weak competitiveness:
    • productivity growing more slowly than in other countries
    • inflation (costs and prices) higher than in trading partners
    • a strong, overvalued exchange rate
    • non-price weakness: quality, design, a small export sector
  • Saving and investment: a country that invests more than it saves must borrow the gap from abroad, so it runs a current account deficit. A country that saves more than it invests runs a surplus.
  • External shocks: a rise in oil prices widens deficits of oil importers and surpluses of oil exporters; a world recession cuts everyone's exports.
  • Say: "a current account deficit" — not: "a balance of payments deficit".

Mission 1

A persistent deficit: why it can matter

  • Exchange rate: pressure to depreciate; dearer imports add to inflation.
  • Interest rates: higher rates may be needed to attract the inflows that finance the deficit. This hurts investment.
  • Foreign ownership: assets are sold to foreigners, so future profits and dividends flow out (primary income debits).
  • Debt: borrowing builds up debt to the rest of the world, with interest to pay.
  • Credit rating: a large, persistent deficit can lower it, raising the cost of borrowing.
  • Demand management: the government may have to use contractionary fiscal or monetary policy to cut spending on imports, at a cost to output and jobs.
  • Economic growth: negative net exports are a drag on AD now, and jobs are lost in export and import-competing industries. But if the deficit pays for investment, it can raise growth later.
Mission 1

A persistent surplus: problems too

  • Domestic consumption and investment: lower than the economy's output. Living standards could be higher now.
  • Exchange rate: pressure to appreciate, which erodes export competitiveness over time.
  • Inflation: strong export demand adds to AD and can pull prices up; an appreciating currency makes imports cheaper, which pulls the other way.
  • Employment: good for jobs in export industries, while it lasts.
  • The surplus is lent or invested abroad: it may earn low returns or be lost.
  • One country's surplus is others' deficit: trading partners may complain or turn to protectionism.
Quick check

Mission 1

Two kinds of policy

  • Expenditure switching: changes relative prices, so spending switches from imports to home-produced goods.
    • a depreciation or devaluation of the currency
    • protectionism: tariffs, quotas, export subsidies
  • Expenditure reducing: cuts total spending, so imports fall too.
    • contractionary fiscal policy: higher taxes, lower government spending
    • contractionary monetary policy: higher interest rates
  • Supply-side policies: long-term competitiveness: training, infrastructure, research, flexible markets.
  • Say: "expenditure reducing cuts all spending, so imports fall with it" — not: "it cuts spending on imports only".
Mission 1

Worked example: the cost of expenditure reducing

  • Illustrative data. Current account deficit: 20 bn. GDP: 1,000 bn.
  • For every 1 bn fall in income, spending on imports falls by 0.25 bn.
  • This is the marginal propensity to import (MPM) = 0.25.
  • Fall in income needed = 20 ÷ 0.25 = 80 bn.
  • That is 80 ÷ 1,000 × 100 = 8% of GDP: a deep recession, with higher unemployment.
  • Not 20 bn: most of the lost spending falls on domestic goods, not imports.

Mission 1

Switching, supply side and surpluses

  • Depreciation: works only if demand for exports and imports is elastic enough (Marshall–Lerner), and only after a lag (J-curve); dearer imports raise inflation.
  • Protectionism: risks retaliation, can break trade rules (WTO), and raises prices for consumers and firms.
  • Supply-side policies: tackle the cause of a structural deficit, but are slow and uncertain.
  • To reduce a surplus:
    • expansionary fiscal or monetary policy: more spending at home, so more imports
    • allow the currency to appreciate
    • remove barriers to imports

Mission 1Evaluate

How much does a deficit matter, and which policy works?

It depends on:

  • The cause: a cyclical deficit corrects itself; a structural one needs supply-side policy.
  • Size, persistence and financing: a small deficit funded by long-term FDI is safer than a large one funded by short-term portfolio flows ("hot money"), which can leave suddenly.
  • What it pays for: investment can raise future output; consumption cannot repay the debt.
  • Conflicts with other objectives: expenditure reducing costs growth and jobs; depreciation adds to inflation.
  • Elasticities and time lags: switching works slowly, and only if demand responds enough.
Mission check

?Country W has a large, persistent current account surplus that its trading partners complain about. Which policy would reduce the surplus by expenditure switching?

Switching policies work through relative prices. To shrink a surplus, they must make imports relatively cheaper, not dearer.

Mission 2

What competitiveness means

  • International competitiveness: the ability of a country's firms to sell goods and services, at home and abroad, against foreign rivals.
  • Price competitiveness: selling at lower prices than rivals for similar quality.
  • Non-price competitiveness: quality, design, reliability, innovation, brand, after-sales service, delivery times.
  • Two price measures: relative unit labour costs and relative export prices.
Mission 2

Unit labour costs

  • Unit labour cost (ULC) = total labour costs ÷ output.
  • Or: labour cost per worker ÷ output per worker (productivity).
  • Illustrative data, same currency:
Labour cost per workerOutput per workerULC
Country A50,0002,000 units50,000 ÷ 2,000 = 25
Country B20,000500 units20,000 ÷ 500 = 40
  • A pays 2.5 times the wage, but its ULC is lower: each A worker produces 4 times as much.
  • Productivity is the key: it lets firms pay higher wages without raising ULC.
  • Say: "unit labour costs rose" — not: "wages rose", unless productivity stood still.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. In a year, labour cost per worker rises by 4% and output per worker rises by 4%. What happens to unit labour costs?
Mission 2

Worked example: relative unit labour costs

  • Relative ULC = home ULC ÷ trading partners' ULC (in a common currency) × 100.
  • Rising: less competitive than in the base year. Falling: more competitive.
  • Illustrative data. Year 1: home ULC = partners' ULC = 40. Index = 100.
  • Year 2: home pay per worker +5%, productivity +1%.
    • Home ULC = 40 × 1.05 ÷ 1.01 = 41.58 (about +4%: 5% − 1%).
    • Partners' ULC +2% = 40.80.
    • Relative ULC = 41.58 ÷ 40.80 × 100 = 101.9: less competitive.
  • Now the home currency depreciates 5% against partners' currencies.
    • Home ULC in partners' currency falls by 5%: 101.9 × 0.95 = 96.8: more competitive.
  • So the exchange rate can outweigh wage and productivity changes.
Mission 2

Relative export prices

  • Relative export prices = index of home export prices ÷ index of rivals' export prices (in a common currency) × 100.
  • Illustrative data: home export price index 104, rivals' 102. Relative export prices = 104 ÷ 102 × 100 = 102.0: less price competitive.
  • Home currency depreciates 3%: 104 × 0.97 ÷ 102 × 100 = 98.9: more price competitive.
  • Export prices depend on ULC, but also on other costs (energy, materials), profit margins and the exchange rate.
  • Say: "relative export prices" compare our export prices with rivals' export prices — not: with our import prices (that is the terms of trade).
Quick check

Mission 2

What drives competitiveness

  • Price factors:
    • wage costs relative to productivity (investment, skills, technology, management)
    • the exchange rate
    • inflation relative to trading partners
    • taxes, regulation, energy costs, infrastructure
  • Non-price factors: quality, design, innovation (research and development), reliability, branding.
  • So productivity, inflation and the exchange rate all influence the current account.

Mission 2

Benefits and problems

  • Benefits of being competitive:
    • export-led growth and more jobs
    • a stronger current account
    • more inward FDI
    • economies of scale from bigger markets
  • Problems of being uncompetitive:
    • current account deficits and falling shares of world markets
    • deindustrialisation and structural unemployment in exposed industries
    • slower growth; pressure on the currency, so dearer imports and inflation
    • less inward investment
  • Competing on low wages keeps costs down but also keeps living standards down. Competing on productivity raises both.
Mission 2Evaluate

How useful are the measures?

It depends on:

  • Non-price factors: a country with high relative ULC can still win on quality and brand.
  • Sector mix: ULC averages over the whole economy; exporters may differ from the average.
  • The exchange rate: it can swing relative costs fast, and reverse just as fast.
  • The route: lower relative ULC from productivity growth is lasting; from wage cuts it lowers living standards.
Mission check

Competitiveness is relative and measured in a common currency. Pay, productivity and the exchange rate all count.

Mission 3

An exchange rate is a price

  • Exchange rate: the price of one currency in terms of another.
  • Example: £1 = $1.25. So $1 = 1 ÷ 1.25 = £0.80.
  • Appreciation: the currency buys more of another currency.
  • Depreciation: the currency buys less of another currency.
  • Floating exchange rate: set by demand and supply in the foreign exchange market, with no target set by the government or central bank.
  • In a fixed system, the government or central bank sets the rate; a change is a devaluation or revaluation (see Mission 4).
  • Say: "the pound appreciated against the dollar" — not: "the exchange rate went up", which depends on which way the rate is quoted.
Mission 3

Worked example: one price, two currencies

  • Rule, when the rate is quoted as foreign currency per 1 unit of home currency: from home to foreign, multiply by the rate; from foreign to home, divide.
  • Rate £1 = $1.30.
  • A £500 phone sells in the US for 500 × 1.30 = $650.
  • $65 jeans cost a UK buyer 65 ÷ 1.30 = £50.
  • The pound appreciates to $1.50. Phone: 500 × 1.50 = $750. Jeans: 65 ÷ 1.50 = £43.33.
  • Appreciation: exports cost foreigners more; imports cost home buyers less.
Mission 3

Worked example: how much did it change?

  • % change = (new rate − old rate) ÷ old rate × 100.
  • Real data: the euro averaged $1.1830 in 2021 and $1.0534 in 2022 (about $1.18 and $1.05).
  • Euro: (1.0534 − 1.1830) ÷ 1.1830 × 100 = −11.0%. The euro depreciated against the dollar.
  • Flip the rate: the dollar averaged 1 ÷ 1.1830 = €0.8453 in 2021 and 1 ÷ 1.0534 = €0.9493 in 2022.
  • Dollar: (0.9493 − 0.8453) ÷ 0.8453 × 100 = +12.3%. The dollar appreciated against the euro.
  • Not the same size: each % change uses a different starting value.
  • A €100 hotel room cost a US visitor $118.30 at the 2021 average rate and $105.34 at the 2022 average.

Mission 3

The foreign exchange market

  • Currencies are traded worldwide, around the clock, mainly through banks. Buyers and sellers include firms, investors, speculators, tourists and central banks.
  • Much of the trading is for financial investment and speculation, not to pay for goods.
  • Demand for a currency comes from foreigners who need it: to buy its exports and to make inflows of investment.
  • Supply of a currency comes from its own residents selling it: to buy imports and to invest abroad (outflows).
  • Demand slopes down: a cheaper currency makes the country's goods and assets cheaper to foreigners, so more of it is demanded.
  • Supply slopes up: a dearer currency makes imports cheaper to residents, so they supply more of it.
  • Equilibrium where demand = supply. Above it, a surplus of the currency pushes the rate down; below it, a shortage pushes it up.

Mission 3

Real case: the euro and the dollar in 2022

  • US central bank (the Federal Reserve): top of its target range for interest rates 0.25% at the start of 2022, 4.50% at the end.
  • ECB deposit facility rate: −0.50% until July 2022, 2.00% at the end of 2022.
  • US interest rates rose further than euro-area rates.
  • Theory: demand for dollars rises as savers buy US assets, so the dollar should appreciate against the euro.
  • Data: the dollar rose 12.3% against the euro, comparing 2021 and 2022 averages.
  • The data fit the theory. But other factors were also changing, so one year's data cannot prove the cause.
Mission 3Evaluate

How far can you predict a currency?

  • It depends on:
    • several factors at once: interest rates may rise while growth slows; the effects can offset
    • expectations: speculators act on what they expect, so a rate can move before anything else changes
    • relative, not absolute: what matters is a country's interest rates, inflation and growth compared with other countries'
    • time horizon: day to day, financial flows dominate; over years, trade and competitiveness matter more
  • A central bank may also intervene (see Mission 4).

Mission 4

Three exchange rate systems

  • Floating: demand and supply in the currency market set the rate (see Mission 3). A rise is an appreciation, a fall a depreciation.
  • Fixed (a peg): the central bank commits to one rate against another currency or a basket. A deliberate cut in the official rate is a devaluation; a deliberate rise, a revaluation.
  • Managed float: the rate floats, but the central bank steps in to limit or steer its movements.
  • Real examples: the Hong Kong dollar has been pegged to the US dollar at about 7.80 since 1983; the euro floats; Singapore manages its currency against a basket of its trading partners' currencies.
  • Say: "the pound depreciated" for a floating currency — not: "the pound was devalued". Devaluation is a policy decision under a fixed rate.
Quick check
?A country pegs its currency to the US dollar. Its government announces that the official rate will be cut from 2.0 to 1.6 US dollars per unit of its currency. Which term describes this?
Mission 4

Holding a fixed rate

  • Step 1: the market for the currency clears at the peg.
  • Step 2: demand for the currency falls (D1 → D2). At the peg, supply now exceeds demand: the market rate would fall below the peg.
  • Step 3: the central bank closes the gap. It buys its own currency, paying with foreign currency reserves. This adds to demand: D2 → D3, back to the peg.
  • Other tools: raise interest rates (attracts financial inflows, so more demand for the currency); exchange controls (limits on buying foreign currency); borrowing reserves from abroad.
  • Pressure the other way (demand rises): the central bank sells its own currency and builds up reserves, or cuts interest rates.

Mission 5

Steps of economic integration

  • Countries can tie their economies together step by step.
  • Free trade area: no tariffs or quotas between members; each keeps its own tariffs on outsiders.
  • Customs union: free trade area + a common external tariff.
  • Common market: customs union + free movement of labour and capital.
  • Monetary union: members share a single currency, managed by one central bank that sets one monetary policy (one interest rate) for all.
  • No exchange rate between members, so no member can devalue against another.
  • Say: "a single currency and a single monetary policy" — not: "a single currency" alone.
Mission 5

The eurozone and the ECB

  • The euro began on 1 January 1999 (accounts and electronic payments); notes and coins followed in 2002.
  • In 2026, 21 of the 27 EU countries use the euro. Bulgaria joined most recently (2026).
  • The European Central Bank (ECB) sets monetary policy for the whole euro area. Its Governing Council includes the governors of every member's national central bank.
  • Primary objective: price stability, aiming for 2% inflation over the medium term.
  • One ECB interest rate applies in every member, whatever its own inflation or unemployment.
  • Fiscal policy (taxes, government spending) stays with each national government, within EU rules on deficits and debt.
Quick check
?Three neighbouring countries already have free trade, a common external tariff and free movement of workers and capital between them. Which extra step would turn them into a monetary union?
Mission 5

What a single currency gains

  • No transaction costs: no fees for changing currency when trading within the union.
  • No exchange-rate uncertainty within the union: firms can price and plan without currency risk.
  • So more trade and more investment (FDI) between members are likely.
  • Price transparency: prices in one currency are easy to compare, which increases competition.
  • Credibility: a country with a history of high inflation can "borrow" the low-inflation record of a strong central bank, so inflation expectations and interest rates may fall.
  • Illustrative data: a firm sells 10 million worth of goods a year to other members, and currency changing costs 1%. Single currency saves 10 m × 0.01 = 0.1 m a year.
Mission 5

What a member gives up

  • No independent monetary policy: the central bank sets one interest rate for the whole union.
  • No exchange rate of its own to fall when its exports become uncompetitive.
  • So it loses two tools for dealing with an asymmetric shock: a shock that hits one member but not others.
  • A rate that suits the average member may be too high for one in recession and too low for one that is booming: "one size fits all".
  • Fiscal rules limit how far a government can borrow to support demand.
  • Real data: in 2013, unemployment averaged 27.8% in Greece and 4.9% in Germany. Both had the same ECB interest rate.
  • Adjustment then falls on wages and prices: an internal devaluation, which is usually slow and painful.
Mission 5

Worked example: one interest rate, different real rates

  • Illustrative data. Union central bank rate = 2% for all members.
  • Real interest rate ≈ nominal rate − inflation rate.
  • Country X is booming; inflation 5%. Real rate ≈ 2 − 5 = −3%.
  • Country Y is in recession; inflation 1%. Real rate ≈ 2 − 1 = +1%.
  • Borrowing is cheapest in real terms where the economy is already overheating.
  • So one rate can make the boom bigger in X and the slump deeper in Y.

Mission 5

When does sharing a currency work? The optimal currency area

  • Optimal currency area (Mundell, 1961): a region where the gains of one currency outweigh the costs of losing separate exchange rates.
  • Conditions that make a monetary union more likely to succeed:
    • similar economic cycles: shocks hit members at the same time (symmetric), so one interest rate suits them all
    • labour mobility: workers move from slumping to booming regions
    • flexible wages and prices: costs can fall quickly where demand is weak
    • fiscal transfers: a shared budget sends money to regions in recession
    • high trade between members: the savings on transaction costs and currency risk are larger
  • Compare: US states share one currency, with a large federal budget that pays more benefits and collects less tax in states with weak economies.
  • Each condition is a matter of degree. Economists disagree on how far the eurozone meets them.
Quick check
?Two countries are thinking of adopting a single currency. Which feature would make them most suitable for one?
Mission 5Evaluate

Is a single currency worth it?

  • It depends on:
    • how similar members' economies are: symmetric shocks make one interest rate suit everyone
    • how well other shock absorbers work: labour mobility, flexible wages, fiscal transfers
    • how much members trade with each other: bigger trade means bigger gains from one currency
    • credibility: a country with a record of high inflation may gain more than one with a trusted central bank
  • Time matters: gains (lower costs, more trade) build slowly; the cost of a bad shock can arrive suddenly.
Mission check

?Northland and Southland share one currency and one central bank. World demand for timber, Southland's main export, collapses. Northland sells no timber and is unaffected. Why might Southland's recession last longer than it would with its own floating currency?

Sharing a currency removes two shock absorbers, so a shock that hits one member alone must be absorbed by its wages, prices and jobs.

Mission 6

Growth is not the same as development

  • Economic growth: an increase in real GDP (output) over time.
  • Economic development: an improvement in people's well-being and living standards. It is multidimensional:
    • higher incomes and less poverty
    • better health and longer lives
    • more education and skills
    • more freedom and choice (Amartya Sen: development as expanding what people are able to do and be)
  • Growth can happen without much development: the gains may go to a few, or come with pollution.
  • Development can happen with slow growth: cheap public health and schooling can lengthen and improve lives.
  • Say: "growth is a rise in real output; development is a rise in well-being" — not: "development means growth".
Mission 6

Monetary indicators

  • GDP per capita: output per person. GNI per capita adds net income from abroad.
  • Compare countries at purchasing power parity (PPP): it corrects for differences in price levels (prices tend to be lower in poorer countries).
Mission 6

Non-monetary indicators

  • Health: life expectancy at birth, infant mortality rate, maternal mortality.
  • Education: adult literacy rate, years of schooling.
  • Inequality: Gini coefficient, income share of the richest 10%.
  • One indicator alone gives only part of the picture.
Quick check
?A government wants one indicator to track whether its new clinics are reducing deaths among babies. Which is best?
Mission 6

The Human Development Index (HDI)

  • Published by the UN Development Programme (UNDP) since 1990.
  • Three dimensions, four indicators:
    • a long and healthy life: life expectancy at birth
    • knowledge (education): expected years of schooling for children; mean years of schooling for adults
    • a decent standard of living: GNI per capita at PPP
  • Each dimension is scored from 0 to 1, then combined. The HDI runs from 0 to 1.
  • UNDP groups: very high (0.800 and above), high (0.700–0.799), medium (0.550–0.699), low (below 0.550).
  • Say: "HDI measures health, education and income" — not: "HDI measures income".

  • Each dimension is scaled between a minimum and a maximum (life expectancy: 20 to 85 years):
    • Health: P (72 − 20) ÷ 65 = 0.800; Q (59 − 20) ÷ 65 = 0.600
    • Education: P 0.675; Q 0.533
    • Income: both 0.696
  • HDI: P = 0.722 (high); Q = 0.606 (medium).
  • Same income, but P's people live longer and learn more.
  • Two design features:
    • Income enters as a logarithm: doubling P's income to 20,000 raises HDI only to 0.756. An extra dollar matters more to the poor.
    • The three scores are combined by a geometric mean, so a very low score in one dimension pulls the HDI down more than a simple average would.

Mission 6

Why development is hard to measure

  • Data quality: large informal and subsistence sectors; surveys are costly and infrequent.
  • Averages hide distribution: a rising mean can leave the poor behind.
  • Value judgements: which dimensions count, and how to weight them?
  • Missing dimensions: freedom, security, culture and the environment are hard to put in numbers.
  • PPP estimates depend on price surveys that may be out of date.
Mission 6

How growth and development are linked

  • Growth can fund development:
    • higher incomes for families
    • more tax revenue for schools, clinics, water and roads
  • Development can fuel growth: healthier, better-educated workers are more productive.
  • But growth may not bring development if:
    • the gains go to a few (rising inequality)
    • growth comes from exporting a resource that employs few people
    • it damages health and the environment
  • So the link is likely but not automatic: it depends on how the gains are shared and spent.
Mission 6Evaluate

Is HDI a better measure of development than GNI per capita?

It depends on:

  • The purpose: HDI captures health and education; GNI per capita at PPP better shows material living standards.
  • Distribution: both are averages.
  • What is left out: HDI has no measure of inequality, freedom, the environment or happiness.
  • Weights: HDI weights its three dimensions equally. That is a judgement, not a fact.
  • So: use HDI as a better starting point than income alone, alongside other indicators.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Imbalances can be cyclical or structural, and reflect saving and investment.
  • Persistent deficits and persistent surpluses both carry costs.
  • Expenditure switching changes relative prices; expenditure reducing cuts total spending (and output); supply-side policy works on competitiveness over time.

Mission 2

  • ULC = labour cost per worker ÷ output per worker. High wages with high productivity can still mean low ULC.
  • Relative ULC and relative export prices compare a country with its rivals in a common currency. Rising = less competitive.
  • Productivity, inflation, the exchange rate and non-price factors drive competitiveness.

Mission 3

  • A floating exchange rate is set by demand for and supply of a currency.
  • Demand comes from foreigners buying exports and investing in; supply from residents buying imports and investing abroad.
  • % change = (new − old) ÷ old × 100; the two currencies' changes are not the same size.

Mission 4

  • Floating: market sets the rate (appreciation/depreciation). Fixed: the central bank holds it (devaluation/revaluation). Managed: it floats, with intervention.
  • A peg is held by buying or selling the currency with reserves, and by interest rates.

Mission 5

  • A monetary union shares one currency and one central bank setting one interest rate.
  • Gains: no transaction costs or currency risk within the union, more trade and investment, price transparency. Costs: no own interest rate or exchange rate to deal with asymmetric shocks.
  • It works best in an optimal currency area: similar cycles, mobile labour, flexible wages, fiscal transfers, high trade.

Mission 6

  • Growth is a rise in real output; development is a rise in well-being on many dimensions.
  • HDI combines health, education and income (GNI per capita at PPP) into one index from 0 to 1.
  • No single number captures development: use several indicators.
