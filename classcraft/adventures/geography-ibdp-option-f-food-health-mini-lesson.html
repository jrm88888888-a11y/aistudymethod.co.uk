Calories per person

  • It measures supply, not intake: food later wasted in shops and homes still counts as supply.

Mission 1

Undernourishment: a regional pattern

  • Prevalence of undernourishment (PoU): the share of people whose calorie intake is insufficient.
  • 2025: 7.8% of the world's population, around 645 million people, down from 8.1% in 2024.
  • Africa: 20.0%, about 309 million people, against 292 million in Asia. Africa now has more hungry people than Asia.
  • More than half of Africa's people (56.6%) faced moderate or severe food insecurity in 2025.
Mission 1

Indicators of malnutrition

  • Stunting: low height-for-age, a sign of long-term (chronic) undernutrition. Wasting: low weight-for-height, a sign of recent, acute undernutrition.
  • Malnutrition also includes vitamin and mineral deficiencies, and overweight and obesity.
  • 150 million children under five are stunted (SOFI 2026).
  • 30.7% of women aged 15–49 had anaemia in 2023 (SOFI 2025).
Quick check

Mission 1

A composite index: the Global Hunger Index (GHI)

  • The GHI combines four indicators:
    • undernourishment and child mortality: one-third of the score each;
    • child stunting and child wasting: one-sixth each.
  • Scores run from 0 (no hunger) to 100. Bands: low (≤9.9), moderate (10.0–19.9), serious (20.0–34.9), alarming (35.0–49.9), extremely alarming (≥50.0).
  • Using both stunting and wasting captures chronic and acute undernutrition.
Mission 1

The GHI's global pattern, 2026

  • World score: 18.1 (moderate), higher than in 2018, which interrupts the decline since 2000.
  • Hunger is serious in Africa south of the Sahara and South Asia.
  • Eight countries are alarming. Four have scores: Somalia 47.0 (122nd of 122 ranked), the Democratic Republic of the Congo 39.5, Chad 38.0 and Madagascar 36.5.
  • Burundi, South Sudan, Sudan and Yemen are rated alarming provisionally, without a score, because data are missing.
Mission 1

The Global Food Security Index (GFSI)

  • Economist Impact's index scores 113 countries on 68 drivers in four pillars: affordability, availability, quality and safety, and sustainability and adaptation.
  • 2022 (its 11th edition, the latest found): Finland top with 83.7, then Ireland (81.7) and Norway (80.5).
  • It found food security "continued to deteriorate after hitting its peak in 2019".
  • It was "Developed by Economist Impact and supported by Corteva Agriscience", an agribusiness.
Quick check

Mission 1

Exam move: judging indicators (AO3)

  • Supply (DES), outcome (PoU, stunting, wasting) and composite (GHI, GFSI) indicators answer different questions.
  • DES is supply, not intake, and a national average hides who goes without.
  • PoU is a modelled estimate; the GHI leans on child indicators and cannot score countries with missing data, such as Yemen and Sudan.
  • The GFSI figures date from 2022, and its sponsor sells seeds and crop chemicals: check the date, and ask who funds an index.
  • Exam move: name the indicator, say what it measures, and give one limitation before you use its numbers.
Mission check

the 2023 supply of 3,016 kcal is an average, while the hunger figure counts people in 2025. Europe's supply per person is over a third larger than Africa's (2,556 kcal), and 20.0% of Africans are undernourished. Supply figures also include food later wasted. Hunger is about who can get food, so geographers use several indicators.

Mission 2

The nutrition transition (Popkin)

  • Barry Popkin's nutrition transition describes how diets and activity shift as societies change:
    • Pattern 2, famine: settled farming societies with recurrent famine;
    • Pattern 3, receding famine: "famine begins to recede as income rises";
    • Pattern 4, nutrition-related non-communicable disease: more processed food, more eating away from home, more edible oils and sugar-sweetened drinks, and less physical activity;
    • Pattern 5, behavioural change: people deliberately eat better and move more.
  • Pattern 1 was the diet of hunter-gatherers.
Mission 2

Faster, and earlier

  • Popkin and Ng: in lower-income countries "changes are occurring at great speed and at earlier stages of countries' economic and social development".
  • Asia's food supply rose from 2,694 to 2,991 kcal per person per day (2010–2023).
  • Animal products' share of Asia's protein supply rose from 34% to 37%, "reflecting rising incomes and changing consumption patterns in the region".
Quick check

Mission 2

Physical processes behind food consumption

  • Climate, soils and relief set which crops and livestock can be produced locally, and so shape traditional diets.
  • Hazards cut harvests and raise prices. In Bangladesh in 1974, nationwide flooding sharply raised food prices and destroyed a crop, cutting work for farm labourers.
  • Physical limits matter less where trade, irrigation and income let people buy food from elsewhere.
Mission 2

Human processes behind food consumption

  • Income: richer households buy more animal products, oils and processed food (Asia, screen 2.2).
  • Urbanisation: city jobs, less physical work, and more ready-made food.
  • Prices and trade: the cost of food against people's income.
  • Culture and religion: food customs and dietary rules.
  • Marketing and conflict: advertising shapes choices; conflict cuts supplies (Missions 8 and 9).
Mission 2

The double burden of malnutrition

  • The double burden of malnutrition is "the coexistence of undernutrition along with overweight, obesity or diet-related noncommunicable diseases".
  • 2022: 2.5 billion adults were overweight, including 890 million living with obesity; 390 million adults were underweight.
  • 2022: 149 million children under five were stunted, 45 million wasted and 37 million overweight.
Mission 2

Why the double burden is growing

  • A 2020 Lancet review finds the double burden "affects most low-income and middle-income countries", and has increased in the poorest of them, mainly because overweight is rising.
  • Causes: "the availability of cheap ultra-processed food and beverages", and "major reductions in physical activity" at work, in transport and at home.
  • Indonesia is "the largest country with a severe DBM".
Mission 2

The trends

  • Child stunting fell from 26.4% (2012) to 23.2% (2024) (SOFI 2025).
  • Child wasting: 7.4% to 6.6%; child overweight: 5.3% to 5.5% (2012–2024, SOFI 2025).
  • Adult obesity rose from 12.1% (2012) to 16.2% (2024) (SOFI 2026).
  • Undernutrition is falling slowly; obesity is rising.
Mission 2

Exam move: linking food and health (synthesis)

  • Diet links the two geographies. Overweight and obesity are metabolic risk factors for non-communicable diseases such as heart disease and diabetes.
  • So the nutrition transition feeds the epidemiological transition (Mission 3).
  • Limits of the model: the patterns overlap within one country, and poorer groups can reach pattern 4 before famine has fully receded: the double burden.
  • Exam move: in "Examine" answers, show the link both ways: poor diets raise disease, and disease (such as diarrhoea) worsens undernutrition.
Mission check

?How can the world, and even one country, have too little food and too much at once?

The nutrition transition brings cheap processed food and less activity faster than poverty disappears. The result is a double burden of malnutrition: stunted children and overweight adults in the same countries, with Indonesia the largest severe case.

Mission 3

Healthy life expectancy: the global pattern

  • Healthy life expectancy (HALE): the years a newborn can expect to live in full health. World HALE in 2023: 62.8 years.
  • By WHO region, 2023:
    • Western Pacific 67.3 · Europe 66.7 · Americas 64.4
    • South-East Asia 61.3 · Eastern Mediterranean 60.4 · Africa 56.2.
  • HALE has recovered more slowly than life expectancy since COVID-19; it was still 0.4 years below its 2019 level in 2023.
Mission 3

Outcomes: infants and mothers

  • IMR: deaths before age 1 per 1,000 live births. MMR: maternal deaths per 100,000 live births.
  • IMR, 2024: world 27.7 per 1,000; low-income countries 46.7; high-income countries 4.2.
  • About 260,000 women died during and after pregnancy and childbirth in 2023: an MMR of 197 per 100,000 live births.
  • Sub-Saharan Africa had around 70% of maternal deaths. Nigeria's MMR was 993; Japan's was 3.
Mission 3

Inputs: sanitation and doctors

  • Inputs are resources put into health; outcomes (HALE, IMR, MMR) are what results.
  • 2024: 58% of the world used safely managed sanitation, and 3.4 billion people lacked it; 354 million practised open defecation.
  • WHO African Region 27%; Europe 80%; Niger 9%.
  • Doctors per 10,000 people (2024): high-income countries 34.5; low-income countries 3.2.

Mission 3

The epidemiological transition (Omran, 1971)

  • Abdel Omran described three successive stages:
    • "The Age of Pestilence and Famine";
    • "The Age of Receding Pandemics";
    • "The Age of Degenerative and Man-Made Diseases".
  • Over time, "pandemics of infection are gradually displaced by degenerative and man-made diseases".
  • It runs alongside the demographic transition: falling death rates first, then longer lives.
Mission 3

The diseases continuum

  • Diseases of poverty: mostly communicable, such as diarrhoea, malaria and tuberculosis, plus undernutrition and deaths in childbirth.
  • Diseases of affluence: non-communicable diseases (NCDs): cardiovascular disease, cancers, chronic respiratory disease and diabetes.
  • WHO's four main behavioural NCD risks: tobacco, physical inactivity, harmful use of alcohol and unhealthy diets.
  • The label "affluence" misleads: 82% of the 18 million NCD deaths before age 70 in 2021 were in low- and middle-income countries.
Mission 3

The evidence for the transition

  • NCDs caused 58% of deaths worldwide in 2000 and 74% in 2023.
  • Eight of the ten leading causes of death in 2023 were NCDs.
  • In 2023, for the first time, communicable diseases caused less than half of all deaths in low-income countries. The transition is uneven: in the poorest countries, infections still cause a large share of deaths.
Mission 3

Ageing and the disease burden

  • By 2030, 1 in 6 people will be 60 or over. People aged 60+ will rise from 1 billion (2020) to 2.1 billion (2050).
  • People aged 80+ are expected to triple to 426 million by 2050; 80% of older people will live in low- and middle-income countries.
  • Common conditions: hearing loss, cataracts, back pain and osteoarthritis, COPD, diabetes, depression and dementia.
  • Dementia rose from the 19th to the 5th leading cause of death between 2000 and 2023.
Quick check
?In a country, NCD death rates at each age have not changed, yet NCDs' share of all deaths rises. What best explains this?
Mission 3

Exam move: one transition, uneven outcomes (AO3)

  • The guide notes the transition "does not apply equally to all sectors of society".
  • Between countries: low-income countries face infections and NCDs together, a double burden of disease. Within countries, poorer groups often keep the diseases of poverty while gaining the risks of the nutrition transition (Mission 2).
  • Limits: Omran's model rests mainly on Western experience. Olshansky and Ault (1986) proposed a fourth stage, "the age of delayed degenerative diseases".
  • New and re-emerging infections, such as HIV and COVID-19 (Missions 11–12), do not fit a one-way shift.
  • Exam move: when you use the model, say where and for whom it fits, and where it does not.
Mission check

?NCDs' share of deaths rose from 58% to 74%. Good news or bad news?

Both. The rise from 58% to 74% is Omran's epidemiological transition: infections recede, people live longer, and NCDs and dementia take over. But HALE trails life expectancy, ageing adds chronic illness, and the poorest countries now face both burdens at once.

Mission 4

Food production as a system

  • Inputs: sunlight, rain and irrigation water, seed, fertiliser, animal feed, fuel, labour and capital.
  • Stores: soil, groundwater, grain stores, livestock.
  • Transfers: animals eating feed, processing, transport.
  • Outputs: food, waste, greenhouse gases and polluted runoff.
  • The merit of the systems approach: it shows where energy and water enter and leak out, so different farms and places can be compared.
Mission 4

Energy efficiency

  • Energy efficiency compares the energy put in with the food energy that comes out.
  • A US study (2003) found producing 1 kcal of plant protein needs about 2.2 kcal of fossil energy; animal protein averaged 25 kcal.
  • Ratios ranged from 4:1 for broiler chicken to 40:1 for beef and 57:1 for lamb.
  • Each step up a food chain loses energy, so feeding crops to animals is less efficient than eating the crops.
Calculate
?A 2013 study found that 36% of the calories produced by the world's crops are used as animal feed, and only 12% of those feed calories end up in human diets as meat and other animal products. Out of every 100 calories of crops, how many reach people as animal products?
Mission 4

Water footprints

  • A water footprint measures the fresh water consumed or polluted to make a product.
    • Green: rainwater stored in the soil and used by plants.
    • Blue: water taken from rivers, lakes or groundwater.
    • Grey: the fresh water needed to dilute pollution to meet water-quality standards.
  • Global averages: beef 15,400 litres per kg; pork 6,000; chicken 4,300.
Mission 4

Per calorie and per gram of protein

  • Per calorie, beef's average water footprint is 20 times that of cereals and starchy roots.
  • Per gram of protein, milk, eggs and chicken need 1.5 times as much water as pulses.
  • Exam move: say which unit you are comparing (per kg, per calorie, per gram of protein), because the gap changes with the unit.
Quick check
?In a region where wells are running dry, which part of a crop's water footprint matters most?
Mission 4

Relative sustainability in different places

  • The same product can have very different footprints. Animal products from grazing systems have smaller blue and grey water footprints than those from industrial systems.
  • A study of 38,700 farms found environmental impact "can vary 50-fold among producers of the same product".
  • But "impacts of the lowest-impact animal products typically exceed those of vegetable substitutes".
Mission 4

Place example: groundwater in north-west India

  • NASA's GRACE satellites measured groundwater falling by 4.0 cm a year (17.7 km³ a year) beneath Rajasthan, Punjab and Haryana (including Delhi).
  • From August 2002 to October 2008 the loss was 109 km³: double the capacity of India's largest surface reservoir.
  • Rainfall was close to normal, so the authors judge pumping for irrigation and other uses the likely cause; 114 million people live in the region.
  • High yields here rest on a blue-water store that is being used faster than it refills.
Mission 4

Exam move: strengths and limits of the systems approach (AO3)

  • Strengths: a common framework for comparing farms, foods and places; it shows leaks of energy, water and nutrients.
  • Limits: the energy ratios come from US systems in 2003; global averages hide local scarcity; calories and litres leave out nutrition, land suitability and jobs; the "up to 70%" ignores grazing land unsuited to crops and the nutritional value of animal foods.
  • Exam move: "Evaluate" means weigh strengths against limitations, then judge.
Mission check

?Of every 100 calories the world's crops produce, how many reach people as meat, milk or eggs?

About 4 calories. 36 of every 100 crop calories go to animal feed, and only 12% of those reach human diets. A systems view shows this energy loss, and water footprints show why place matters: north-west India's groundwater fell by 109 km³ in six years.

Mission 5: By 1980, new high-yielding seeds grew on about 40% of Asia's cereal land; by 2000, about 80%. Why did they spread fast in some places and slowly in others?

Make a guess: By 1980, new high-yielding seeds grew on about 40% of Asia's cereal land; by 2000, about 80%. Why did they spread fast in some places and slowly in others?

Mission 5

Diffusion

  • Diffusion: the spread of an idea, innovation or disease from its source (hearth) across space over time.
  • Expansion diffusion: the phenomenon spreads while staying in the hearth.
    • Contagious: to nearby people and places first.
    • Hierarchical: through a hierarchy, from large cities or key people downwards.
  • Relocation diffusion: carriers move, taking it to new places.
  • These ideas come from Torsten Hägerstrand's work on innovation diffusion.
Mission 5

Adoption over time, and barriers

  • Adoption usually follows an S-curve: a few early adopters, then rapid take-up, then a slow finish.
  • Adoption/acquisition: Everett Rogers (1962) grouped adopters as innovators, early adopters, early majority, late majority and laggards.
  • Distance decay: places closer to the hearth, or better connected to it, adopt sooner.
  • Barriers may be physical (mountains, climate), economic (cost, credit) or political (laws, borders). Absorbing barriers stop diffusion; permeable barriers slow it.
Quick check
?A new disease reaches a distant capital city when an infected traveller arrives by plane, and then spreads to that city's neighbourhoods. Which sequence fits?
Mission 5

An agricultural innovation: Asia's Green Revolution

  • High-yielding modern varieties (MVs) of wheat and rice spread across Asia from the late 1960s.
  • About 40% of Asia's cereal area grew MVs by 1980, and about 80% by 2000.
  • Asia's cereal production rose from 313 to 650 million tonnes a year between 1970 and 1995.
Mission 5

Why it spread fast where it did

  • The new varieties "worked best for wheat and rice in the best-irrigated areas".
  • Governments built input and credit systems and subsidised "fertilizer, power, and water".
  • By 1972, Asian countries spent an average of 15.4% of government spending on agriculture.
  • Heavily irrigated north-west India shows the long-term cost of this model: falling groundwater (Mission 4).
Mission 5

Barriers to the Green Revolution

  • Physical: rain-fed and drought-prone areas gained less; varieties suited to poorer regions came only "within 10 to 15 years".
  • Economic: seed, fertiliser and water cost money, so credit decided who could adopt.
  • Political: governments decided where input and credit systems were built. Land tenure shaped who gained: "where the land system was more feudal, poverty reduction was more limited".
  • Costs: falling groundwater, salt build-up and heavy chemical use. Green revolutions "are not necessarily pro poor or environmentally benign".
Mission 5

Diseases diffuse, and meet barriers too

  • Air travel carries diseases between big cities (relocation, often hierarchical); local spread is contagious (Mission 12).
  • Physical: cold highlands limit malaria, but in warmer years it reaches higher altitudes in Ethiopia and Colombia.
  • Economic: the ability to pay for vaccines and treatment. Political: quarantine, travel restrictions and border checks.
  • Barriers slow diffusion; few stop it completely.
Mission 5

Representing diffusion (skills)

  • Sequence maps: shade or date areas by when the disease or innovation arrived.
  • Flow maps: arrows show routes and volumes.
  • Cumulative line graphs: the S-curve of adoption or cases.
  • Cluster maps: researchers in Haiti mapped cholera clusters by date along a river (Mission 7).
  • Exam move: annotate a map with dates and arrows, and say what the pattern shows.
Quick check

Mission check

?Why did the new seeds spread fast in some places and slowly in others?

This is expansion diffusion shaped by barriers. The seeds spread fastest on irrigated land with credit and subsidies, and slowest where physical (dry land) and economic (the cost of seed, fertiliser and water) barriers stood in the way, and where governments had not built input and credit systems (political). Land tenure then shaped who gained.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Malaria killed an estimated 610,000 people in 2024. Why does it kill mostly in one continent, and mostly young children?

Make a guess: Malaria killed an estimated 610,000 people in 2024. Why does it kill mostly in one continent, and mostly young children?

Mission 6

A vector-borne disease

  • A vector-borne disease is carried between people by a living organism, the vector.
  • Malaria is caused by Plasmodium parasites (five species infect humans; P. falciparum and P. vivax are the most dangerous).
  • It spreads mainly through the bites of infected female Anopheles mosquitoes. Symptoms usually start 10–15 days after the bite.
Mission 6

Incidence and pattern

  • 2024: an estimated 282 million cases and 610,000 deaths in 80 countries.
  • The WHO African Region had 95% of cases (265 million) and 95% of deaths (579,000).
  • Nigeria had the largest share of deaths, 31.9%, followed by the Democratic Republic of the Congo (11.7%) and Niger (6.1%). WHO's wording calls these shares of the Region's deaths, but the base is unclear: they add to only 49.7%.
Mission 6

Physical factors

  • Temperature: a model validated with African data puts optimal transmission at 25 °C, with transmission falling sharply above 28 °C.
  • Rainfall: pools of still water are breeding sites for mosquitoes.
  • Altitude: cooler highlands limit transmission. In warmer years, malaria reached higher altitudes in the highlands of Ethiopia and Colombia.
Mission 6

Human factors

  • Poverty and housing: open eaves and unscreened windows let mosquitoes in; nets and treatment cost money.
  • Health systems: access to testing and treatment. Conflict and instability disrupt health services.
  • Funding: US$3.9 billion was invested in 2024, less than half of the US$9.3 billion target for 2025.
  • Urban spread: An. stephensi "thrives in urban settings, endures high temperatures and is resistant to many of the insecticides used in public health".
Mission 6

Diffusion and resistance

  • Relocation diffusion of a vector: An. stephensi is native to parts of South Asia and the Arabian Peninsula and has now invaded 9 African countries.
  • Drug resistance: partial resistance to artemisinin, the key drug, is confirmed in Eritrea, Rwanda, Uganda and the United Republic of Tanzania.
  • Resistance is confirmed or suspected in at least 8 African countries: resistance diffuses too.
Quick check
?Which of these is relocation diffusion in the malaria system?
Mission 6

Demographic impacts

  • Children under 5 accounted for about three-quarters of malaria deaths in the WHO African Region.
  • Infants, children under 5, pregnant women and girls, travellers and people with HIV or AIDS are at higher risk of severe malaria.
  • WHO notes symptoms may be milder for people who have had malaria before. Young children have had few infections, so they have little protection.
Mission 6

Socio-economic impacts (AO3)

  • Gallup and Sachs (2001): countries with intensive malaria had 1995 incomes only 33% (about a third) of those without malaria.
  • Allowing for other factors, they grew 1.3% less per person per year (1965–1990).
  • Evaluate: this is a correlation across countries. The authors argue that high malaria in poor countries is "not mainly a consequence of poverty"; but the link can also run the other way, since poverty limits nets, housing and treatment.
  • Lost school and work days, and spending on treatment, fall hardest on poor households.
Mission 6

Responses

  • Prevention: insecticide-treated nets and indoor residual spraying; vaccines RTS,S (recommended 2021) and R21 (2023), now used routinely in 24 countries.
  • Treatment: artemisinin-based combination therapies.
  • Nigeria: the R21 vaccine was introduced in Kebbi (north) and Bayelsa (south) in December 2024, with 1 million doses: 846,200 from Gavi and 153,800 bought by the government.
  • Tools prevented an estimated 170 million cases and 1 million deaths in 2024.
Mission check

?Why does malaria kill mostly in one continent, and mostly young children?

95% of deaths were in the WHO African Region, where temperatures near 25 °C and rainfall suit transmission, and poverty, conflict and funding gaps limit nets and treatment. About 3 in 4 deaths there are children under 5, who have had little earlier exposure.

Mission 7

Mission 7: In October 2010, cholera was reported in Haiti. By February 2019 there had been 820,000 cases. How did it arrive, and why did it spread so fast?

Make a guess: In October 2010, cholera was reported in Haiti. By February 2019 there had been 820,000 cases. How did it arrive, and why did it spread so fast?

Mission 7

A water-borne disease

  • A water-borne disease spreads through contaminated water, often from human faeces.
  • Cholera is "an acute diarrhoeal infection caused by consuming food or water contaminated with the bacterium Vibrio cholerae".
  • Symptoms appear 12 hours to 5 days after infection. Most infected people have no symptoms but pass the bacteria in their faeces for 1–10 days, so it spreads unseen.
Mission 7

Global incidence

  • WHO estimates 1.3 to 4.0 million cases and 21,000 to 143,000 deaths a year.
  • In 2025, 47 countries reported 451,499 cases and 7,870 deaths to WHO, which WHO says are likely below the true burden.
  • Cholera is linked to limited safe water, sanitation and hygiene, and to conflict, displacement, floods and drought.
Mission 7

Haiti, October 2010

  • "The first cholera outbreak was reported in Haiti in October 2010".
  • Researchers mapped the first cases. The earliest cluster was near Mirebalais (16–19 October) "next to a United Nations camp with deficient sanitation"; the next ran along the Artibonite River (20–28 October).
  • They concluded their findings "strongly suggest that contamination of the Artibonite and 1 of its tributaries downstream from a military camp triggered the epidemic".
Mission 7

How it spread

  • Relocation: this was Haiti's first reported outbreak, so the bacteria were brought in; the first cluster was beside the camp.
  • Expansion along the river: in the researchers' model, communes downstream of the camp and communes on the coastal plain each had roughly 4.6–4.9 times the risk of other communes.
  • Centrifugal spread: three further clusters in November, as cases moved outward.
  • Skill: this is a cluster map by date. Like John Snow's 1854 map of cholera deaths around the Broad Street pump in London, it links cases to a contaminated water source.
Mission 7

Why Haiti was so vulnerable

  • Basic sanitation, 2010: Haiti 25% of people; rural Haiti 17%.

What counts as famine

  • The IPC (Integrated Food Security Phase Classification) defines Famine (Phase 5) by three thresholds in one area:

  • In Bangladesh in 1974, floods raised food prices and destroyed a crop; farm workers' real incomes "declined so much that this group was disproportionately stricken by starvation".
  • The key term is entitlement: whether people can command food through work, wages, trade or aid.

Mission 11

Prevention or treatment?

  • Prevention stops infection: vaccines, nets, clean water and sanitation, condoms, pre-exposure medicines.
  • Treatment cures or controls disease in people already ill: oral rehydration, antimalarials, antiretroviral therapy (ART).
  • Prevention protects whole communities before harm is done; treatment saves the people already sick. Most strategies need both.
  • The best mix depends on the means of infection, who is at risk, and what a government chooses to fund.
Mission 11

HIV: scale and trends

  • HIV spreads through sexual contact, blood and from mother to child.
  • 2025: 41.0 million people living with HIV; 1.2 million new infections; 570,000 AIDS-related deaths.
  • 32.1 million people (78%) were on antiretroviral therapy.
  • New infections are down 65% since the 1994 peak; AIDS-related deaths are down 73% since 2004.
Mission 11

Treatment as prevention (scientific intervention)

  • The HPTN 052 trial (2011) enrolled 1,763 couples in nine countries in which one partner had HIV.
  • Of 28 transmissions linked to the infected partner, only 1 was in the group that started ART early.
  • So early treatment lowers the amount of virus and sharply cuts onward spread: treatment is also prevention.
  • In July 2025 WHO recommended lenacapavir, "the first twice-yearly injectable PrEP product", to prevent infection.

Mission 11

Social marginalisation

  • Compared with all adults aged 15–49, UNAIDS reports HIV risk 18 times as high among gay men and other men who have sex with men, 17 times among sex workers and transgender women, and 34 times among people who inject drugs.
  • Where these groups face stigma or criminal penalties, they are less likely to seek testing, prevention or treatment.
  • Reaching marginalised groups is a geographical question: where services are, and who feels safe using them.

Government priorities

  • At the peak of South Africa's epidemic, the government, "going against consensus scientific opinion", argued that HIV did not cause AIDS, and declined donated nevirapine and Global Fund grants.

Mission 12

From outbreak to pandemic

  • Epidemic: more cases than normally expected in an area or community. Pandemic: an epidemic that spreads across several countries or continents.
  • 31 December 2019: a cluster of pneumonia cases was reported in Wuhan, China.
  • 13 January 2020: the first recorded case outside China, in Thailand.
  • 30 January 2020: WHO declared a Public Health Emergency of International Concern (PHEIC), its 6th since the International Health Regulations of 2005. 11 March 2020: WHO said COVID-19 "can be characterized as a pandemic".
Mission 12

Epidemiology: how it spread

  • The virus "spreads through the air via infectious respiratory particles"; transmission "is more likely in close contact or shared indoor spaces".
  • Older adults, people with underlying conditions and unvaccinated people are at higher risk of severe illness.
  • Air travel moved it between cities (relocation and hierarchical diffusion); crowded indoor spaces spread it locally (contagious diffusion).
  • Key terms: incubation period (infection to symptoms), reproduction number, R (the average number of people each case infects) and pre-symptomatic spread (passing it on before feeling ill).
Mission 12

  • In September 2019 the Global Preparedness Monitoring Board warned of "a very real threat of a rapidly moving, highly lethal pandemic of a respiratory pathogen".
  • It said such a pandemic could kill "50 to 80 million people" and wipe out "nearly 5% of the world's economy".
  • Its verdict: "The world is not prepared."
