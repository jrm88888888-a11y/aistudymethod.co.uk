Mission 1

Mission 1: Why doesn't selection always push a population in the same direction?

Make a guess: Why doesn't selection always push a population in the same direction?

Mission 1

Three shapes of selection

  • Directional: one extreme is favoured. The mean shifts.
  • Stabilising: the intermediate (modal) phenotype is favoured; both extremes are selected against. The mean stays; the range narrows.
  • All of them change allele frequencies. Stabilising selection removes alleles for extreme phenotypes.
  • Say: "stabilising selection still changes allele frequencies" — not: "no change in the mean, so no evolution".
Mission 1

Named examples

  • Directional: antibiotic resistance in bacteria.
  • Stabilising: human birth mass. In a large study of births at a London hospital, mortality was lowest at intermediate birth masses and higher for very light and very heavy babies (Karn and Penrose, 1951).
Quick check
?In a study of human births in the mid-twentieth century, babies of intermediate birth mass had the highest survival, while both very light and very heavy babies had higher mortality. Which type of selection does this show, and why?
Quick check
?Which statement about directional and stabilising selection is correct?
Mission check

?In a hypothetical songbird, clutch size (number of eggs) is heritable. Parents with very large clutches cannot feed all their chicks, and many starve; parents with very small clutches raise few young. Over many generations, the mean clutch size stays at 4 eggs, but nests with 2 or 6 eggs become rarer. Which statement is correct?

The mean stayed put, yet the population still evolved. Selection can hold the middle as well as push an extreme.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why do small, isolated populations end up so genetically different from everyone else?

Make a guess: Why do small, isolated populations end up so genetically different from everyone else?

Mission 2

Chance in the gene pool

  • Gene pool: all the alleles of all the genes in a population.
  • Each generation's alleles are a sample of the last generation's.
  • Which individuals happen to breed, and which alleles go into the gametes, is partly chance.
  • Genetic drift: a random change in allele frequencies from one generation to the next, unrelated to whether the alleles help survival.
  • Drift can make an allele fixed (frequency 1) or lost (frequency 0), even a harmful or useful one.
  • When a river, mountains or distance split a population, no alleles move between the parts, so drift changes each part separately.
  • Say: "drift: chance change in allele frequency" — not: "drift: alleles drifting between populations".

Quick check
?Two populations of a hypothetical plant species, one of 20 plants and one of 20,000 plants, have the same allele frequencies for a gene whose alleles have no effect on survival or reproduction. Which statement best explains why the allele frequencies in the small population are more likely to change from one generation to the next?
Mission 2

Bottlenecks and founders

  • Genetic bottleneck: a population is cut to a few individuals for at least one generation.
    • The survivors carry a chance sample of alleles. Many alleles are lost.
    • Even after numbers recover, genetic diversity stays low.
    • Northern elephant seals: hunting left perhaps 20–100 by the 1890s. Now there are well over 100,000, with very little genetic variation.
  • Founder effect: a few individuals start a new, isolated population.
    • Allele frequencies reflect the founders, not the source population.
    • Ellis–van Creveld syndrome (a rare inherited condition) is far more common among the Old Order Amish of Lancaster County, Pennsylvania, than elsewhere; it has been traced to one founding couple.
  • Say: "survivors of a bottleneck are a chance sample" — not: "the survivors were the fittest".
Quick check
?Which statement correctly describes the role of random occurrences in the evolution of a population?
Mission check

?A storm carries six birds of a mainland species to a distant island whose habitat is very like the mainland's. A hundred generations later, the island population is still small, and it differs greatly from the mainland in the frequencies of several alleles that have no known effect on survival. Which explanation is best supported?

Few individuals, little gene flow: chance alone can make a population's gene pool unlike any other.

Mission 3

Mission 3: A harmful allele can hide in healthy carriers. How can you work out how many people carry it?

Make a guess: A harmful allele can hide in healthy carriers. How can you work out how many people carry it?

Mission 3

Populations and gene pools

  • Population: organisms of one species, in the same place at the same time, that can interbreed.
  • Gene pool: all the genes, and all their alleles, in a population.
  • Allele frequency: the fraction of all copies of a gene that are one allele.
  • Two alleles: frequency of A = p, frequency of a = q.
  • Each diploid has 2 copies of each gene. 500 individuals → 1000 copies.
  • Say: "allele frequency counts copies" — not: "the fraction of individuals that carry it".
Mission 3Worked example

Count the copies

A population of 400 snails: 196 AA, 168 Aa, 36 aa.

  • Step 1: total copies = 2 × 400 = 800.
  • Step 2: copies of A = (2 × 196) + 168 = 392 + 168 = 560.
  • Step 3: p = 560 ÷ 800 = 0.70.
  • Step 4: q = 1 − 0.70 = 0.30. Check: (2 × 36 + 168) ÷ 800 = 240 ÷ 800 = 0.30.
  • AA individuals give 2 copies; Aa give 1. Never count individuals.

Mission 3

The Hardy–Weinberg equations

  • p + q = 1 (all copies are A or a).
  • p² + 2pq + q² = 1 (all individuals are AA, Aa or aa).
  • p² = AA. 2pq = Aa. q² = aa.
  • Why 2pq? Aa forms two ways: A egg + a sperm, or a egg + A sperm.
  • Dominant phenotype = p² + 2pq = 1 − q². Recessive phenotype = q².
  • Prediction: allele frequencies stay the same from generation to generation.
  • Both equations are given in the exam when needed.
Mission 3Worked example

Find the hidden carriers

Real figure: in the UK, about 1 in 2,500 babies has cystic fibrosis, a recessive condition.

  • Step 1: affected = aa = q². So q² = 1 ÷ 2,500 = 0.0004.
  • Step 2: q = √0.0004 = 0.02.
  • Step 3: p = 1 − 0.02 = 0.98.
  • Step 4: carriers = Aa = 2pq = 2 × 0.98 × 0.02 = 0.039.
  • So about 1 in 25 people is a carrier: about 98 carriers for every affected person.
  • Start from q², the only phenotype that shows one genotype.
Quick check
?In a large population, 16% of individuals show the phenotype of the homozygous recessive genotype for a gene with two alleles. Assuming Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium, what is the frequency of the homozygous dominant genotype?
Mission 3

When the prediction holds

The allele frequencies stay constant only if:

  • the population is large (no chance changes: genetic drift),
  • mating is random,
  • there are no new mutations,
  • there is no migration in or out,
  • there is no natural selection: every genotype survives and reproduces equally.
  • Say: "equilibrium: frequencies stay the same" — not: "equilibrium: p = q = 0.5".
Mission 3

Read the data: MN blood groups

Real data: MN blood group genotypes in 6,129 people in the USA (a classic dataset). M and N are codominant, so each blood type shows its genotype.

MMMNNN
Observed178730391303

  • Expected MM = 0.5395² × 6129 ≈ 1784. Expected MN = 2 × 0.5395 × 0.4605 × 6129 ≈ 3045.
  • Keep four decimal places in p and q, or expected counts drift.
  • Observed and expected are very close.
  • A χ² test on these numbers gives a value far below any critical value.
Quick check

Mission 3

When frequencies change

  • Evolution: a change in allele frequencies in a population over generations.
  • Selection, drift, migration and mutation change allele frequencies.
  • Say: "populations evolve" — not: "individuals evolve".
Mission 3

Recessive alleles hide

  • A harmful recessive allele is selected against only in aa individuals.
  • Most copies sit in healthy Aa carriers (cystic fibrosis: about 98 carriers per affected person).
  • So selection lowers q slowly, and ever more slowly as q falls.
  • Being dominant does not make an allele spread.
Quick check
?A large population of a hypothetical mammal species is in Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium at a gene with alleles R and r, with r at a frequency of 0.2; R is completely dominant to r. A new disease then appears, and all rr individuals die before reproducing, while RR and Rr individuals survive and reproduce equally well. Which prediction about the frequency of r over the next several generations is best supported?
Mission check

?In a large, randomly mating population of a hypothetical moth species, pale wings are caused by a recessive allele. 9% of the moths have pale wings. Assuming Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium, what proportion of the moths are heterozygous?

Count the ones you can see (q²), take the square root, and the hidden carriers appear: 2pq.

Mission 4

Mission 4: If two groups of the same animal never meet again, how do they end up as different species?

Make a guess: If two groups of the same animal never meet again, how do they end up as different species?

Mission 4

What counts as a species?

  • Biological species concept: a group of organisms that can interbreed and produce fertile offspring.
  • Horse × donkey → mule. Offspring, yes; fertile, no. So horse and donkey are two species.
  • Say: "can produce fertile offspring" — not: "can produce offspring".
Quick check
?According to the biological species concept, what is a species?
Mission 4

Where the definition struggles

  • Often it cannot be tested:
    • fossils cannot be crossed;
    • populations that never meet cannot show it in the wild.
  • It does not fit organisms that reproduce asexually.
  • Some pairs of species still produce some fertile hybrids (common in plants).
  • So competing species concepts exist: shared traits, DNA, ancestry. None fits every case.
Mission 4

Speciation means splitting

  • Speciation: one species splits into two or more.
  • The key step: populations become reproductively isolated. Gene flow between them stops.
  • Their gene pools then accumulate differences, until members can no longer interbreed to produce fertile offspring.
  • Usually gradual. Part-way through, "one species or two?" can be an arbitrary call.
  • A lineage that changes over time without splitting has not speciated.
Mission 4

Allopatric speciation, step by step

  • (1) One population. Gene flow keeps its gene pool mixed.
  • (2) A geographical barrier (river, sea, mountains) splits it. Gene flow stops. This is allopatric speciation.
  • (3) Each gene pool now changes on its own: new mutations, different selection pressures, and genetic drift in small populations. Allele frequencies diverge.
  • (4) In time, the two can no longer interbreed to produce fertile offspring: two species.
  • OCR calls the barrier a geographical isolating mechanism.
  • Say: "isolation stopped gene flow; selection changed allele frequencies" — not: "the river caused mutations" or "they changed in order to adapt".

Mission 4

What keeps species apart

  • Isolating mechanisms stop two gene pools mixing.
  • Before fertilisation (pre-zygotic):
    • geographical: they live in different places;
    • seasonal (temporal): they breed at different times;
    • behavioural: courtship signals differ;
    • morphological (mechanical): reproductive parts or flowers do not fit;
    • gametic: sperm cannot fertilise the other species' eggs.
  • After fertilisation (post-zygotic):
    • the hybrid dies before it can breed;
    • hybrid sterility: the hybrid lives but cannot breed. Mule: 32 horse + 31 donkey chromosomes = 63; the unmatched sets cannot pair properly in meiosis.
Quick check
?Two closely related bird species live in the same forest. The males of each species sing a different song, and females respond only to the song of their own species. What is the significance of this for the two species?
Mission check

?A population of lizards was split when rising sea level cut off an island from the mainland. Many generations later, island and mainland lizards can no longer produce fertile offspring together. Which statement about this speciation is correct?

The barrier only stops gene flow. Mutation, selection and drift do the rest, over many generations.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Can a new species appear right beside its parents, or even in a single generation?

Make a guess: Can a new species appear right beside its parents, or even in a single generation?

Mission 5

Speciation without a barrier

  • Sympatric speciation: populations in the same area become reproductively isolated.
  • No geographical barrier. Gene flow is cut by:
    • timing: they breed at different times (temporal isolation);
    • behaviour: different courtship or mate choice;
    • ecology: they use different foods, hosts or soils;
    • chromosomes: a sudden change in chromosome number.
  • Example: two Howea palm species on tiny Lord Howe Island. They grow on different soils and flower at different times.
  • OCR calls these reproductive isolating mechanisms.
Mission 5

Allopatric and sympatric side by side

  • Both need reproductive isolation: gene flow must stop.
  • Both then need the gene pools to diverge until fertile interbreeding fails.
  • Allopatric: a geographical barrier; populations live apart.
  • Sympatric: same area; isolation by timing, behaviour, ecology or chromosomes.
  • Say: "sympatric means without a geographical barrier" — not: "a smaller barrier".
Quick check
?What do allopatric speciation and sympatric speciation have in common?
Mission 5

A new species in one generation

  • (1) Species P (2n = 14) × species Q (2n = 18). Hybrid: 7 + 9 = 16 chromosomes, none with a partner. Sterile.
  • (2) The chromosome number doubles (e.g. a failed cell division): 32. Every chromosome now has a partner. Meiosis works: fertile.
  • (3) Polyploid × P: offspring get 16 + 7 = 23, unpaired. Sterile. The polyploid is isolated from both parents: a new species, formed sympatrically, at once.
  • Polyploid: more than two complete sets of chromosomes. Common in plants (bread wheat has six sets).
  • Illustrative chromosome numbers.
Quick check
?Suppose one Persicaria species has 20 chromosomes in each diploid cell and another has 24. A hybrid forms between them, and in one hybrid plant the chromosome number doubles. Which statement is correct?
Mission check

?Two populations of a fly species live in the same orchard but lay their eggs on different fruit species, which ripen at different times, so the flies rarely mate with each other. Which type of speciation could this lead to, and why?

No mountain or sea is needed. Anything that stops gene flow, even a calendar or a doubled set of chromosomes, can start a new species.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Directional selection favours one extreme and shifts the mean; stabilising selection favours the intermediate and narrows the range; both change allele frequencies.

Mission 2

  • Genetic drift is a random change in allele frequencies; it matters most in small populations, where chance sampling has large effects.
  • Bottlenecks and founder events leave a small, chance sample of alleles.

Mission 3

  • p + q = 1 and p² + 2pq + q² = 1: from the recessive phenotype (q²) you can find every allele and genotype frequency.
  • Allele frequencies stay constant only in a large, randomly mating population with no mutation, migration or selection; a change in allele frequencies is evolution.

Mission 4

  • A species can interbreed to produce fertile offspring; speciation is one species splitting into two once gene flow between populations stops and their gene pools diverge.
  • Isolating mechanisms (geographical, seasonal, behavioural, morphological, hybrid sterility) keep species' gene pools apart.

Mission 5

  • Sympatric speciation happens in one area when timing, behaviour, ecology or chromosomes stop gene flow; allopatric needs a geographical barrier; both need reproductive isolation, then divergence.
  • Hybridisation plus doubling of the chromosome number can make a fertile, isolated plant species in one generation.
