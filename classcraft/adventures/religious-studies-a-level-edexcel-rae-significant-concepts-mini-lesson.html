Mission 1

Three environmental issues

  • Sustainability: meeting present needs without compromising the ability of future generations to meet theirs (Brundtland Report, Our Common Future, 1987).
  • Waste management: reduce, reuse, recycle; landfill, plastics in the oceans, electronic waste often shipped to poorer countries.
  • Climate change: long-term warming driven mainly by greenhouse gases from burning fossil fuels and from deforestation and farming. Effects fall hardest on the poorest and on future generations.
  • Conservation: protecting species, habitats and resources from loss.
  • Ethical questions: who counts (future people, animals, ecosystems)? Who should pay? Is nature valuable in itself or only for us?
Mission 1Read the source

Dominion and stewardship

> "Be fruitful, multiply, fill the earth, and subdue it. Have dominion over the fish of the sea, over the birds of the sky, and over every living thing that moves on the earth." (Genesis 1:28, WEBBE)

> "The LORD God took the man, and put him into the garden of Eden to cultivate and keep it." (Genesis 2:15, WEBBE)

> "The earth is the LORD's, with its fullness; the world, and those who dwell in it." (Psalm 24:1, WEBBE)

Q1 According to Psalm 24:1, who owns the earth?

Show answer

God; humans hold it on trust.

Q2 How could Genesis 2:15 limit the meaning of "dominion" in Genesis 1:28?

Show answer

To "keep" the garden is to care for it: rule as a steward, not as an owner.

Mission 1

Christian stewardship and conservation

  • Stewardship: humans care for God's creation on God's behalf and are accountable to God for it.
  • Catholic: Pope Francis, Laudato Si' (2015), "on care for our common home": integral ecology (care for the poor and care for the earth are one task); he rejects a "tyrannical anthropocentrism" that ignores other creatures (§68).
  • Orthodox: Ecumenical Patriarch Bartholomew (b. 1940) has called harm to the natural world a sin.
  • Evangelical: many speak of creation care; some others give the environment lower priority, expecting God to renew creation at the end.
  • Hope: creation itself "will be delivered from the bondage of decay" (Romans 8:21, WEBBE).
  • Conservation as worship: preserving species and places honours the Creator.
Mission 1

Is Christianity to blame?

  • Lynn White Jr. (1907–1987), "The Historical Roots of Our Ecologic Crisis", Science (1967): Western Christianity is "the most anthropocentric religion the world has seen" (short phrase).
  • His argument (paraphrase): dominion taught that nature exists for human use, which fed exploitation by science and technology.
  • He proposed Francis of Assisi as a patron saint of ecologists: a Christian alternative.
  • Replies: dominion means stewardship (Genesis 2:15; Psalm 24:1); exploitation also flourished in non-Christian societies; Christian groups now lead much environmental action.
Mission 1

A secular perspective: utilitarianism

  • Utilitarian conservation: protect the environment because damage causes suffering and lost well-being.
  • Peter Singer (b. 1946), Practical Ethics (1979): count the interests of all sentient beings, including animals and future generations.
  • So: cut emissions now if future suffering avoided outweighs present costs; protect wilderness for the pleasure and health it gives.
  • Critics: future effects are uncertain; an ecosystem or species with no sentient interests counts only as a means.
  • Deep ecologists would still class this as shallow: nature has value only because of what it does for those who can feel.

Mission 1

Lovelock: the Gaia hypothesis

  • James Lovelock (1919–2022), scientist, Gaia: A New Look at Life on Earth (1979), developed with Lynn Margulis.
  • Gaia: the earth's living things and their non-living surroundings act as one self-regulating system, keeping conditions (temperature, the make-up of the air) fit for life.
  • Humans are not rulers or stewards but one part of the system.
  • If humans upset the balance, Gaia may move to a new state that suits life but not us. In The Revenge of Gaia (2006) he warned that climate change could make much of the earth uninhabitable for humans.
  • Lovelock used "Gaia" (the Greek earth goddess) as a metaphor, not as a god.
  • Critics: Richard Dawkins and others said natural selection cannot produce a planet-wide system with a purpose.
Mission 1

Næss: deep ecology

  • Arne Næss (1912–2009), Norwegian philosopher, "The Shallow and the Deep, Long-Range Ecology Movement" (1973).
  • Shallow ecology: fights pollution and resource depletion for the health and affluence of people in developed countries.
  • Deep ecology: all living things have intrinsic value, regardless of their usefulness to humans; "biospherical egalitarianism", in principle.
  • Platform (Næss and George Sessions, 1984; paraphrase): human and non-human flourishing has value in itself; humans have no right to reduce the richness of life except to meet vital needs; policies must change deeply.
  • Self-realisation: the mature self identifies with the wider whole of nature.
  • Critics: ignores human poverty and justice (Murray Bookchin); the platform's call for a smaller human population is controversial; hard to apply.

Mission 1Diversity

Other religious perspectives

  • Islam: humans are khalifah (vicegerents): "I am about to place a viceroy in the earth" (Qur'an 2:30, Pickthall). God "hath set the measure, that ye exceed not the measure" (55:7–8): keep the balance (mizan).
  • Judaism: bal tashchit ("do not destroy"), from the command not to cut down fruit trees in a siege (Deuteronomy 20:19): needless waste is forbidden.
  • Buddhism: interdependence (dependent origination) and compassion for all beings; many Buddhists link ecology to reducing craving.
  • Hinduism: the divine present in nature; the Chipko movement (1970s India), drawing on Gandhian non-violence, protected trees by hugging them; many Hindus see it as living out reverence for nature.
  • Shared theme: human responsibility, not ownership. Difference: how far nature has value in itself.
Mission 1

Law, examples and social attitudes

  • International: Kyoto Protocol (1997); Paris Agreement (2015): keep warming well below 2°C and aim for 1.5°C.
  • UK: Climate Change Act 2008, amended in 2019 to a net zero target by 2050.
  • Waste: landfill tax (1996); carrier-bag charges (England, 2015); ban on plastic microbeads (2018).
  • Attitudes: youth climate strikes (from 2018); direct-action groups divide opinion; costs of change (energy bills, jobs) are hotly debated.
  • Religious action: faith groups divesting from fossil fuels; Laudato Si' widely read beyond the Catholic Church.
Mission 1Evaluate

Are religious perspectives valuable in environmental debate?

  • Claim: Religious perspectives are valuable in debates about the environment.
  • For: Stewardship gives a strong motive (accountability to God) and reaches billions of believers.
  • For: Religions link ecology to justice for the poor (Laudato Si') and resist consumerism.
  • Against: White: dominion helped cause the crisis. Stewardship is still human-centred (Næss would call it shallow).
  • Against: Secular reasons (Singer; Lovelock's science) are shared by all; religious arguments persuade only believers. Some believers' end-times hopes reduce urgency.
  • Judgement criteria: Does the view motivate action? Is it human-centred or nature-centred? Can it be shared in a plural society? Does it fit the science?
Mission 1Worked answer

Using a named scholar

Task: "Stewardship is an adequate response to the environmental crisis." Evaluate.

  • Line of argument: stewardship is a strong but incomplete response.
  • Support: it combines care with accountability (Psalm 24:1; Genesis 2:15) and motivates action (Laudato Si').
  • Counter: Næss: stewardship is still shallow, valuing nature for God's purposes and human use; deep change is needed. Lovelock: humans are one part of Gaia, not its managers.
  • Critique of the counter: Laudato Si' itself rejects "tyrannical anthropocentrism" and says creatures have value in God's sight; deep ecology is hard to apply to human need.
  • Judgement: decided by your criteria (e.g. is a human-centred ethic enough?), stated and defended.

Mission 2

What does equality mean?

  • Equal worth (dignity): every human being matters equally.
  • Equality before the law: the same rights and the same rules for all.
  • Equality of opportunity: the same chances (education, jobs); equality of outcome: similar results (income, health).
  • Discrimination: treating people unfairly because of a characteristic such as race, sex or disability. Prejudice: the attitude behind it.
  • Treating everyone identically can be unfair: a ramp, not stairs for all, gives a wheelchair user equal access.
  • Key debate: does equality require only fair rules, or action to change unequal results (e.g. positive action)?
Mission 2Read the source

Equal in the image of God

> "God created man in his own image. In God's image he created him; male and female he created them." (Genesis 1:27, WEBBE)

> "There is neither Jew nor Greek, there is neither slave nor free man, there is neither male nor female; for you are all one in Christ Jesus." (Galatians 3:28, WEBBE)

> "Truly I perceive that God doesn't show favouritism" (Acts 10:34, WEBBE)

  • Imago Dei (image of God): the basis of equal worth in Christian ethics.

Q1 Which verse grounds equality in creation and which in salvation?

Show answer

Genesis 1:27: all are made in God's image; Galatians 3:28: all are one in Christ.

Q2 Why might some Christians read Galatians 3:28 as spiritual rather than social equality?

Show answer

Paul's letters elsewhere address slaves and masters without abolishing slavery; some read "one in Christ" as about salvation, not social order.

Mission 2

A secular perspective

  • Humanism: equal dignity rests on our shared humanity, reason and capacity to suffer, not on God.
  • The Universal Declaration of Human Rights (1948), Article 1: "All human beings are born free and equal in dignity and rights."
  • John Rawls (1921–2002), A Theory of Justice (1971): choose principles of justice behind a veil of ignorance (not knowing your race, sex, ability or wealth).
  • You would choose equal basic liberties and allow inequalities only if they benefit the least advantaged (the difference principle).
  • Critics: Robert Nozick (1938–2002), Anarchy, State, and Utopia (1974): forced redistribution violates liberty.

Mission 2

Martin Luther King: the campaign

  • Martin Luther King Jr. (1929–1968), Baptist minister, Atlanta and Montgomery, USA.
  • 1955–56 Montgomery bus boycott, after Rosa Parks' arrest: buses desegregated by court order.
  • 1957 co-founds the Southern Christian Leadership Conference (SCLC).
  • 1963 Birmingham campaign; Letter from Birmingham Jail; March on Washington, "I Have a Dream" speech.
  • 1964 Civil Rights Act (ends legal segregation in public places and discrimination in employment); Nobel Peace Prize.
  • 1965 Selma to Montgomery marches; Voting Rights Act.
  • 1968 assassinated in Memphis while supporting striking sanitation workers; his last campaign was for the poor of every race.
Mission 2

King's ideas

  • Agape: love for every person, including enemies ("love your enemies", Matthew 5:44). The aim is reconciliation, the beloved community, not defeat of opponents.
  • Nonviolent direct action: from Jesus' teaching and Gandhi's methods. Peaceful protest creates tension that forces a community to negotiate.
  • Just and unjust laws (Letter, 1963, paraphrase): following Augustine and Aquinas, a law that degrades human personality is unjust and may be broken openly, lovingly, accepting the penalty.
  • Interconnection: "Injustice anywhere is a threat to justice everywhere" (Letter, 1963, short phrase).
  • He quoted the prophet Amos: "let justice roll on like rivers, and righteousness like a mighty stream." (Amos 5:24, WEBBE)
  • Critics: Malcolm X (1925–1965) for some years rejected nonviolence as leaving Black people defenceless; white clergy in 1963 called the protests "unwise and untimely".
Mission 2

Joni Eareckson Tada: life and work

  • Joni Eareckson Tada (b. 1949), evangelical Christian writer and disability advocate, USA.
  • 1967, aged 17: a diving accident left her quadriplegic (paralysed from the shoulders down).
  • After despair and questioning, her faith became central; she learned to paint holding a brush in her teeth.
  • 1976 autobiography Joni; 1979 founds Joni and Friends, a Christian ministry for disabled people and their families.
  • Wheels for the World: restores and gives wheelchairs and Bibles in many countries.
  • From 1987: served on the US National Council on the Handicapped (now the National Council on Disability), which helped shape the Americans with Disabilities Act (1990).
Mission 2

Tada's ideas, and the debate

  • Equal value: disabled people bear God's image fully; churches must include them, as in Jesus' parable: "bring in the poor, maimed, blind, and lame" (Luke 14:21, WEBBE).
  • Suffering and God's purpose: God is sovereign and can bring good through suffering (Romans 8:28; 2 Corinthians 12:9); hope of a resurrection body.
  • Sanctity of life: opposes euthanasia and assisted suicide, which she argues send the message that disabled lives are worth less.
  • Debate: the social model of disability (from 1970s UK disability activists; Mike Oliver, 1983) says people are disabled mainly by barriers in society, not by their bodies.
  • Nancy Eiesland (1964–2009), The Disabled God (1994): rejects reading disability as sin or as a special trial from God; pictures the risen Christ with wounds as a disabled God.
  • Supporters: Tada's theology gives meaning and hope; she also campaigns for access and rights, as the social model asks.

Mission 2Diversity

Religion on both sides

  • Race: some Christians defended slavery and segregation, misusing texts such as the "curse" on Canaan (Genesis 9:25); the Dutch Reformed Church supported apartheid for decades.
  • Others led the opposition: abolitionists (e.g. William Wilberforce), the Black churches of the civil rights movement, Archbishop Desmond Tutu (1931–2021) against apartheid.
  • Gender: egalitarian Christians (most Protestant churches with women ministers) vs complementarian Christians and Catholic and Orthodox teaching (equal dignity, different roles; ordained priesthood for men).
  • Disability: healing stories read as signs of the kingdom, or (wrongly, Jesus says in John 9:3) as punishment for sin.
  • Other religions: Islam teaches that humans were made nations and tribes "that ye may know one another", and the noblest is "the best in conduct" (Qur'an 49:13, Pickthall); Sikhism rejects caste through the shared langar meal.
Mission 2

Law and social attitudes

  • USA: Civil Rights Act 1964; Voting Rights Act 1965; Americans with Disabilities Act 1990.
  • UK race: Race Relations Acts 1965 and 1976 (1976 created the Commission for Racial Equality).
  • UK sex: Equal Pay Act 1970; Sex Discrimination Act 1975. UK disability: Disability Discrimination Act 1995.
  • Equality Act 2010: replaced these with one law protecting nine characteristics, including race, sex, disability and religion or belief.
  • Social attitudes shift over time; laws can lead or follow them. Debate continues on positive action and on clashes between equality rights and religious freedom.
Mission 2Evaluate

Are religious perspectives valuable in the equality debate?

  • Claim: Religious perspectives are valuable in debates about equality.
  • For: Imago Dei grounds equal worth beyond any law; King's faith gave the civil rights movement its method and moral force.
  • For: Tada shows religious communities reaching disabled people that the state misses.
  • Against: Religion was used to justify slavery, segregation and apartheid; some traditions still restrict women's roles.
  • Against: Secular grounds (UDHR, Rawls) are shared by all in a plural society; some disability theology (suffering as God's purpose) may weaken the push for change, critics say.
  • Judgement criteria: Has the perspective advanced equality in practice? Is it consistent with its own teaching? Can it be shared beyond believers?

Mission 3

Who counts morally?

  • Every answer about animals rests on one question: do animals have moral status (do they matter for their own sake)?
  • No direct status: René Descartes (1596–1650), Discourse on the Method (1637), Part V: animals are like machines, without minds.
  • Indirect duties only: Thomas Aquinas (c. 1225–1274) and Immanuel Kant (1724–1804, Lectures on Ethics): animals are for human use; cruelty is wrong mainly because it makes people cruel to humans.
  • Direct status through sentience: Jeremy Bentham (1748–1832), An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation (1789), ch. XVII:

> "The question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?"

  • Sentience = the capacity to feel pain and pleasure.
  • UK law now takes sentience as the test: the Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act 2022 covers vertebrates, octopuses, squid, crabs and lobsters.
Mission 3Read the source

Dominion and stewardship

> "Be fruitful, multiply, fill the earth, and subdue it. Have dominion over the fish of the sea, over the birds of the sky, and over every living thing that moves on the earth." (Genesis 1:28, WEBBE)

> "The LORD God took the man, and put him into the garden of Eden to cultivate and keep it." (Genesis 2:15, WEBBE)

> "A righteous man respects the life of his animal, but the tender mercies of the wicked are cruel." (Proverbs 12:10, WEBBE)

  • After the Flood, animals are given as food: "Every moving thing that lives will be food for you." (Genesis 9:3)
  • Dominion: humans rule over animals. Stewardship: for many Christians, that rule is held in trust from God, who owns creation.

Q1 Which verse could a Christian use to defend eating meat?

Show answer

Genesis 9:3; also Genesis 1:28 on dominion.

Q2 Which verse limits how animals may be treated?

Show answer

Proverbs 12:10: the righteous care for their animals; Genesis 2:15: "keep" implies care.

Mission 3

Welfare or rights?

  • Animal welfare: animals may be used, but suffering must be kept to a minimum. Most UK law takes this view.
  • Peter Singer (b. 1946), Animal Liberation (1975), a preference utilitarian: equal interests deserve equal consideration, whatever the species. Favouring humans just for being human is speciesism (term coined by Richard Ryder, 1970). Singer does not rest his case on rights.
  • Tom Regan (1938–2017), The Case for Animal Rights (1983): every subject-of-a-life (a being with beliefs, desires, memory and a welfare of its own) has inherent value and rights. Rights cannot be traded for benefits: he calls for abolition of animal farming, hunting and experiments, not reform.
  • Andrew Linzey (b. 1952), Christianity and the Rights of Animals (1987): theos-rights. Animals have rights because God, their creator, has rights in them.

Mission 3

Animals as food and intensive farming

  • Intensive (factory) farming: large numbers kept indoors at high density to produce cheap meat, eggs and milk (e.g. broiler chickens, enriched cages for hens, indoor pig units).
  • For: cheap protein for many people; efficient use of land per unit of food; disease controlled indoors.
  • Against: restricted natural behaviour and suffering; heavy antibiotic use; pollution and greenhouse emissions.
  • Reform, not abolition, is the UK pattern: conventional battery cages (EU, 2012) and sow stalls (UK, 1999) are banned.
  • Alternatives: free-range and organic farming; vegetarian and vegan diets; eating less meat.
  • Here animal welfare meets sustainability and climate change: livestock use land, water and feed and produce methane (see Mission 1).
Mission 3Diversity

Religious and secular views on eating animals

  • Catholic: animals may be used for food and clothing; causing them to suffer or die needlessly is contrary to human dignity (Catechism 2417–2418, paraphrase).
  • Other Christians: Orthodox Christians abstain from meat on fast days; Seventh-day Adventists encourage a vegetarian diet; Linzey argues for vegetarianism as a Christian ideal.
  • Judaism: meat is permitted (kosher, with shechitah, slaughter by one swift cut); tza'ar ba'alei chayim forbids causing animals needless pain.
  • Islam: meat is permitted if halal: the animal is slaughtered with Allah's name pronounced over it; Muslims cite the Prophet's teaching on mercy to animals.
  • Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism: ahimsa (non-harm) leads many to vegetarianism; Theravada monks may accept meat offered to them if the animal was not killed for them.
  • Secular: utilitarians (Singer) argue against factory farming; many humanists support welfare reform and eating less meat.
Mission 3Evaluate

Should animals have rights, or is welfare enough?

  • Claim: Animals should have rights, not just welfare protection.
  • For: Sentient animals have interests of their own (Bentham); subjects-of-a-life have inherent value (Regan).
  • For: Welfare law still permits killing and confinement; only rights would stop it. Linzey: God's rights in creatures demand respect.
  • Against: Contract thinkers argue that rights need reason and reciprocity; animals cannot bear duties.
  • Against: Equal consideration of interests can protect animals without rights language (Singer); rights would forbid even life-saving research. Catholic teaching gives humans duties of care, not animal rights.
  • Judgement criteria: What grounds moral status (reason, sentience, being a subject-of-a-life, God's creation)? Must rights be reciprocal? Which approach protects animals better in practice?
  • Spec issue: the appropriateness and value of religious perspectives (dominion, stewardship, theos-rights) in this debate.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Stewardship: Christians care for creation held in trust from God (Genesis 2:15; Psalm 24:1; Laudato Si'); White blamed dominion for the crisis.
  • Secular utilitarianism (Singer) counts all sentient beings and future generations; deep ecologists would still call it shallow.
  • Lovelock: the earth is a self-regulating system, Gaia; Næss: deep ecology gives all life intrinsic value.

Mission 2

  • Equality can mean equal worth, equal rights, equal opportunity or equal outcome; Christians ground it in the image of God (Genesis 1:27; Galatians 3:28), humanists in shared humanity (UDHR; Rawls).
  • King: agape, nonviolent direct action, just and unjust laws; Montgomery 1955, Birmingham and Washington 1963, Civil Rights Act 1964, Voting Rights Act 1965.
  • Tada: disability advocate; inclusion, God's purpose in suffering, sanctity of life; debated with the social model and Eiesland.

Mission 3

  • Views on animals depend on moral status: none (Descartes), indirect (Aquinas, Kant), sentience (Bentham, Singer), rights as subjects-of-a-life (Regan) or theos-rights (Linzey).
