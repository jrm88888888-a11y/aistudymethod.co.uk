Significant Concepts in Ethics

Before studying particular theories you need the toolkit of ethics: the vocabulary that lets you classify and compare any moral argument.

This lesson maps meta / normative / applied ethics, the deontological vs teleological divide, and absolutism vs relativism. Answer the questions and collect ⭐.

The map

Three levels of ethics

  • Meta-ethics asks what moral words mean and whether moral facts exist (e.g. what does "good" mean?).
  • Normative ethics proposes standards for right action (e.g. natural law, utilitarianism).
  • Applied ethics uses those standards on concrete issues (e.g. abortion, war).
Deon vs teleo

Deontological and teleological

  • Deontological ethics judges an act by the act itself — its rightness lies in duties, rules or intentions, not results (e.g. Kant, aspects of natural law).
  • Teleological / consequentialist ethics judges an act by its end or consequences (e.g. utilitarianism, situation ethics).

Absolute vs relative

Absolutism and relativism

  • Moral absolutism: some acts are right or wrong for everyone, always, regardless of circumstances (e.g. natural law’s core precepts).
  • Moral relativism: right and wrong depend on culture, situation or individual (e.g. situation ethics is relativistic — only love is fixed).

Approaches

The major approaches

  • Natural law (Aquinas): reason discerns God-given precepts — largely deontological/absolutist.
  • Kantian deontology: act only on maxims you could will as universal law — deontological/absolutist.
  • Utilitarianism (Bentham, Mill): maximise welfare — teleological/relativist.
  • Virtue ethics (Aristotle): focus on character rather than acts or rules.

Meta-ethics preview

Cognitive and non-cognitive

In meta-ethics, cognitivists think moral statements can be true or false (they describe something); non-cognitivists think they express attitudes or commands rather than facts. You will study this in detail under Ethical Language.
