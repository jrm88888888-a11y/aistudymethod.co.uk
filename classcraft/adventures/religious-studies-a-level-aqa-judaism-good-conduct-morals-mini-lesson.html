Mission 1

Mission 1: If the Torah holds 613 commandments, why did only ten go on the stone tablets?

Make a guess: If the Torah holds 613 commandments, why did only ten go on the stone tablets?

Mission 1

A mitzvah is a commandment

  • Mitzvah (plural mitzvot) means commandment: something God commands, not just a kind act.
  • The Talmud counts 613 mitzvot in the Torah: 248 positive ("do") and 365 negative ("do not") (Makkot 23b).
  • Many apply only in the Land of Israel, in Temple times or to priests; fewer than 300 can be kept by most Jews today.
  • A Jew becomes bar or bat mitzvah, "son" or "daughter of the commandment": now responsible for keeping them.
  • Keeping mitzvot is how Jews live out the covenant made at Sinai.
Mission 1

Towards God, towards people

  • The rabbis sort mitzvot by direction:
  • Bein adam la-Makom: between a person and God (e.g. Shabbat, prayer, kashrut).
  • Bein adam la-chavero: between a person and another person (e.g. honesty, charity, not gossiping).
  • Both kinds are commands of God: wronging a person is also a sin against God.
  • Mishnah Yoma 8:9 (paraphrase): Yom Kippur atones for sins against God; for sins against another person, you must first put things right with that person.
Mission 1Read the source

"Even the ten words"

> "I am the LORD thy God, who brought thee out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of bondage." (Exodus 20:2, JPS 1917)

> "And He declared unto you His covenant, which He commanded you to perform, even the ten words; and He wrote them upon two tables of stone." (Deuteronomy 4:13, JPS 1917)

  • The Torah calls them the ten words (Hebrew aseret ha-devarim); the rabbis call them Aseret ha-Dibrot, the Ten Sayings.

Q1 Why is "Ten Sayings" a better name than "Ten Commandments" for Exodus 20:2?

Show answer

It is a statement about God and the Exodus, not an order.

Q2 What does Deuteronomy 4:13 link the ten words to?

Show answer

The covenant God commanded Israel to keep.

Mission 1

Two tablets

  • The usual Jewish count:
  • Tablet 1: (1) I am the LORD your God · (2) no other gods, no idols · (3) do not take God's name in vain · (4) keep Shabbat · (5) honour father and mother.
  • Tablet 2: (6) do not murder · (7) do not commit adultery · (8) do not steal (the Talmud reads this as kidnapping) · (9) no false witness · (10) do not covet.
  • Common reading: tablet 1 is between people and God, tablet 2 between people.
  • Why are parents on tablet 1? The Talmud calls God, father and mother partners in making a person (Kiddushin 30b).
  • Christians number them differently (most start with "no other gods"): do not mix the two systems.
  • Many rabbis read the Ten as headings that hold all 613: e.g. "keep Shabbat" heads the festival laws, "do not steal" heads honest trade (Rashi on Exodus 24:12).

Mission 1Diversity

Orthodox, Reform, Hasidic

  • Orthodox: all 613 come from God (Torah min ha-shamayim, "Torah from heaven"); every mitzvah that can be kept today is binding; the Oral Torah fixes how.
  • Reform: the Torah was written by inspired humans; the ethical mitzvot remain central; ritual mitzvot are chosen by informed choice. Pittsburgh Platform (1885): "we accept as binding only its moral laws". Later Reform statements (Pittsburgh 1999) encourage more ritual practice.
  • Hasidic: fully Orthodox in practice, often stricter (hiddur mitzvah, beautifying the mitzvah); stress joy, kavanah (intention) and devekut (clinging to God) while doing each mitzvah.
  • Masorti (Conservative) Jews hold halakhah binding but developing.
Mission 1

Free to choose, responsible for the choice

  • "I have set before thee life and death … therefore choose life" (Deuteronomy 30:19, JPS 1917): humans are free to obey or disobey.
  • Rabbis teach two inclinations: yetzer ha-ra (selfish drive) and yetzer ha-tov (good drive); mitzvot train the first and strengthen the second.
  • Rabbi Akiva (Avot 3:15, paraphrase): all is foreseen by God, yet freedom of choice is given.
  • Individual reasoning: halakhah needs judgement: weighing which mitzvah applies, asking a rabbi, and (for Reform Jews) personal autonomy.
  • Top principle: pikuach nephesh, saving life, overrides almost every mitzvah (see Mission 2).
  • Your spec: "the place of individual reasoning and decision-making".

Mission 1Evaluate

Do mitzvot divide Jews?

  • Claim: The concept of mitzvot divides Judaism.
  • For: Different views on authority (God-given or human-inspired) lead to different practice: some Jews cannot eat in other Jews' homes or pray in each other's synagogues.
  • For: Disputes over Shabbat, kashrut and conversion standards split communities and institutions.
  • Against: All groups share the same Torah, the same 613 count and the core ethical mitzvot.
  • Against: Mitzvot shape shared moments: Pesach, Yom Kippur and Shabbat are kept in some form by most Jews.
  • Judgement criteria: Is the division about mitzvot or about authority? Does shared practice outweigh different standards? Is diversity a sign of division or of life?

Make a guess: Jewish law says break Shabbat to save a life. Could it ever allow weapons that kill millions?

Mission 2

Every life a world

  • Sanctity of life: Jews believe human life is holy because it comes from God and belongs to God.
  • Humans are made "in the image of God" (Genesis 1:27, JPS 1917), so in Jewish teaching killing a person attacks God's image (Genesis 9:6).
  • Mishnah Sanhedrin 4:5 (paraphrase): whoever destroys one life is as if they destroyed a whole world; whoever saves one life, as if they saved a whole world.
  • In Jewish teaching, every life has infinite value: one life is not worth less than many.
  • "Thou shalt not murder" (Exodus 20:13, JPS 1917).
Mission 2Read the source

"He shall live by them"

> "Ye shall therefore keep My statutes, and Mine ordinances, which if a man do, he shall live by them: I am the LORD." (Leviticus 18:5, JPS 1917)

> "… neither shalt thou stand idly by the blood of thy neighbour: I am the LORD." (Leviticus 19:16, JPS 1917)

  • Talmud (Yoma 85b, paraphrase): "live by them", not die by them. The mitzvot are given for life.

Q1 How does Yoma 85b use Leviticus 18:5 to justify breaking a mitzvah?

Show answer

The mitzvot exist for life, so when keeping one would cost a life, life comes first.

Q2 What extra duty does Leviticus 19:16 add?

Show answer

A duty to act to rescue, not only to avoid killing.

Mission 2

Pikuach nefesh overrides

  • Pikuach nefesh = "watching over a life": saving a life overrides almost every mitzvah.
  • Examples: driving to hospital on Shabbat; eating non-kosher food or medicine when needed; breaking a fast.
  • It is a duty, not just a permission: the person who acts fastest is praised (Yoma 84b).
  • It applies in doubt too: if a life might be at risk, act.
  • Many Jews see it as showing the Torah's purpose: "Her ways are ways of pleasantness" (Proverbs 3:17, JPS 1917).
  • AQA spelling: pikuach nephesh.
Mission 2

The limits

  • Three exceptions (Sanhedrin 74a): a Jew must die rather than commit idolatry, murder or forbidden sexual relations.
  • On murder the Talmud asks: "Who says your blood is redder?" You may not kill an innocent person to save yourself (Sanhedrin 74a; Pesachim 25b).
  • So pikuach nefesh does not allow trading one life for another.
  • It applies to an identifiable person in danger; how far it reaches to future or unknown people is debated (key for embryo research).

Mission 2

Who decides?

  • Halakhah gives room for individual judgement in danger.
  • Mishnah Yoma 8:5 and Yoma 83a (paraphrase): on Yom Kippur a sick person is fed if doctors say so; if the patient says they need food, feed them even against the doctors.
  • "The heart knoweth its own bitterness" (Proverbs 14:10, JPS 1917): the person in danger knows.
  • Jerusalem Talmud (Yoma 8:5, paraphrase): the one who stops to ask a rabbi first, when a life is at stake, is like one who sheds blood.
  • Orthodox Jews normally ask a rabbi (posek) on hard questions; Reform Jews stress personal autonomy and conscience.
  • Spec: "the place of individual reasoning and decision-making".
Mission 2

War: obligatory and optional

  • Judaism is not pacifist in principle, but war is a last resort.
  • Milchemet mitzvah (obligatory war): commanded by God in the Torah, and self-defence when Israel is attacked (Maimonides, Laws of Kings 5:1).
  • Milchemet reshut (optional war), e.g. to extend borders: needed the king and the Sanhedrin of 71 (Mishnah Sanhedrin 1:5). With no Sanhedrin today, many rabbis say no optional war can be authorised.
  • Self-defence: "If someone comes to kill you, rise early and kill him first" (Sanhedrin 72a, paraphrase).
  • Some Jews (e.g. Rabbi Aaron Samuel Tamaret, 1869–1931) argued for pacifism: a minority view.
Mission 2Read the source

Rules of war

> "When thou drawest nigh unto a city to fight against it, then proclaim peace unto it." (Deuteronomy 20:10, JPS 1917)

> "… thou shalt not destroy the trees thereof by wielding an axe against them; for thou mayest eat of them … for is the tree of the field man, that it should be besieged of thee?" (Deuteronomy 20:19, JPS 1917)

  • Further rules: men who are afraid go home (Deuteronomy 20:8); a siege must leave one side open so people can escape (Maimonides, Laws of Kings 6:7).
  • Bal tashchit ("do not destroy"): the rabbis widen Deuteronomy 20:19 into a ban on all needless destruction.

Show answer

An offer of peace.

Q2 What does the tree rule suggest about war's effect on the environment?

Show answer

Mission 2

Weapons of mass destruction

  • WMD: nuclear, chemical and biological weapons that kill indiscriminately and on a vast scale.
  • Rules above that WMD seem to break: offer of peace, an open side to escape, protection of trees and land.
  • Pikuach nefesh and "a whole world" (Mishnah Sanhedrin 4:5): mass killing destroys countless worlds.
  • But: self-defence is an obligation; a state may hold weapons to deter attack, aiming that they are never used.
  • After the Holocaust, many Jews stress that a people must be able to defend itself.
  • Different Jewish views exist: there is no single ruling for all Jews.
Mission 2Evaluate

Can Jewish teaching allow WMD?

  • Claim: Jewish teaching cannot accept the use of weapons of mass destruction.
  • For: They cannot separate fighters from civilians; the siege and tree rules aim to limit harm.
  • For: Sanctity of life: one life is a whole world; bal tashchit forbids needless destruction.
  • Against: Self-defence is a milchemet mitzvah; deterrence may protect life by preventing war.
  • Against: The Torah gives rules for war, not an absolute ban on weapons; leaders must judge real threats.
  • Judgement criteria: Use or possession? Can deterrence be moral if use would not be? Does halakhah for ancient war fit modern weapons?
Mission 2

Seeking peace

  • "Seek peace, and pursue it" (Psalm 34:14 [Heb. 34:15], JPS 1917): peace must be actively pursued.
  • Hope for the future: "they shall beat their swords into plowshares … neither shall they learn war any more" (Isaiah 2:4, JPS 1917).
  • Shalom means wholeness, not just no fighting; the Talmud calls Shalom a name of God (Shabbat 10b).
  • The Amidah ends with a prayer for peace.

Mission 3

When does a person begin?

  • In halakhah the unborn child's status grows with time:
  • Up to 40 days after conception: "mere water" (Yevamot 69b), a striking phrase for its low legal status.
  • After 40 days: like "a limb of its mother" (Chullin 58a): part of her body, not yet a separate person.
  • At birth (when the head emerges): a full nefesh (person) (Mishnah Ohalot 7:6; Sanhedrin 72b).
  • Before birth, the mother's life comes first: if a birth endangers her life, the fetus is removed (Mishnah Ohalot 7:6, paraphrase).
  • But the unborn child is still potential life, never valueless: harming it without serious reason is forbidden.
Mission 3Read the source

A fine, not a life

> "And if men strive together, and hurt a woman with child, so that her fruit depart, and yet no harm follow, he shall be surely fined …" (Exodus 21:22, JPS 1917)

> "But if any harm follow, then thou shalt give life for life," (Exodus 21:23, JPS 1917)

  • The rabbis read "harm" as the death of the woman.

Q1 What penalty is given for the loss of the unborn child?

Show answer

A money fine.

Q2 What does this suggest about the fetus's status compared with the mother's?

Show answer

It is valued, but not equal to a born person: only her death brings "life for life".

Mission 3

The unborn child and abortion

  • Mishnah Ohalot 7:6 (paraphrase): if a birth endangers the mother's life, the fetus is removed, for her life comes first; once the greater part (in the Talmud's version, Sanhedrin 72b: the head) has emerged, one life may not be set aside for another.
  • Maimonides (Laws of the Murderer 1:9) calls the fetus that threatens the mother a rodef ("pursuer").
  • Orthodox: abortion is allowed (sometimes required) to save the mother's life; some authorities also allow it for grave risk to her health, including mental health; never for convenience.
  • Reform and Liberal: the decision belongs to the woman, guided by Jewish values and conscience; support legal access.
  • No Jewish view treats the fetus as worthless; none treats it as fully equal to the mother before birth.
Quick check

Mission 3

Embryos and stem cells

  • Stem cells can become many types of cell; embryonic stem cells (from an embryo about 5 days old) can become almost any.
  • Taking them destroys the embryo. Most come from spare IVF embryos, made for fertility treatment and not used.
  • Hope: treatments for diseases such as Parkinson's, diabetes, spinal injury (pikuach nefesh).
  • UK law allows research on embryos only up to 14 days (Human Fertilisation and Embryology Act 1990).
  • Alternatives: adult stem cells; induced pluripotent cells (adult cells reprogrammed, since 2006).
Mission 3

An embryo outside the womb

  • A pre-implantation embryo in a dish cannot develop into a baby unless placed in a womb.
  • Many rabbis argue it has less status than even a 40-day fetus: it is not "in its mother" at all.
  • So destroying it is not murder, and some say not abortion either.
  • Others warn: it is still potential human life; creating embryos to destroy them treats life as a tool.
  • Israel's law, shaped partly by these rabbinic views, permits embryonic stem-cell research and bans reproductive cloning.
Mission 3

Pikuach nefesh and research

  • The Torah permits and commands healing: "cause him to be thoroughly healed" (Exodus 21:19, JPS 1917; Bava Kamma 85a).
  • For research: if pikuach nefesh overrides almost every mitzvah, it can justify using embryos that will be discarded anyway, to save future patients.
  • Against: pikuach nefesh classically needs a person in front of us in present danger (Rabbi Ezekiel Landau, 1713–1793); future patients from research may not count.
  • Reply: today, sick people who could benefit are real and identifiable worldwide.
  • And pikuach nefesh never permits murder (see Mission 2): so the question turns on whether the embryo is a person.

Mission 3Evaluate

Is pikuach nefesh compatible with embryo research?

  • Claim: Pikuach nefesh is compatible with embryo research.
  • For: Healing is a mitzvah; some Orthodox rabbis argue a dish embryo is not a person; spare embryos would be discarded anyway.
  • For: Stem-cell research shows real potential to save and improve lives.
  • Against: Pikuach nefesh classically needs an identifiable person in present danger; research benefits are uncertain and future.
  • Against: Other rabbis say early life is still protected; alternatives such as reprogrammed cells exist; creating embryos to destroy them risks treating life as a tool.
  • Judgement criteria: What is the embryo's status? Does pikuach nefesh cover future patients? Spare embryos or embryos made for research? Are alternatives good enough?
Mission check

?A rabbi is asked about three cases: a 5-day embryo frozen in a fertility clinic; a 10-week fetus in the womb; a baby whose head has emerged during birth. How do most Orthodox authorities rank their status, lowest first?

Jewish law weighs potential life by stage and place, while an actual life comes first. That scale is what lets many rabbis permit research on the cells in the dish, and what makes others cautious.

Mission 4: If the earth belongs to God, what are humans allowed to do with it?

Make a guess: If the earth belongs to God, what are humans allowed to do with it?

Mission 4

The earth is God's

  • Jewish teaching starts from ownership: the world belongs to God, not to humans.
  • "The earth is the LORD'S, and the fulness thereof; the world, and they that dwell therein." (Psalm 24:1, JPS 1917)
  • Of the Land of Israel: "the land is Mine; for ye are strangers and settlers with Me." (Leviticus 25:23, JPS 1917)
  • So humans are tenants or trustees: they may use the world, but must answer to its owner.
  • Stewardship: caring for something on behalf of its owner, and being accountable for it.
Mission 4Read the source

Dominion and keeping

> "have dominion over the fish of the sea, and over the fowl of the air, and over every living thing that creepeth upon the earth." (Genesis 1:28, JPS 1917)

> "And the LORD God took the man, and put him into the garden of Eden to dress it and to keep it." (Genesis 2:15, JPS 1917)

  • Hebrew of Genesis 2:15: le'ovdah u-leshomrah, "to work it and to guard it".
  • A midrash (Ecclesiastes Rabbah 7:13, paraphrase): God shows Adam the trees of Eden and warns him not to spoil the world, for there will be no one to repair it after him.

Q1 Which verse gives humans power, and which gives them a duty?

Show answer

Genesis 1:28 gives power, "dominion"; Genesis 2:15 gives a duty, "to keep it".

Q2 How does many Jews' reading of the two verses together limit dominion?

Show answer

Dominion is power held in trust: humans rule as keepers, answerable to God.

Mission 4

Bal tashchit: do not destroy

  • In war, Israel must not cut down an enemy city's fruit trees: "for thou mayest eat of them, but thou shalt not cut them down" (Deuteronomy 20:19, JPS 1917).
  • The verse asks: "is the tree of the field man, that it should be besieged of thee?" The tree is not the enemy.
  • The rabbis widened this into a general principle: bal tashchit, "do not destroy" needlessly.
  • Talmud (Shabbat 129a; Kiddushin 32a): breaking vessels or tearing clothes for no reason is forbidden.
  • Maimonides (Mishneh Torah, Laws of Kings 6:10, paraphrase): the ban covers anyone who needlessly destroys vessels, clothes, buildings, springs or food.
  • Not a ban on all use: waste and pointless destruction are the wrong.
Mission 4

Animals: tza'ar ba'alei chayim

  • Tza'ar ba'alei chayim: "the suffering of living creatures". Causing animals needless pain is forbidden; many authorities treat this as a Torah law (Talmud Bava Metzia 32b).
  • Animals rest on Shabbat too: "that thine ox and thine ass may have rest" (Exodus 23:12, JPS 1917).
  • "Thou shalt not muzzle the ox when he treadeth out the corn." (Deuteronomy 25:4, JPS 1917)
  • Send away the mother bird before taking the young (Deuteronomy 22:6–7).
  • Feed your animals before you eat yourself (Talmud Berakhot 40a, from Deuteronomy 11:15).
  • "A righteous man regardeth the life of his beast" (Proverbs 12:10, JPS 1917).
  • Meat is permitted (Genesis 9:3), but slaughter must follow shechitah, which aims at a swift death.
Mission 4

Shemittah and Jubilee

  • Shemittah (release): every seventh year the land of Israel rests. "In the seventh year shall be a sabbath of solemn rest for the land" (Leviticus 25:4, JPS 1917).
  • No sowing or pruning; what grows by itself is free for the poor and for animals (Exodus 23:10–11). Debts are released (Deuteronomy 15:1–2).
  • Jubilee (Yovel): every fiftieth year, liberty is proclaimed and land returns to its original family (Leviticus 25:10).
  • Message: the land is held in trust; no one owns it for ever; the poor and the soil both get relief.
  • Today: many rabbis hold shemittah binding only in the Land of Israel, and by rabbinic law while most Jews live outside it. Since the late 1800s some rabbis allow a temporary sale of land to a non-Jew (heter mechirah), supported by Rav Abraham Isaac Kook (1865–1935); many Haredi Jews reject it and buy produce grown elsewhere. The Jubilee is not observed today.

Mission 4

A changing understanding

  • 1967: historian Lynn White Jr (1907–1987), "The Historical Roots of Our Ecologic Crisis" (Science), blames the "dominion" of Genesis 1:28, read in Western Christianity, for treating nature as there only for human use.
  • Jewish thinkers replied: in the Tenakh and rabbinic law, dominion is limited by keeping, bal tashchit, shemittah and care for animals.
  • As science showed pollution, climate change and species loss, stewardship came to mean global duties, not only local ones (trees, animals, one field).
  • Examples: Jewish environmental groups (e.g. COEJL in the USA, founded 1993; EcoJudaism in the UK); Tu BiShvat, the New Year for Trees, now kept by many as an environmental festival; synagogues cutting energy use and waste.
  • So older principles are applied to new problems: bal tashchit to energy and plastic waste; "to keep it" to future generations.
Mission 4Diversity

Different Jewish emphases

  • Orthodox writers usually ground stewardship in halakhah: bal tashchit, tza'ar ba'alei chayim and shemittah as binding laws, with humans above animals.
  • Progressive Jews often stress tikkun olam ("repairing the world") and see environmental action as a moral and prophetic duty.
  • Meat: most Jews eat kosher meat. Some argue for vegetarianism: Genesis 1:29 gives humans plants to eat, and meat is only permitted after the Flood (Genesis 9:3). Rav Kook saw a vegetarian future as an ideal of the Messianic Age (paraphrase).
  • Shechitah debates: some European states have banned slaughter without stunning; most Jewish authorities defend shechitah as humane.
  • Human uniqueness: most agree humans have a special status (image of God) but disagree on how far animals' interests limit human use.

Mission 4Evaluate

Is Judaism's teaching enough for today's crisis?

  • Claim: Traditional Jewish teaching on stewardship is adequate for today's environmental crisis.
  • For: God's ownership (Psalm 24:1) makes all use accountable; bal tashchit already forbids waste.
  • For: Shemittah models rest and limits on growth; tza'ar ba'alei chayim gives animals moral standing.
  • Against: The laws were written for local farming, not global problems such as climate change; applying them needs new interpretation.
  • Against: Genesis 1:28 sets humans over nature; critics such as White argue this encourages exploitation.
  • Judgement criteria: Is dominion read with Genesis 2:15? Can principles stretch to new cases without losing their meaning? Does Jewish practice match the teaching?
Mission 4Worked answer

Linking belief, source and development

  • Belief: Jews are stewards, not owners, of the world.
  • Source: "The earth is the LORD'S" (Psalm 24:1); "to dress it and to keep it" (Genesis 2:15).
  • Development: so human use is limited: bal tashchit forbids needless destruction (Deuteronomy 20:19), even of an enemy's trees.
  • Application: today many Jews apply this to energy, waste and climate change.
  • Diversity: Orthodox Jews stress halakhic duties; many Progressive Jews link it to tikkun olam.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • A mitzvah is a commandment from God; the Talmud counts 613, between people and God and between people.
  • The Ten Sayings (Aseret ha-Dibrot) begin with God's self-introduction; rabbinic tradition (Saadia, Rashi) reads them as ten categories of the 613.
  • Orthodox Jews hold all 613 binding; Reform Jews treat ethical mitzvot as binding and ritual as informed choice; Hasidic Jews keep Orthodox practice with stress on joy and intention.
  • Humans are free to choose and responsible for their choices; pikuach nephesh overrides almost every mitzvah.

Mission 2

  • In Jewish teaching, life is holy because humans are made in God's image; saving one life is like saving a world.
  • Pikuach nefesh overrides almost every mitzvah, even in doubt; it never permits idolatry, murder or forbidden relations.
  • Obligatory wars (including self-defence) and optional wars (needing the Sanhedrin); rules of war limit harm; Jews differ on whether WMD can ever be justified, for example as deterrence.

Mission 3

  • The unborn child's status grows: "mere water" up to 40 days, part of the mother after, a full person at birth; the mother's life comes first.
  • Many Orthodox rabbis permit research on spare embryos outside the womb, as not yet persons, under pikuach nefesh; others urge caution.

Mission 4

  • The earth is God's (Psalm 24:1); humans are stewards, given dominion (Genesis 1:28) but told "to keep it" (Genesis 2:15).
  • Bal tashchit (do not destroy needlessly) and shemittah (the land's seventh-year rest) limit human use.
  • Tza'ar ba'alei chayim forbids needless animal suffering; modern Jews apply these principles to global problems.
