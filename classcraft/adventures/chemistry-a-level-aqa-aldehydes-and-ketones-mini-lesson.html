Mission 1

Mission 1: Why does cyanide add across a carbonyl double bond but leave an alkene double bond alone?

Make a guess: Why does cyanide add across a carbonyl double bond but leave an alkene double bond alone?

Mission 1

The carbonyl group

  • Carbonyl: C=O.
  • Aldehyde: C=O at the end of the chain, with an H on it. R–CHO. Name ends -al: ethanal, CH3CHO.
  • Ketone: C=O inside the chain. R–CO–R′. Name ends -one: propanone, CH3COCH3.
  • The C=O carbon is trigonal planar (bond angles about 120°).
Mission 1

Why nucleophiles choose C=O

  • O is more electronegative: Cδ+=Oδ−.
  • A nucleophile (lone-pair donor) is attracted to the δ+ carbon.
  • Alkene C=C: non-polar and electron-rich. It repels nucleophiles and attracts electrophiles.
  • So C=O undergoes nucleophilic addition: nucleophile to C, then H to O.
Mission 1

Reduction with NaBH4

  • NaBH4 in water (or aqueous methanol), warm.
  • Aldehyde → primary alcohol: CH3CHO + 2[H] → CH3CH2OH
  • Ketone → secondary alcohol: CH3COCH3 + 2[H] → CH3CH(OH)CH3
  • [H] stands for the reducing agent.
  • The nucleophile is the hydride ion, H⁻.
Mission 1

Mechanism: NaBH4

  • Arrow 1: from the lone pair of H⁻ to the carbonyl C.
  • Arrow 2: from the C=O π bond to the O. An alkoxide ion, C–O⁻, forms.
  • Arrow 3: from a lone pair on O⁻ to an H of H2O.
  • Arrow 4: from that O–H bond to its O. OH⁻ is released.
Quick check
?Butanone, CH3COCH2CH3, is reduced.
What is the organic product?
  • Reagent: NaBH4(aq).
Mission 1

Adding cyanide: hydroxynitriles

  • Ethanal + HCN → 2-hydroxypropanenitrile, CH3CH(OH)CN
  • Propanone + HCN → 2-hydroxy-2-methylpropanenitrile, (CH3)2C(OH)CN
  • The chain gains one carbon.
  • Reagent: KCN, followed by dilute acid.
  • HCN is a weak acid: little CN⁻ on its own. The cyanide salt supplies the CN⁻ nucleophile.
  • KCN hazards: highly toxic if swallowed or absorbed. With acid it releases toxic HCN gas. Use a fume cupboard, gloves and small amounts.
Mission 1

Mechanism: cyanide

  • Show Cδ+=Oδ− dipole.
  • Arrow 1: from the lone pair on the C of CN⁻ to the carbonyl C.
  • Arrow 2: from the C=O π bond to the O. An alkoxide intermediate, C–O⁻, forms.
  • Arrow 3: from a lone pair on O⁻ to H⁺ (from the dilute acid).
Quick check
?In the mechanism for the reaction of propanone with cyanide, where does the first curly arrow start and end?
Mission 1

Why a racemic mixture forms

  • The C=O group is planar.
  • CN⁻ attacks from above or below with equal probability.
  • If the carbon ends up with four different groups, it is a chiral centre.
  • So the product is a 50:50 mixture of enantiomers: a racemic mixture.
  • It does not rotate plane-polarised light.
  • Aldehydes (except methanal) and unsymmetrical ketones give racemates. Propanone does not: two CH3 groups.
Quick check
?Which carbonyl compound gives a racemic mixture when it reacts with HCN?
Mission check

?Hex-5-en-2-one, CH2=CHCH2CH2COCH3, has a C=C bond and a C=O bond.
What is the organic product?
  • It is warmed with NaBH4(aq).

Nucleophiles (H⁻, CN⁻) are drawn to the δ+ carbon of C=O. The C=C bond has no δ+ end, so they pass it by.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Aldehydes and ketones look alike. Why does only one of them turn a test tube into a mirror?

Make a guess: Aldehydes and ketones look alike. Why does only one of them turn a test tube into a mirror?

Mission 2

Why aldehydes are easy to oxidise

  • Aldehyde: an H on the carbonyl carbon. Ketone: two carbons on it.
  • Mild oxidising agents turn that C–H into C–OH: aldehyde → carboxylic acid.
  • RCHO + [O] → RCOOH
  • Ketones would need a C–C bond broken: mild oxidising agents cannot do it.
  • The oxidising agent is reduced, and changes colour. That is the test.
Mission 2

Tollens' reagent: the silver mirror

  • Make it fresh: AgNO3(aq) + a drop of NaOH(aq) → brown precipitate; add dilute NH3(aq) until it just dissolves. This gives [Ag(NH3)2]⁺.
  • Warm in a water bath, not over a flame.
  • Aldehyde: silver mirror (or grey-black precipitate). Ketone: no change.
  • Ag⁺ + e⁻ → Ag: silver ions are reduced; the aldehyde is oxidised.
  • Pour away straight after use: stored reagent can form explosive solids.
Mission 2

Fehling's and Benedict's solutions

  • Blue solution of a copper(II) complex, in alkali.
  • Warm with an aldehyde: brick-red precipitate of copper(I) oxide, Cu2O.
  • Cu²⁺ + e⁻ → Cu⁺
  • Ketone: stays blue.
  • Aromatic aldehydes such as benzaldehyde give little or no change with Fehling's: use Tollens'.
Quick check
?Propanal is warmed with Tollens' reagent and a silver mirror forms. Which statement is correct?
Mission 2

Required practical 6: alcohols and aldehydes

  • Unknowns: propanal, propanone, propan-1-ol.
  • Step 1: Tollens' (or Fehling's). Silver mirror (or brick-red) → propanal.
  • Step 2: warm the other two with acidified K2Cr2O7.
  • Orange → green → propan-1-ol (a primary alcohol is oxidised).
  • Stays orange → propanone (a ketone resists oxidation).
  • Tertiary alcohols also stay orange.
Quick check
?Propanal and propanone are each warmed with Fehling's solution. What is seen?
Mission check

?4-Oxopentanal, CH3COCH2CH2CHO, has an aldehyde group and a ketone group. It is warmed with Tollens' reagent. What happens?

The H on an aldehyde's carbonyl carbon is what makes it easy to oxidise. That is why only aldehydes give a silver mirror.

Mission 3

Mission 3: How can a chemist make a molecule's carbon chain exactly one atom longer?

Make a guess: How can a chemist make a molecule's carbon chain exactly one atom longer?

Mission 3

Building the skeleton

  • Most organic reactions only swap functional groups. The carbon chain stays the same length.
  • To make a longer chain, you need a reaction that forms a new C–C bond.
  • The trick: a carbon nucleophile, a carbon atom with a lone pair or a δ− charge.
  • Cyanide, ⁻:C≡N, is the simplest: it adds one carbon.
Mission 3

Cyanide + halogenoalkane

  • Reagent: KCN in ethanol, heat under reflux.
  • CH3CH2Br + CN⁻ → CH3CH2CN + Br⁻
  • Product: propanenitrile. The chain grows from 2 C to 3 C.
  • Naming: count the C≡N carbon in the chain. CH3CH2CN is propanenitrile.
  • Mechanism: nucleophilic substitution. Lone pair on the C of CN⁻ attacks the δ+ C; the C–Br electrons go to Br.
  • KCN is highly toxic. Acid turns cyanide into HCN gas, so cyanide and acid only ever meet in a fume cupboard, in small amounts, under controlled conditions.
Mission 3

Cyanide + carbonyl

  • Aldehydes and ketones add cyanide across C=O: hydroxynitriles (see Mission 1).
  • Reagent: KCN, followed by dilute acid.
  • CH3CHO → CH3CH(OH)CN, 2-hydroxypropanenitrile. Chain: 2 C → 3 C.
  • Mechanism: nucleophilic addition. Product is racemic when the new C is chiral.
  • KCN hazards: toxic if swallowed or absorbed; releases HCN gas with acid. Fume cupboard, gloves, small amounts.
Quick check
?1-Bromobutane is heated under reflux with potassium cyanide in ethanol. What is the organic product?
Mission 3

Nitrile → amine

  • Reduction with LiAlH4, or with H2 and a nickel catalyst: CH3CH2CN + 4[H] → CH3CH2CH2NH2
  • Product: propylamine (propan-1-amine), one carbon longer than bromoethane.
  • Unlike ammonia + halogenoalkane, this gives only the primary amine: no further substitution.
Quick check
?To make a hydroxynitrile, an aldehyde is reacted with KCN, followed by dilute acid, rather than with HCN itself. What is the main reason?
Mission check

?Bromomethane, CH3Br, has one carbon. Which route turns it into ethylamine, CH3CH2NH2?

Cyanide adds the extra carbon, then you choose what the C≡N becomes: an amine, or (by hydrolysis) an acid.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • C=O is polar: nucleophiles add to the δ+ carbon, then the O gains H. C=C is not polar: no nucleophilic addition.
  • Attack from either face of the planar C=O gives a racemic mixture when a chiral centre forms.

Mission 2

  • Aldehydes (H on the C=O carbon) are oxidised to carboxylic acids; ketones are not. The reagent is reduced and changes colour.

Mission 3

  • CN⁻ adds one carbon: with halogenoalkanes (substitution) or carbonyls (addition).
  • Nitriles can be reduced to amines.
