Mission 1

Mission 1: When a price is too high or too low, what pulls it back to where buyers and sellers agree?

Make a guess: When a price is too high or too low, what pulls it back to where buyers and sellers agree?

Mission 1

Equilibrium: where the curves cross

  • Equilibrium: the price at which quantity demanded = quantity supplied.
  • At that price the market clears: every unit offered is bought, every buyer willing to pay it gets one.
  • No pressure for the price to change, unless something else changes.
  • Label it Pe (equilibrium price) and Qe (equilibrium quantity).
  • In this model: many buyers and many sellers, none big enough to set the price alone (a competitive market).
Mission 1

Price too high: excess supply

  • At a price above Pe, quantity supplied > quantity demanded.
  • This is excess supply, or a surplus.
  • Size of the surplus = Qs − Qd at that price.
  • Signs: unsold stock piling up, discounts, "clearance" sales.
  • Only Qd is actually sold: firms cannot sell more than buyers want.
  • Market forces: sellers cut the price to shift their stock.
Mission 1

Price too low: excess demand

  • At a price below Pe, quantity demanded > quantity supplied.
  • This is excess demand, or a shortage.
  • Size of the shortage = Qd − Qs at that price.
  • Signs: queues, waiting lists, "sold out", resale above the original price.
  • Only Qs is actually bought: buyers cannot buy what is not supplied.
  • Market forces: buyers compete, so the price is bid up.
  • A shortage is not the same as scarcity. Scarcity is always present; a shortage exists only when the price is below equilibrium.

Mission 1

Market forces: movements, not shifts

  • Surplus → price falls → quantity demanded rises and quantity supplied falls → until Qd = Qs.
  • Shortage → price rises → quantity demanded falls and quantity supplied rises → until Qd = Qs.
  • These are movements along the curves. Neither curve shifts: the price is on the axis, so it cannot shift its own curve.
  • Say: "the price falls, causing an extension of demand and a contraction of supply" — not: "demand rises and supply falls".

Mission 1Evaluate

Does a market always clear?

  • The model predicts the price moves to Pe. How fast, and whether it gets there, depends on:
  • Price flexibility: some prices are sticky. Firms with printed price lists, long contracts, or worries about customer goodwill (e.g. concert and sports tickets) may leave a shortage.
  • Information: buyers and sellers must notice the surplus or shortage before they react.
  • Time lags: farmers cannot grow more until the next harvest, so a shortage can last a season.
  • Intervention: a legal maximum or minimum price stops the market clearing.
  • And the target moves: if a curve shifts, the market chases a new equilibrium (see Mission 2).
Mission check

In a shortage the short side, supply, limits sales, so a rising price can raise the quantity traded until Qd = Qs.

Mission 2

A shift starts a chain

  • Something other than the good's own price changes. One curve shifts.
  • Example: incomes rise and the good is normal. Demand shifts right, D1 → D2.
  • At the old price P1 there is now excess demand (a shortage).
  • The price is bid up. Suppliers respond by moving along S.
  • New equilibrium: higher price P2, higher quantity Q2.
  • Say: "a rise in demand causes an extension of supply" — not: "supply increases".

  • Supply shifts: price and quantity move opposite ways.
  • Always ask first: which curve does the event shift? Usually only one.
  • A supply shift right means more is offered at every price, not a move "down" the graph.

Mission 2

How far? It depends on elasticity

  • Same leftward shift of supply, two demand curves:
  • Price inelastic demand (drawn steeper through the starting point): large price rise, small fall in quantity.
  • Price elastic demand (drawn flatter through the same point): small price rise, large fall in quantity.
  • Slope is only a drawing guide here: elasticity is about percentage changes.
  • The same logic applies to supply: inelastic supply turns a demand shift mainly into a price change.
  • So markets with inelastic demand and supply have volatile prices. Example: farm commodities, where a harvest cannot be changed until next season.
  • Elasticity in full: (demand) (supply).
Mission 2Case

Cocoa, 2024

  • Most of the world's cocoa is grown in West Africa.
  • Late 2024: hot weather with less rain threatened crops in Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana. Another global supply deficit was expected.
  • Supply shifted left. Chocolate makers have few substitutes for cocoa beans.
  • Result: cocoa futures in London rose 66% in three months: from US$6,896 per tonne (1 Oct 2024) to US$11,441 (31 Dec 2024).
  • On the diagram: S1 → S2, price P1 → P2 up sharply, quantity Q1 → Q2 down.
Mission 2

Real markets: housing and energy

  • Housing: demand rises with population, incomes and cheaper or easier mortgages.
  • Supply is price inelastic: land, planning rules and years of building. So rising demand mostly raises prices, not the number of homes.
  • Energy: in 2022, Russia cut pipeline gas deliveries to Europe. Supply shifted left.
  • Demand for gas is inelastic in the short run (heating, power stations), so European wholesale gas prices hit record highs.
  • Over time, higher prices brought more liquefied gas by ship (extension of supply) and lower use (contraction of demand).
  • Commodities: The earlier screens.

Mission 2Evaluate

How far can we trust the model?

  • The model predicts the direction of change well. How useful it is depends on:
  • Ceteris paribus: real markets face many changes at once, so outcomes are often double shifts with uncertain results.
  • Competition: it assumes many buyers and sellers who take the price. Firms with market power set prices.
  • Information and rationality: it assumes buyers and sellers know prices and respond to them. Habits and limited information slow responses.
  • Speed of adjustment: prices can be sticky, or overshoot, so markets can spend time out of equilibrium.
  • We never see the curves: data show only prices and quantities traded, so we must work out which curve shifted from other evidence.
Mission check

?Over one year, the price of a good rose sharply, but the quantity bought and sold stayed the same. The demand curve slopes down and the supply curve slopes up. Which change could explain this?

When price and quantity move in a pattern no single shift can give, look for two shifts working together.

Mission 3

What a free market gets right

  • Each point on the demand curve shows the marginal benefit (MB) of one more unit to buyers.
  • Each point on the supply curve shows the marginal cost (MC) of one more unit to sellers.
  • At competitive equilibrium, P = MB = MC.
  • Consumer surplus: what buyers would pay minus what they do pay.
  • Producer surplus: what sellers receive minus the least they would accept.
  • Consumer + producer surplus = community surplus.
  • If there is no market failure, equilibrium makes this total as large as possible: allocative efficiency.
Mission 3

Market equilibrium vs the social optimum

  • Buyers and sellers weigh only their private benefits and costs: market equilibrium is where MPB = MPC.
  • Society counts all benefits and costs: the social optimum is where MSB = MSC.
  • Allocative efficiency: resources make the goods society values most; the value of the last unit equals its full cost, MSB = MSC (for a single firm: P = MC).
  • No outside effects: MPB = MSB and MPC = MSC, so the market reaches the social optimum.
  • Outside effects: the market equilibrium and the social optimum are at different quantities.
  • Say: "the market equilibrium is not the socially optimum position" — not: "the market is not in equilibrium". A market can be in equilibrium and still be inefficient.
Quick check

Mission 3Graph lab

Too much, too little

Illustrative data: MSB = 120 − Q and MSC = 20 + Q. The social optimum is Q = 50.

  • Takeaway: any quantity other than MSB = MSC creates a welfare loss, whether output is too high or too low.
Mission 3

Worked example: measuring the welfare loss

  • Illustrative data: MSB = 120 − Q; MSC = 20 + Q (price in $, quantity in units per week).
  • Optimum: 120 − Q = 20 + Q, so Q = 50, value 70.
  • Output is 65 instead. At Q = 65: MSC = 20 + 65 = 85; MSB = 120 − 65 = 55.
  • Each extra unit from 50 to 65 costs society more than it is worth.
  • Welfare loss = triangle between MSC and MSB, from Q = 50 to 65.
  • Area = ½ × base × height = ½ × (65 − 50) × (85 − 55) = ½ × 15 × 30 = 225.
  • Total surplus at the optimum = ½ × 50 × (120 − 20) = 2,500. At 65 it falls to 2,500 − 225 = 2,275.
Quick check

Mission 3

What market failure means

  • Market failure: the price mechanism fails to allocate resources efficiently, so the market outcome is not the social optimum.
  • The result is too much or too little of a good, and a welfare loss.
  • Complete market failure: no market exists at all: a missing market (e.g. street lighting).
  • Partial market failure: a market exists, but at the wrong price or quantity (e.g. too much tobacco, too little vaccination).
Mission 3

Sources of market failure

SourceWhat goes wrongMission
Negative externalitiesCost to third parties: overproduction
Positive externalitiesBenefit to third parties: underproduction
Public goodsFree riders: often no market at all
  • Information gaps (asymmetric information): buyers or sellers misjudge value.
  • Market power (monopoly): output restricted, price above MC.
Mission 3

Private, external, social

  • Externality: a cost or benefit to a third party, not paid for or received through the market.
  • Social cost = private cost + external cost. At the margin: MSC = MPC + MEC.
  • Social benefit = private benefit + external benefit. At the margin: MSB = MPB + MEB.
  • External cost from production (e.g. pollution): MSC above MPC, so the market overproduces.
  • External benefit from consumption (e.g. vaccination): MSB above MPB, so the market underproduces.
  • Either way: a welfare (deadweight) loss triangle that points to the social optimum.
Mission 3

Market power

  • A firm with market power (e.g. a monopoly) can hold output back to keep its price high.
  • It charges the price on the demand curve at that output: P > MC.
  • So buyers value the next unit more than it costs to make, but it is not produced: underproduction.
  • Result: a welfare (deadweight) loss, even with no externalities.
Quick check
?In a region, a factory's smoke harms nearby farms' crops, and there is no way to charge the factory for this. Which statement is correct?
Mission 3

Why governments step in

  • Reasons for intervention:
  • to correct market failure (move output towards MSB = MSC)
  • to promote equity and support low-income households
  • to support firms (e.g. farmers, new industries)
  • to influence production and consumption (e.g. more vaccination, less smoking)
  • to raise government revenue.
  • Intervention can also fail: a tax or rule set at the wrong level can create a new welfare loss.
Mission check

?Illustrative data: a competitive market for bread has no externalities and settles at 1,000 loaves a day. The government pays bakers to raise output to 1,200 loaves a day. What happens to total surplus from bread?

With no market failure, equilibrium is already the efficient quantity. Moving away from it in either direction loses surplus.

Mission 4

A tax on spending

  • An indirect tax is a tax on spending on goods and services.
  • The seller hands the tax to the government. Buyers pay it only through the price.
  • Examples: VAT and sales taxes; duties on fuel, alcohol and tobacco.
  • To the seller, the tax is like an extra cost of each unit sold.
Mission 4

Specific or ad valorem?

  • Specific tax: a fixed amount per unit (e.g. per litre of fuel).
    • S shifts up by the same amount at every quantity: parallel.
  • Ad valorem tax: a percentage of the price (e.g. VAT).
    • The tax per unit grows as the price rises: the new curve pivots, diverging from S.
  • Example (illustrative data), a 20% tax on the pre-tax price:
    • pre-tax price £5 → tax £1
    • pre-tax price £10 → tax £2
Quick check
?A government charges a tax of 15% on the pre-tax price of every phone sold. How does it change the supply curve?
Mission 4

The tax diagram

  • Build it in steps (a tax of t per unit):
  • 1. Start at equilibrium: Pe, Qe.
  • 2. Supply shifts up by the tax: S → S + tax. Vertical gap = t.
  • 3. New equilibrium: price buyers pay rises to P1; quantity falls to Q1.
  • Sellers keep P1 − t per unit.
  • The price rises by less than the tax (unless demand is perfectly inelastic or supply perfectly elastic).
  • Say: "supply decreases; there is a contraction of demand" — not: "demand falls".
Mission 4

Who bears the tax?

  • The incidence of a tax: how its burden is shared between consumers and producers.
  • Consumers' share per unit = P1 − Pe (the rise in the price they pay).
  • Producers' share per unit = Pe − (P1 − t) (the fall in the price they keep).
  • The two shares add up to the tax, t.
  • Government revenue = t × Q1: the rectangle between P1 and P1 − t, from 0 to Q1.
  • Who hands the money over (the formal incidence or impact) does not decide who bears it.
  • Say: "impact" for who pays the tax authority; "incidence" for who bears the burden.

Mission 4

Worked example

  • Illustrative data. Before the tax: Pe = £6, Qe = 60 units.
  • Specific tax: £1.50 per unit. After the tax: consumers pay £7, Q1 = 50.
  • Producers keep £7 − £1.50 = £5.50.
  • Consumers' burden: (£7 − £6) × 50 = £50.
  • Producers' burden: (£6 − £5.50) × 50 = £25.
  • Government revenue: £1.50 × 50 = £75 (= £50 + £25 ✓).
  • Consumers bear 2/3 of the tax: demand here is less elastic than supply (PED −1, PES 2 at the start).
Quick check
?Illustrative data. Before a tax, a good sells at 5.00 per unit and 100 units are traded. After a tax of 2.00 per unit, consumers pay 6.20 and 76 units are traded. What is the total burden of the tax on producers?
Mission 4

Elasticity decides the split

  • The side of the market that is less price elastic bears more of the tax.
  • Inelastic demand (e.g. cigarettes, fuel): sellers raise the price and buyers keep buying, so consumers bear most.
  • Elastic demand: buyers switch away, so sellers must absorb the tax: producers bear most.
  • Extreme cases:
    • perfectly inelastic demand: consumers bear it all
    • perfectly elastic demand: producers bear it all
  • Approximate consumers' share = PES ÷ (PES + |PED|).
  • Say: "demand is price inelastic" — not: "demand is low".

Start: a tax of 1.50 per unit; PED −1, PES 2. Consumers bear 2/3 of each unit's tax.

  • Takeaway: less elastic demand → consumers bear a bigger share (here 8/9), and Q falls less.

Mission 4

Stakeholders

  • Consumers: pay a higher price, buy less; consumer surplus falls.
  • Producers: keep less per unit, sell less; producer surplus falls. Revenue falls.
  • Workers: fewer units made can mean fewer jobs in the industry.
  • Government: gains revenue (t × Q1) to spend; can target goods it wants used less.
  • Society:
    • where the good has a negative externality, the tax can move output toward the social optimum
  • Indirect taxes are often regressive: lower-income households spend a larger share of income on them.
Mission 4

Case: the UK sugar levy

  • Soft Drinks Industry Levy, from April 2018, charged on producers and importers. Rates at the start:
    • 18p per litre: drinks with 5 g to 8 g of sugar per 100 ml
    • 24p per litre: 8 g or more
  • (Both rates have risen each year since April 2025.)
  • Below 5 g: no levy. So firms could avoid it by cutting sugar.
  • 2015 to 2018, average sugar in UK soft drinks fell from 4.4 g to 2.9 g per 100 ml.
  • Most of the fall came from reformulation, not from buyers switching.
  • Caution: the study shows a trend; it cannot prove the levy alone caused it.
Mission 4Evaluate

Does the tax work?

  • It depends on PED:
    • inelastic demand → high revenue, small fall in Q
    • elastic demand → bigger cut in consumption, less revenue
    • one tax rarely maximises both
  • It depends on information: to correct an externality, the tax should match the external cost. That is hard to measure.
  • It depends on time and side effects: PED grows in the long run; very high taxes can encourage smuggling and illegal markets.
Mission check

?A city has a fixed number of parking spaces and no space to add more. The city now charges the owners a fixed tax for each space used per day. Demand for parking is unchanged. Who bears the tax?

The less elastic side bears more. When one side cannot respond at all, it bears the whole tax, whoever is legally charged.

Mission 5

A payment per unit

  • A subsidy is a payment from the government to producers that lowers their costs.
  • A specific subsidy pays a fixed amount per unit.
  • An ad valorem subsidy pays a percentage of the price.
  • Why governments use them:
    • lower prices of goods they want used more, e.g. public transport
    • support producers' incomes, e.g. farmers
    • raise output where there are external benefits
Mission 5

The subsidy diagram

  • Build it in steps (a subsidy of s per unit):
  • 1. Start at equilibrium: Pe, Qe.
  • 2. Supply shifts down by the subsidy: S → S − subsidy. Vertical gap = s.
  • 3. New equilibrium: the price buyers pay falls to P1; quantity rises to Q1.
  • Producers receive P1 + s per unit.
  • The price falls by less than the subsidy (unless demand is perfectly inelastic or supply perfectly elastic).
  • Say: "supply increases; there is an extension of demand" — not: "demand increases".
Mission 5

Who gains, and what it costs

  • Cost to the government = s × Q1: the rectangle between P1 + s and P1, from 0 to Q1.
  • That rectangle splits in two:
    • consumers' gain per unit = Pe − P1
    • producers' gain per unit = (P1 + s) − Pe
  • The two gains per unit add up to the subsidy, s.
  • This split is the incidence of the subsidy.
  • Say: "impact" for who receives the payment; "incidence" for who ends up with the benefit.

Worked example

  • Illustrative data. Before: Pe = £8, Qe = 60 units.
  • Specific subsidy: £3 per unit. After: consumers pay £6, Q1 = 70.
  • Producers receive £6 + £3 = £9.
  • Cost to government: £3 × 70 = £210.
  • Consumers' share: (£8 − £6) × 70 = £140.
  • Producers' share: (£9 − £8) × 70 = £70.
  • Consumers get 2/3: here demand is less elastic than supply (PED −0.67, PES 1.33 at the start).

?Illustrative data. Before a subsidy, a good sells at 10.00 per unit and 200 units are traded. After a subsidy of 4.00 per unit, consumers pay 7.00 and 260 units are traded. What is the total cost of the subsidy to the government?
Mission 5

Elasticity decides the split

  • The side of the market that is less price elastic gets more of the subsidy.
  • Inelastic demand: the price falls a lot → consumers gain most.
  • Elastic demand: the price barely falls → producers keep most.
  • Extreme cases:
    • perfectly inelastic demand: consumers get it all; Q does not change
    • perfectly elastic demand: price unchanged; producers get it all
  • More elastic demand and supply → a bigger rise in Q (and a bigger bill).
  • Approximate consumers' share = PES ÷ (PES + |PED|).
Mission 5Graph lab

Start: a subsidy of 2 per unit; PED −0.5, PES 1. Consumers get 2/3 of each unit's subsidy.

  • Takeaway: more elastic demand → the price falls less, so producers keep more (consumers' share 1/3) and Q rises more.
Quick check

Mission 5

Stakeholders

  • Consumers: pay less, buy more; consumer surplus rises.
  • Producers: receive more per unit, sell more; producer surplus rises.
  • Workers: more output can mean more jobs in the industry.
  • Government: pays s × Q1. Opportunity cost: that money cannot fund schools or hospitals, or must come from taxes or borrowing.
  • Producers may come to depend on the subsidy, with less pressure to cut costs.
Mission 5

Case: paying the buyer instead

  • From 2023 to 30 September 2025, US buyers of qualifying new electric cars could claim a federal tax credit of up to $7,500.
  • This subsidy went to buyers, so it shifted demand up, not supply.
  • The split is the same: it depends on elasticities, not on who receives the payment.
  • If car supply is inelastic, sellers can raise prices and keep much of the credit.
Mission 5Evaluate

Is the subsidy worth it?

  • It depends on elasticities: with elastic demand, the price barely falls and most of the money goes to producers.
  • It depends on information: to correct an external benefit, the subsidy should match its size. That is hard to measure.
  • It depends on the opportunity cost: the same money could fund other spending or lower taxes.
  • It depends on time and side effects: subsidies are hard to remove once firms and voters rely on them; firms may stay inefficient.
Mission check

?A small country's wheat farmers sell all their wheat at the world price, which they are too small to change. The government pays farmers a subsidy for every tonne they grow. Who gains the subsidy?

Farmers can sell any amount at the world price, so the price does not fall. They receive the world price plus the whole subsidy.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Equilibrium: the price where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied, so the market clears.
  • Above it: surplus (Qs − Qd), the price falls. Below it: shortage (Qd − Qs), the price rises. Both adjust by movements along the curves.

Mission 2

  • A shift creates excess demand or supply at the old price; the price then changes and the market moves along the other curve to a new equilibrium.
  • Demand shifts move price and quantity the same way; supply shifts move them opposite ways. Two shifts at once leave one result uncertain.
  • The less elastic the curves, the more the price moves.

Mission 3

  • At the social optimum MSB = MSC and total surplus is as large as possible: allocative efficiency.
  • Market failure (for example externalities and public goods) leaves output above or below that point, causing a welfare loss.

Mission 4

  • An indirect tax shifts supply up by the tax; the price rises by less than the tax and quantity falls.
  • The burden falls more on whichever side is less price elastic, whoever hands the money over.
  • Specific taxes shift S in parallel; ad valorem taxes pivot it.

Mission 5

  • A subsidy shifts supply down by the subsidy; the price falls by less than the subsidy and quantity rises.
  • The government pays s × Q1; the less elastic side of the market gets more of it.
