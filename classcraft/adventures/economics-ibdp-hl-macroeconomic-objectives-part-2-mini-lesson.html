Mission 1

Mission 1: If all pay rose as fast as prices, would inflation still hurt anyone?

Make a guess: If all pay rose as fast as prices, would inflation still hurt anyone?

Mission 1

Four words for changing prices

  • Inflation: a sustained rise in the general price level.
  • Deflation: a sustained fall in the price level. The inflation rate is negative.
  • Disinflation: the inflation rate falls but stays positive. Prices still rise, more slowly.
  • Hyperinflation: extremely rapid inflation (a common benchmark: over 50% a month). Money loses its value fast.
  • Say: "inflation fell from 4% to 2%: disinflation" — not: "prices fell".
Mission 1

Worked example: reading the inflation rate

  • Illustrative data. Consumer price index (CPI) at the end of each year:

  • Year 2: (260 − 250) ÷ 250 × 100 = 4.0%. Inflation.
  • Year 3: (265.2 − 260) ÷ 260 × 100 = 2.0%. The rate fell but prices still rose: disinflation.
  • Year 4: (262.5 − 265.2) ÷ 265.2 × 100 = −1.0%. Prices fell: deflation.

Mission 1

Expected or unexpected?

  • Nominal: measured in money. Real: adjusted for inflation (what the money buys).
  • Real interest rate ≈ nominal interest rate − inflation rate.
  • If inflation is fully expected, people build it in:
    • wages, pensions and benefits rise with prices
    • lenders set interest rates high enough to cover it
  • Then real incomes and real debts stay about the same.
  • If inflation is unexpected, contracts fixed in money terms no longer match prices. Real values change: wealth is redistributed.
  • Even fully expected inflation still has some costs.
  • Say: "a fall in real income" — not: "income fell" when money income rose more slowly than prices.
Mission 1

Worked example: who wins from a surprise?

  • Illustrative data. A bank lends 10,000 for one year at a fixed 5%. Repayment: 10,500.
  • Both sides expect 2% inflation. Expected real interest rate ≈ 5 − 2 = 3%.
  • Inflation turns out to be 7%.
  • Real interest rate ≈ 5 − 7 = −2%.
  • In start-of-year prices, 10,500 ÷ 1.07 = 9,813. The bank gets back less buying power than it lent.
  • Lender loses; borrower gains.
  • Also losing: savers on fixed rates, workers on fixed money wages, people on fixed money pensions.
  • Unexpected deflation works the other way: lenders gain, borrowers lose.

Mission 1

The costs of inflation

  • Menu costs: time and money spent changing prices (price lists, labels, websites).
  • Shoe-leather costs: time and effort spent holding less cash and moving money around to protect its value.
  • Uncertainty: high, variable inflation makes future costs and revenue hard to predict, so firms invest less. Growth can suffer.
  • Savings discouraged: if interest rates lag behind inflation, saving loses real value.
  • International competitiveness: if inflation is higher than in trading partners, exports get dearer and imports relatively cheaper (unless the exchange rate falls to offset it). The current account can worsen.
  • Inefficient resource allocation: when all prices rise fast, changes in relative prices are hard to spot, so price signals work less well.
  • Most costs grow with the rate of inflation and with how unpredictable it is.
Mission 1

Hyperinflation: when money stops working

  • At very high inflation, money fails at its jobs:
    • store of value: savings in cash are wiped out
    • medium of exchange: sellers refuse it and people turn to barter or foreign currency
    • unit of account: prices change too fast to compare
  • Real world: Zimbabwe's official annual inflation reached 231 million per cent in July 2008.
  • In 2009 Zimbabwe allowed foreign currencies such as the US dollar and the South African rand, and people stopped using its own currency.
  • Hyperinflations usually end only with a new currency or a new monetary regime (money growth).
Mission 1

Deflation: good news or bad?

  • Benign ("good") deflation: SRAS shifts right (higher productivity, cheaper commodities, new technology).
    • Price level falls, real output rises, unemployment falls.
  • Malign ("bad") deflation: AD shifts left (weak confidence, a credit crunch, a world recession).
    • Price level falls, real output falls, unemployment rises.
  • A fall in AD leaves a deflationary gap.
  • Most worries about deflation are about the malign kind.

Mission 1

The costs of deflation

  • Deferred consumption: if prices are expected to fall, households wait to buy. AD falls further.
  • Real value of debt rises: debts are fixed in money, but prices and incomes fall. Borrowers cut spending; some go bankrupt.
  • Unemployment: firms' revenue falls, but money wages are sticky downwards, so real wages rise and firms cut jobs (cyclical unemployment).
  • Policy ineffectiveness: interest rates cannot go far below zero, so real interest rates (≈ nominal − inflation) stay high when prices fall.
  • Uncertainty, redistribution (borrowers to lenders) and inefficient allocation also apply, as with inflation.
  • Deflationary spiral: falling prices → spending delayed → AD falls → output and jobs fall → prices fall further → …
  • Real world: US consumer prices fell by about a quarter from 1929 to 1933.
  • Real world: Japan's consumer prices fell in most years from 1999 to 2012, with interest rates close to zero.
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A firm owes 100,000 on a loan with no interest. Over a year, the price level falls by 10%, and so do the prices the firm charges. What happens to the real burden of the debt?
Mission 1

Which hurts more: unemployment or inflation?

  • Unemployment: lost output that is never recovered; the costs fall heavily on the jobless (lost income, skills, health).
  • Inflation: costs are spread across everyone; small at low, steady rates; large when high and volatile.
  • Survey evidence: one study of 12 European countries (Di Tella, MacCulloch and Oswald, 2001) found that a 1-point rise in unemployment lowered reported well-being about 1.7 times as much as a 1-point rise in inflation.
  • But very high inflation can damage growth and future jobs, so the answer depends on how high each one is.
  • Policy often faces a short-run trade-off between the two (see Mission 3).
Mission 1Evaluate

How much does inflation hurt?

It depends on:

  • The rate and its stability. Low, steady inflation costs little; high, volatile inflation costs a lot.
  • Whether it is expected. Expected inflation is built into wages and interest rates; surprises redistribute.
  • Indexation. Pensions, benefits and tax thresholds linked to prices protect people on fixed incomes.
  • Inflation abroad. What matters for competitiveness is inflation relative to trading partners, and the exchange rate.
  • Low inflation is not zero inflation. Many central banks target about 2%: a small positive rate lets real wages adjust without money pay cuts and leaves room above zero for interest rates.
Mission check

?Illustrative data. A family takes a one-year loan from a bank at a fixed 4% interest rate. Both sides expect 3% inflation. Instead, prices fall by 1% over the year. Who gains from the surprise?

What matters is the surprise: when prices do not match what contracts assumed, wealth moves between lenders and borrowers.

Mission 2

Mission 2: If a country's yearly deficit keeps shrinking, how can its debt still be growing?

Make a guess: If a country's yearly deficit keeps shrinking, how can its debt still be growing?

Mission 2

Three kinds of spending

  • Current spending: day-to-day running costs that are used up within the year. Salaries of teachers and nurses, medicines, office supplies.
  • Capital spending: investment in assets that last for years. New roads, rail lines, hospitals, school buildings.
  • Transfer payments: money paid to people with no good or service in return. Pensions, unemployment benefits, child benefit.
  • Transfers move income from taxpayers to recipients. They are not part of government spending (G) in AD, because no output is bought.
  • G is a component of AD. Capital spending can also raise LRAS over time (better infrastructure, more productive workers).
Mission 2

Where the money comes from, and what for

  • Direct taxes: on income and wealth, paid straight to the government. Income tax, corporation tax.
  • Indirect taxes: on spending, collected by sellers. VAT (sales tax), excise duties on fuel, alcohol and tobacco.
  • Also: sales of goods and services by state-owned enterprises (e.g. public transport fares), and sales of government assets (privatisation).
  • Goals of fiscal policy: low and stable inflation · low unemployment · a stable environment for long-term growth · smaller business cycle swings · a fairer income distribution · external balance.
Quick check
?Which of these is an example of government capital expenditure?
Mission 2

The budget balance

  • Budget balance = tax revenue − government spending (incl. transfers), over one year.
  • Deficit: spending > revenue. Surplus: revenue > spending. Balanced budget: equal.
  • Illustrative data (billion): tax revenue 900 · current spending 650 · capital spending 100 · transfers 250.
  • Spending = 650 + 100 + 250 = 1,000.
  • Budget balance = 900 − 1,000 = −100: a deficit of 100.
  • A deficit means the government injects more into the economy than it takes out in tax: other things equal, it adds to AD.
Mission 2

Deficit vs debt

  • Deficit is a flow: how much more the government spends than it raises in one year.
  • National debt is a stock: the total owed, built up from all past borrowing.
  • Each year's deficit is borrowed, so it is added to the debt. A surplus can be used to pay debt down.
  • Picture a bath: the deficit is the water flowing in this year; the debt is the water in the tub.
  • So a smaller deficit still adds to the debt, just more slowly. Paying the debt down needs a surplus.
  • Say: "the deficit fell, so the debt grew more slowly" — not: "the deficit fell, so the debt fell".
Quick check
?A government's deficit falls from 80 billion last year to 50 billion this year. Nothing else changes. What happens to the national debt this year?
Mission 2

Measuring the burden: debt as a % of GDP

  • Debt is compared with nominal GDP, because GDP is the income from which taxes are paid.
  • Debt-to-GDP ratio = (national debt ÷ nominal GDP) × 100.
  • Rough figures for gross government debt, 2023: Japan over 200% · Italy about 135% · US about 120% · UK about 100% · Germany about 62%.
  • Worked example (illustrative data, billion): debt 800 · nominal GDP 1,000 → ratio 80%.
  • Next year nominal GDP grows 5% to 1,050.

Mission 2

Why high debt can limit a government

Costs of a high debt:

  • Debt interest: money spent on interest cannot be spent on schools or health, or returned in lower taxes.
  • Credit ratings: agencies grade the risk that a government will not repay. A downgrade can raise the interest rate it must pay.
  • Example: in April 2010 Greece's rating was cut to "junk" by S&P. Its 10-year bond yields rose above 10%, and it needed EU and IMF loans.
  • Future taxes and spending: higher taxes or spending cuts may be needed later.
  • Less room to borrow in the next recession.
  • A sustainable debt is one the government can keep servicing (paying the interest and repaying loans as they fall due) without needing ever more borrowing. These costs are why debt is a constraint on fiscal policy.

Possible benefits of a deficit:

  • It supports AD and jobs in a recession.
  • Borrowing for capital spending can raise future output, which helps pay the interest.
Mission 2Evaluate

Is a deficit a problem?

It depends on:

  • Why it exists: a cyclical deficit falls as the economy recovers; a structural deficit does not.
  • What it pays for: capital spending adds assets that can raise productive capacity; most current spending pays for today's services.
  • Interest rate vs growth: if the interest rate on the debt is below the growth rate of nominal GDP, the debt ratio is easier to keep stable.
  • Who lends: domestic lenders, long-dated bonds and borrowing in the country's own currency usually reduce the risk of a sudden crisis.
  • Starting level and confidence: the same deficit worries lenders more when debt is already high.
  • Economists disagree on how high is too high. There is no single safe debt ratio.
Mission check

?Illustrative data: a government's national debt is 300 billion at the start of Year 1. Its budget balances are: Year 1, surplus of 20 billion · Year 2, surplus of 5 billion · Year 3, deficit of 15 billion. What is the national debt at the end of Year 3?

The deficit is the yearly flow; the debt is the stock. Every deficit adds to the debt, however small.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Can a country buy lower unemployment by accepting a little more inflation?

Make a guess: Can a country buy lower unemployment by accepting a little more inflation?

Mission 3

The original Phillips curve

  • In 1958, the economist A. W. Phillips plotted UK data from 1861 to 1957.
  • When unemployment was low, money wages rose fast. When unemployment was high, wages rose slowly.
  • Faster wage growth feeds into faster price rises, so the idea became a link between inflation and unemployment.
  • The curve slopes down: lower unemployment goes with higher inflation.
  • It is now called the short-run Phillips curve (SRPC).
  • Axes: inflation rate (%) up the side, unemployment rate (%) along the bottom.
Mission 3

Why a trade-off? Back to AD/AS

  • Step 1: AD rises. The economy moves up along SRAS.
  • Real output rises, so firms hire: unemployment falls.
  • The price level rises faster: inflation rises.
  • Step 2: the same change is a movement up along the SRPC.
  • A fall in AD does the reverse: down along SRAS, down along the SRPC.
  • Say: "a movement along the SRPC" — not: "a shift of the SRPC", when AD changes.

Mission 3

Real world: the trade-off holds, then breaks

  • 1950s–60s: UK data fitted the curve well. Many thought governments could choose a point on it.
  • 1970s: UK inflation averaged about 24% in 1975 (RPI), while unemployment also rose.
  • High inflation and high unemployment: stagflation.
  • Points no longer sat on one curve: the SRPC had shifted outwards.
  • Two reasons: oil-price supply shocks, and workers and firms coming to expect high inflation.
Mission 3

Expectations shift the short-run curve

  • Milton Friedman (1968) and Edmund Phelps (1967): the trade-off is only temporary.
  • Adaptive expectations: people expect inflation to be what it was recently.
  • (1) Point A: unemployment at its natural rate, inflation 2%, expected inflation 2%.
  • (2) AD rises. Workers' money wages rise, but prices rise faster than they expected. Real wages fall, firms hire: move along SRPC1 to B (lower unemployment, higher inflation).
  • (3) Workers now expect the higher inflation and bargain for higher money wages. Real wages recover, firms stop the extra hiring: unemployment returns to the natural rate at C. SRPC1 → SRPC2: each SRPC is drawn for one expected inflation rate.
  • Result: same unemployment, higher inflation.
Mission 3

The long-run Phillips curve

  • Join points A and C: the long-run Phillips curve (LRPC) is vertical at the natural rate of unemployment.
  • In the long run there is no trade-off: pushing unemployment below the natural rate only raises inflation.
  • The natural rate of unemployment: unemployment when the labour market is in equilibrium. It is frictional plus structural unemployment.
  • NAIRU: the non-accelerating inflation rate of unemployment. Below it, inflation keeps rising. It is often treated as the same as the natural rate.
  • Vertical LRPC = vertical LRAS: the classical (neo-classical) view.
Mission 3

Worked example: chasing low unemployment

  • Illustrative data. Natural rate = 5%. Expected inflation starts at 2%.
  • Short-run rule: inflation = expected inflation + (5 − unemployment rate).
  • Year 1: government holds unemployment at 3%. Inflation = 2 + 2 = 4%.
  • Year 2: people now expect 4%. Inflation = 4 + 2 = 6%.
  • Year 3: expect 6%. Inflation = 6 + 2 = 8%.
  • Each year the SRPC shifts up. Holding unemployment below the NAIRU makes inflation accelerate.
  • Stop pushing: unemployment returns to 5% with inflation stuck at 8%.
  • Bringing inflation back down needs unemployment above 5% for a time: at 7%, inflation = 8 − 2 = 6%.

Mission 3

Along the curve, or a shift?

  • Change in AD (demand shock): movement along the SRPC.
  • Supply shock (e.g. a sharp rise in oil or energy prices): SRAS shifts left, so the SRPC shifts right (up). Higher inflation at every unemployment rate.
  • A favourable supply shock (cheaper energy, faster productivity growth) shifts the SRPC left (down).
  • A change in expected inflation shifts the SRPC.
  • A change in the natural rate shifts the LRPC. Better skills matching, job search help or more flexible labour markets move it left. Long spells of unemployment that erode skills (hysteresis) can move it right.
Quick check
?A country invests heavily in solar and wind power. Over several years, firms' energy costs fall sharply. Other things equal, what happens on a Phillips curve diagram?
Mission 3Evaluate

How useful is the Phillips curve?

It depends on:

  • Time horizon: a short-run trade-off may exist.
  • In the long run, if expectations adapt, it disappears.
  • Supply shocks: they shift the SRPC, so past data may not predict the future.
  • Measurement: the NAIRU cannot be observed. It is estimated, and it changes over time.
  • Credible inflation targets: if people trust the central bank, expectations stay near target. The curve can look flat.
  • UK evidence: in 2019 unemployment was 3.8%, yet CPI inflation was only 1.8%.
  • Global factors (cheap imports, energy prices) also drive inflation, not just domestic unemployment.
Mission check

?A sudden rise in world energy prices hits an economy. Over a year, inflation rises from 2% to 6% and unemployment rises from 4% to 5%. Which best describes this on a Phillips curve diagram?

A lower-unemployment, higher-inflation deal exists only along one short-run curve. Supply shocks can shift that curve.

Mission 4

The big four objectives

ObjectiveWhat "success" looks likeWhy it matters
Economic growthSteady, sustainable rise in real GDPHigher living standards; more tax revenue
Low unemploymentFew people who want work are without itLost output; lost income; poverty and poor health
Price stabilityLow and stable inflationUncertainty and lost spending power when inflation is high or erratic
Balance of paymentsA sustainable current account positionA large deficit relies on borrowing from, or selling assets to, the rest of the world
  • Many central banks target inflation of about 2%.
  • Say: "low and stable inflation" — not: "falling prices". Deflation is not the goal.
Mission 4

More objectives

  • Environment / sustainability: growth that does not run down natural resources or harm future generations.
  • Greater equality: a fairer distribution of income and wealth (redistribution).
  • Objectives can support each other: growth creates jobs and raises tax revenue.
  • But they can also conflict: a trade-off means getting more of one costs some of another.
Quick check
?Illustrative data for one economy this year: real GDP growth 2.1%, unemployment 4.0%, inflation 6.5% (target 2%), current account deficit 1% of GDP. Which objective is furthest from being met?
Mission 4

Conflict: low unemployment vs low inflation

  • (1) AD1 → AD2 in a slump: real output rises a lot, the price level a little. Unemployment falls.
  • (2) AD2 → AD3 near full capacity: real output rises a little, the price level a lot.
  • Near capacity, firms compete for scarce workers: wages and prices rise.
  • So pushing unemployment ever lower tends to raise inflation. Cooling inflation tends to raise unemployment.
  • This is the short-run trade-off shown by the Phillips curve (see Mission 3).
Mission 4

Conflict: growth vs inflation and the current account

  • Demand-led growth faster than potential output opens a positive output gap: inflation rises.
  • This is also called an inflationary gap.
  • Supply-led growth (LRAS shifts right) raises output without adding to inflation.

Mission 4

Conflict: growth vs the environment

  • More output usually means more energy and raw materials, more emissions and more waste.
  • These are negative externalities: the costs fall on third parties and future generations.
  • Growth that uses up non-renewable resources may not be sustainable.
  • But the link can weaken: UK territorial greenhouse gas emissions fell by about 50% between 1990 and 2022, while real GDP grew.
  • Caution: part of that fall came from making less at home and importing more. Emissions measured by what the UK consumes fell by less.
  • Green technology, carbon taxes and regulation aim to decouple growth from emissions. How far that can go is debated.
Mission 4

Conflict: growth vs equity

  • Growth may reward some groups more: owners of capital, highly skilled workers, booming regions.
  • So inequality can widen even as average incomes rise.
  • Redistribution (progressive taxes, benefits) can narrow the gap.
  • One view: high taxes and generous benefits reduce incentives to work and invest, so equity costs some efficiency (Okun, 1975).
  • Another view: IMF research (Ostry, Berg and Tsangarides, 2014) found lower inequality was linked to faster and more durable growth, and redistribution was generally not harmful to growth except in extreme cases.
  • Which effect dominates depends on the policy design and the country.
Mission 4

Can conflicts be reconciled?

  • Supply-side policies (education, training, infrastructure, competition) shift LRAS right.
  • Then growth and falling unemployment come with less inflation.
  • But supply-side policies take years to work.
  • Policy mix: use different tools for different goals, e.g. interest rates for inflation, supply-side policies for growth.
  • Prioritise: tackle the most pressing problem first, accepting a cost elsewhere for a time.
Quick check
?A government wants faster growth without higher inflation. Which policy is most likely to achieve both over time?
Mission 4Evaluate

Which objective should come first?

It depends on:

  • Where the economy is: a deep slump makes jobs the priority; a positive output gap makes inflation the priority.
  • The cause: a demand shock and a supply shock call for different responses.
  • Time horizon: short-run trade-offs may ease in the long run if capacity grows.
  • Value judgements: how much inflation is worth a lower jobless rate, or how much growth is worth more emissions, is normative. Economists can show the trade-off; voters and governments choose.
Mission check

?An economy is already close to full capacity. The government cuts income tax to give growth a boost. Which outcome is most likely in the short run?

Pushing demand when capacity is nearly used up trades one objective (growth) against another (low inflation).

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Inflation is a rising price level; disinflation is a falling positive rate; deflation is a falling price level.
  • Unexpected inflation moves wealth from lenders, savers and people on fixed incomes to borrowers; unexpected deflation does the reverse.
  • Inflation also brings menu and shoe-leather costs, uncertainty and weaker competitiveness; malign deflation can spiral into falling output and jobs.

Mission 2

  • Current, capital and transfer spending. Transfers are not part of G.
  • Deficit = flow (one year); debt = stock (all past borrowing). A smaller deficit still adds to the debt.
  • The burden is measured as debt ÷ nominal GDP; the ratio can fall even when the debt rises.

Mission 3

  • The short-run Phillips curve slopes down: a rise in AD lowers unemployment and raises inflation.
  • Supply shocks shift the SRPC; changes in AD move the economy along it.
  • With adaptive expectations, the SRPC shifts up whenever unemployment is held below the natural rate. The LRPC is vertical at the natural rate (NAIRU).

Mission 4

  • Main objectives: growth, low unemployment, low and stable inflation, a sustainable current account; also budget, environment and equality.
  • Near full capacity, pushing unemployment down tends to push inflation up.
  • Demand-led growth risks inflation; growth may harm the environment and widen inequality.
  • Supply-side policies can ease conflicts, but slowly. Choosing priorities involves value judgements.
