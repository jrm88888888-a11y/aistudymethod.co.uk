Mission 1

Three questions

  • A friend asks you to lie to cover for them. Three ways to decide:
  • What is my duty? Some acts are right or wrong in themselves. → Deontological (Greek deon, "duty").
  • What will happen? Rightness depends on the outcome or purpose. → Teleological (Greek telos, "end, purpose") or consequentialist.
  • What kind of person should I be? Ethics is about character, not single acts. → Character-based (virtue ethics).
  • These are normative theories: they say how we ought to decide.
Mission 1

Deontological approaches

  • Focus on the act and the rule or duty behind it, not the result.
  • Kant (1724–1804): act from duty, according to the good regardless of consequences.
  • Divine command theory: right is what God commands.
  • Strength: clear duties; rights cannot be traded away. Weakness: rules can clash, and keeping them may cause harm.
Mission 1

Teleological approaches

  • Focus on the end: an act is right if it produces the best outcome.
  • Utilitarianism (Bentham, Mill): the greatest happiness or utility (see Mission 2).
  • Situation ethics (Joseph Fletcher, 1905–1991, Situation Ethics: The New Morality, 1966): the most loving outcome. AQA labels it teleological.
  • Strength: flexible; takes real people and outcomes seriously. Weakness: results are hard to predict; may justify harming a minority.
Mission 1

Character-based approaches

  • Asks: what would a virtuous person do? Who am I becoming?
  • Aristotle (384–322 BCE), Nicomachean Ethics: virtues are learned by habit; the goal is eudaimonia (flourishing).
  • Strength: matches how moral life is lived over time. Weakness: gives less precise guidance in a dilemma.

Mission 1

Absolutism and relativism

  • Moral absolutism: some acts are always right or wrong, in every time, place and situation (e.g. "never kill the innocent").
  • Moral relativism: rightness depends on the situation, culture or outcome; there are no exceptionless rules.
  • Deontological theories are usually absolutist; teleological theories usually relativist.
  • But relativist theories may still have one fixed principle: utility for Bentham; agape (love) for Fletcher. The application varies, not the principle.
  • Edexcel names absolutism (natural moral law) and relativism (utilitarianism) as key concepts.
Mission 1Read the source

Customs and the king

Herodotus (c. 484–c. 425 BCE), Histories III.38, paraphrased:

  • King Darius asked some Greeks what price would make them eat their dead fathers. They said nothing could.
  • He asked the Callatians, who did eat their dead, what would make them burn their fathers, as Greeks did. They cried out at such a terrible thing.
  • Herodotus concludes, with the poet Pindar, that custom is king of all.
  • Cultural (descriptive) relativism: moral codes differ between cultures. Normative relativism: so no code is truer than another.

Show answer

Q2 Does the fact that cultures disagree prove there is no right answer?

Show answer

No: that is a further step, which absolutists reject. Both peoples honour their dead.

Mission 1

Legalism, antinomianism, situationism

  • Fletcher's three approaches to moral decisions (Situation Ethics, 1966):
  • Legalism: rigid, pre-set rules applied whatever the situation.
  • Edexcel names legalism, with absolutism, as a key concept in ethics.
  • Antinomianism (Greek, "against law"): no rules or principles at all; each decision made on the spot.
  • Situationism: the middle way: enter each situation with the wisdom of rules, but be ready to set them aside if agape demands it.
  • Fletcher's support: the lawyer's question and the Good Samaritan (Luke 10:25–37); Paul's hymn to love (1 Corinthians 13).

> "Love is patient and is kind. Love doesn't envy. Love doesn't brag, is not proud," (1 Corinthians 13:4, World English Bible, British Edition)

Mission 1

Mixed approaches

  • Not every theory fits one box.
  • Rule utilitarianism (Mill): judges by outcomes, but at the level of rules.
  • Proportionalism (Bernard Hoose, Proportionalism: The American Debate and its European Roots, 1987): keep natural law's rules, but break one when there is a proportionate reason.
  • Edexcel: proportionalism is a contemporary adaptation of natural moral law.
  • So "absolutist vs relativist" is often a spectrum, not two boxes.
Mission 1Diversity

Moral absolutes in an age of relativism

  • Catholic teaching: some acts are intrinsically evil whatever the intention or circumstances (John Paul II, Veritatis Splendor, 1993, §80). Cardinal Joseph Ratzinger (later Benedict XVI) warned of a "dictatorship of relativism" (homily, 18 April 2005).
  • Many Protestants: absolutes come from scripture (e.g. the Ten Commandments), interpreted with care.
  • Liberal Christians: one absolute, love, applied flexibly (Fletcher; J. A. T. Robinson, Honest to God, 1963).
  • Virtue responses: MacIntyre: modern moral argument is fragmented because we lost a shared tradition; Hauerwas: the Church is a community whose story forms character.
  • Secular views: some humanists defend universal human rights without religion; others accept cultural relativism.
Mission 1Evaluate

Absolutism or relativism?

  • Claim: An absolutist approach is better for moral decision-making than a relativist one.
  • For absolutism: Clear and consistent; protects the innocent and minorities (some acts are never allowed); allows moral criticism of other cultures (e.g. of slavery).
  • For relativism: Respects cultural difference; flexible in hard cases; takes outcomes and persons seriously (Fletcher's "people over rules").
  • Against absolutism: Rules clash (lie or betray?); can be cruel in extreme cases.
  • Against relativism: "Who decides?"; risks justifying anything; cannot say any culture was wrong.
  • Judgement criteria: Can there be moral progress without some absolutes? Is there a middle way between fixed rules and case-by-case judgement? Does it fit how people actually decide?

Mission 2

Judging by results

  • Teleological (from telos, end or goal): an act is judged by the goal it achieves.
  • Consequentialist: what makes an act right or wrong is its consequences, not the act in itself or the motive.
  • Utilitarianism: the right act is the one with the best consequences for happiness: the most pleasure and least pain overall.
  • Founded in its classic form by Jeremy Bentham (1748–1832), An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation (printed 1780, published 1789).
  • Contrast: deontological ethics (Kant, natural law) judge the act itself, by duty or rule.
Mission 2

Bentham's world

  • Enlightenment: morality and law should rest on reason and evidence, not tradition or religious authority.
  • Empiricism: Hume had grounded morals in human feelings of pleasure and approval.
  • The phrase "greatest happiness for the greatest numbers" had already appeared in Francis Hutcheson (1725); Bentham also credited Joseph Priestley and the Italian penal reformer Cesare Beccaria (1764).
  • Industrial Revolution Britain: growing cities, harsh punishments, a chaotic legal system that Bentham attacked in A Fragment on Government (1776).
  • Bentham wanted a science of morals and law that anyone could check.
  • Spec: social, political and cultural influences on the emergence of the theory.
Mission 2Read the source

Two sovereign masters

Bentham, An Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation (1789), chapter I, paragraph 1:

> "Nature has placed mankind under the governance of two sovereign masters, pain and pleasure. It is for them alone to point out what we ought to do, as well as to determine what we shall do."

  • Two claims: pleasure and pain determine what we do (a claim about human psychology), and point out what we ought to do (a moral claim).

Mission 2

The principle of utility

  • Utility: the tendency of an act to produce pleasure, benefit or happiness, or to prevent pain.
  • Principle of utility: approve an act if it tends to increase the happiness of those affected; disapprove if it tends to decrease it.
  • "The greatest happiness of the greatest number" (A Fragment on Government, 1776): the measure of right and wrong.
  • Hedonism: pleasure is the only thing good in itself; pain the only thing bad in itself. Happiness = pleasure and the absence of pain.
  • Equality: "everybody to count for one, nobody for more than one" (Bentham's rule, as Mill reports it).
  • Quantity only: kinds of pleasure do not differ in value. Bentham: push-pin (a children's game) is as valuable as poetry, if it gives as much pleasure.

Mission 2

The hedonic calculus

Bentham's way of measuring the pleasure or pain an act will cause (IPML, chapter IV), seven factors:

  • Intensity: how strong? · Duration: how long?
  • Certainty: how likely? · Propinquity (remoteness): how soon?
  • Fecundity: will it lead to more pleasure? · Purity: is it free from pain afterwards?
  • Extent: how many people are affected?
  • Bentham's own memory verse: "Intense, long, certain, speedy, fruitful, pure— / Such marks in pleasures and in pains endure."
Quick check
?Reading for pleasure tends to make further pleasures possible: new interests, friendships and skills. Which factor of the hedonic calculus does this describe?
Mission 2

Act utilitarianism

  • Act utilitarianism: judge each act, in its own situation, by its consequences. No act is right or wrong in itself.
  • So lying, or breaking a promise, can be right if it produces the greatest balance of pleasure over pain here.
  • Relativist: the right act is relative to the situation and its consequences; only the principle of utility is fixed.
  • Bentham applied it above all to law and government: a law is good if it increases the community's happiness.
Mission 2

Bentham the reformer

  • Law: punishment is itself a pain, so it is justified only if it prevents greater pain. Bentham attacked cruel and useless punishments.
  • Prisons: the Panopticon (1791), a design to make prisons reform rather than simply punish.
  • Democracy: wider voting rights and secret ballots, so government serves the happiness of all, not a privileged few.
  • Animals: "The question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?" (IPML, chapter XVII, footnote).
  • Private morality: an unpublished essay (c. 1785) argued that homosexual acts should not be punished, since they cause no harm.
  • Followers ("philosophical radicals") influenced the Reform Act 1832 and the Poor Law of 1834.
Mission 2Evaluate

Is Bentham's act utilitarianism a good guide?

  • Claim: Bentham's act utilitarianism is a good way to decide what is right.
  • For: Simple and practical; based on something everyone recognises (pleasure and pain); every person counts equally.
  • For: Looks at real consequences; secular, so usable by anyone; inspired real reforms (and includes animals).
  • Against: Can justify injustice: harming an innocent minority if the majority gain enough pleasure.
  • Against: Consequences are hard to predict; pleasures are hard to measure and compare; motives and rights seem to count for nothing.
  • Judgement criteria: Can pleasure be measured? Does justice for individuals matter more than totals? Is pleasure really the only good?
Mission 2Worked answer

Using the calculus

Task: explain how Bentham's hedonic calculus would decide whether a town should replace a park with a car park.

  • Point: Bentham would compare the pleasure and pain each option produces for everyone affected.
  • Apply the factors: extent (many shoppers vs local families), duration (permanent loss of green space), fecundity (exercise and community events lead to further pleasures), purity (pollution afterwards).
  • Develop: The option with the greater balance of pleasure over pain is right. No act is right in itself: this is act utilitarianism.
  • Link: The answer could change with the facts (no act is right or wrong in itself), which is both a strength (flexibility) and a weakness (uncertainty).

Mission 3

Mill and the "pig philosophy" objection

  • John Stuart Mill (1806–1873), raised by his father James Mill as a utilitarian; Bentham was a family friend.
  • Works: On Liberty (1859); Utilitarianism (magazine 1861, book 1863); The Subjection of Women (1869).
  • Critics mocked Bentham's theory as "a doctrine worthy only of swine": if all pleasures are equal, a pig's contentment is as good as a human life.
  • Mill keeps the greatest happiness principle, but argues that pleasures differ in quality, not only quantity.
Mission 3

Higher and lower pleasures

  • Higher pleasures: of the mind: intellect, imagination, feelings, moral sentiments (learning, art, friendship, doing good).
  • Lower pleasures: of the body: food, drink, sex, rest.
  • Higher pleasures are worth more in kind: a small amount can outweigh a large amount of lower pleasure.
  • Competent judges: the test is what people who have experienced both prefer.
  • Mill does not say lower pleasures are bad. They matter; they are simply not the best.
Mission 3Read the source

Socrates dissatisfied

Mill, Utilitarianism (1863), chapter 2:

> "It is better to be a human being dissatisfied than a pig satisfied; better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied."

  • Mill adds (paraphrase): if the fool or the pig thinks otherwise, it is because they know only their own side of the question.

Mission 3

The harm principle

On Liberty (1859), chapter 1:

> "…the only purpose for which power can be rightfully exercised over any member of a civilised community, against his will, is to prevent harm to others."

  • Society may not compel you for your own good: it may advise, persuade and warn.
  • Liberty of thought and speech, of tastes and pursuits, and of association.
  • Mill's example (paraphrase): you may stop someone stepping onto an unsafe bridge if there is no time to warn them; if they know the risk, the choice is theirs.
  • Why it is utilitarian: free people develop themselves and discover new truths, which increases happiness in the long run.

Mission 3

Mill the reformer

  • The Subjection of Women (1869): the legal subordination of women is wrong and holds back human happiness.
  • As an MP, Mill proposed votes for women in the debates on the Reform Act 1867 (defeated).
  • Defended freedom of speech, representative government and wider education.
  • Change in law and social attitudes: Mill's arguments are still used for decriminalising private conduct that harms no one else.
  • Spec: application of the theory in political and social reform.
Mission 3

Rules from experience

  • Objection to Bentham: there is no time to calculate before every act.
  • Mill (paraphrase): humanity has been learning the effects of actions throughout its history. Those lessons are stored in secondary principles (rules such as "do not lie", "keep promises").
  • Sailors do not calculate the stars before every voyage; they take the Nautical Almanac, already calculated (Utilitarianism, chapter 2).
  • So in normal life we follow the rules; the principle of utility settles conflicts between them.
Mission 3

Rule utilitarianism: strong and weak

  • Rule utilitarianism: an act is right if it follows a rule which, if generally followed, produces the greatest happiness.
  • Strong rule utilitarianism: never break a well-founded rule, even when breaking it would bring more happiness this time.
  • Weak rule utilitarianism: follow the rules normally, but break one in an exceptional case where obeying would clearly cause more harm.
  • Example: "do not lie" maximises trust in general. Strong: keep it always. Weak: may lie to save a life.
Quick check
?A doctor's rule is to keep patients' information confidential. Which describes a weak rule utilitarian doctor?
Mission 3

Was Mill a rule utilitarian?

  • Mill never uses the term. "Rule utilitarianism" was named in the 20th century; J. O. Urmson (1953) argued Mill held a version of it.
  • Others read Mill as an act utilitarian who used rules as rules of thumb.
  • Either way, Mill gives rules a far bigger place than Bentham.
Mission 3Evaluate

Is rule utilitarianism better than act utilitarianism?

  • Claim: Rule utilitarianism is a better basis for moral decisions than act utilitarianism.
  • For: Protects justice and rights (no punishing the innocent for the majority's pleasure); quicker; predictable; promotes trust and the common good.
  • Against: J. J. C. Smart (1956): if a rule should be broken whenever breaking it produces more happiness, rule utilitarianism collapses into act utilitarianism; if not, it is irrational "rule worship".
  • Against (higher pleasures): who decides what is "higher"? The ranking can look elitist and culturally biased.
  • Judgement criteria: Can rules be justified only by consequences? What about exceptional cases? Does it promote justice better?
Mission check

Mill changed utilitarianism twice: some pleasures are higher, and rules and liberty matter. Critics ask whether he is still a utilitarian at all.

Mission 4

Preference utilitarianism

  • Maximise the satisfaction of preferences (or interests), not pleasure: what people would choose, on reflection.
  • R. M. Hare (1919–2002), Moral Thinking (1981): put yourself in the place of everyone affected.
  • Peter Singer (b. 1946), Animal Liberation (1975), Practical Ethics (1979; 3rd edition 2011): equal consideration of interests.
  • Any being that can suffer has interests, so animals count. Favouring humans simply for being human is speciesism.
  • Respects choices that do not maximise pleasure (e.g. a person who prefers truth to comfort).
Mission 4

Negative and ideal utilitarianism

  • Negative utilitarianism: minimise suffering rather than maximise happiness. Karl Popper (1902–1994), The Open Society and Its Enemies (1945), argued that relieving suffering is the more urgent duty.
  • Objection (R. N. Smart, 1958): taken strictly, painlessly ending all life would remove all suffering.
  • Ideal utilitarianism: maximise goods other than pleasure too. G. E. Moore (1873–1958), Principia Ethica (1903): the greatest goods are friendship and the enjoyment of beauty. Hastings Rashdall (1858–1924) named the view (The Theory of Good and Evil, 1907).
  • Spec: "the development of the theory, including Preference, Negative and Ideal Utilitarianism".

Mission 4Read the source

Mill and the golden rule

Mill, Utilitarianism (1863; text of the 7th edition, 1879), chapter 2:

> "In the golden rule of Jesus of Nazareth, we read the complete spirit of the ethics of utility. To do as one would be done by, and to love one's neighbour as oneself, constitute the ideal perfection of utilitarian morality."

  • William Paley (1743–1805), The Principles of Moral and Political Philosophy (1785) (paraphrase): God wills the happiness of his creatures, so doing good to others in obedience to God is virtue: a theological utilitarianism.

Q1 Which two biblical commands does Mill link to utility?

Show answer

Q2 How does Paley ground utility differently from Bentham?

Show answer

Mission 4Diversity

Christian utilitarianism?

  • Compatible: concern for the neighbour's welfare; Jesus healing and feeding crowds; Fletcher's "agapeic calculus"; Paley's theological utilitarianism.
  • Incompatible: Paul rejects doing evil "that good may come" (Romans 3:8); some acts are wrong in themselves (Catholic teaching, Veritatis Splendor 1993); sanctity of life: every person has a worth that cannot be traded.
  • Different goal: Christian ethics aims at holiness and love of God, not only happiness in this life.
  • Many Protestant ethicists use consequences as one factor among others; Catholic moral theology rejects pure consequentialism.
Mission 4

Can pleasure be measured?

  • Problems: pleasures are subjective; different kinds cannot be put on one scale; consequences are hard to predict; how far ahead should we count?
  • Problems: who counts? Future generations, animals, people far away?
  • Replies: rough estimates are enough for most choices; rules store past experience (Mill's almanac); preferences can be asked about and compared.
  • Practice: modern health systems measure "quality-adjusted life years" to compare treatments: a utilitarian kind of measure.
Mission 4

Utilitarianism in today's world

  • Public policy: cost–benefit analysis in transport, health and climate decisions; triage in emergencies.
  • Effective altruism: Singer, The Life You Can Save (2009): give where it reduces the most suffering.
  • Law and social attitudes: Bentham and Mill's arguments shaped reforms on punishment, voting, women's rights and private conduct.

Mission 4Evaluate

Is utilitarianism a helpful method?

  • Claim: Utilitarianism provides a helpful method of moral decision-making.
  • For: Clear principle; impartial ("everybody to count for one"); takes real consequences seriously; usable by believers and non-believers alike; adaptable (rule, preference).
  • For: Has driven reforms widely seen as progress; extends concern to animals and distant strangers.
  • Against: Can justify injustice to minorities; ignores motives, rights and special duties (to family, promises).
  • Against: Measurement and prediction problems; no intrinsic wrongs: no act is wrong in itself, so any act could be permitted if the consequences are good enough; may encourage immoral acts "for the greater good".
  • Judgement criteria: Is pleasure (or preference) the only intrinsic good? Does it promote justice? Can it work in contemporary society? Is it compatible with religious ethics?
Mission 4Worked answer

Evaluating with a scholar

Task: "Utilitarianism cannot protect justice." Evaluate.

  • Line of argument: the claim is strong against act utilitarianism, weaker against rule utilitarianism.
  • Support: Bentham's calculus could approve punishing an innocent person if enough people gain.
  • Counter: Mill's rules (e.g. fair trials) maximise happiness generally; a weak rule utilitarian keeps them in almost all cases.
  • Critique of the counter: Smart: then either rules collapse into act utilitarianism, or they become "rule worship".
  • Judgement: decided by your own criteria (e.g. can rules be justified only by consequences?), stated and defended.
  • AO2: keep one line of argument from introduction to a justified conclusion.

Mission 5

Three ways to make a moral decision

  • Joseph Fletcher (1905–1991), American Episcopal priest and ethicist; Situation Ethics: The New Morality (1966).
  • Legalism: fixed rules decide in advance, for every case. Fletcher: it puts the law before people (his examples: some Pharisees; rigid Catholic and Protestant moral codes).
  • Antinomianism ("against law"): no rules or principles at all; each decision is spontaneous. Fletcher: it is unprincipled and chaotic.
  • Situationism: the middle way. Enter each situation with the moral rules of your community as guides, but set any of them aside if love is better served.
Mission 5

The "new morality" of the 1960s

  • After 1945, many in Britain and America questioned inherited authority: in church, family, sexual morality and law.
  • John A. T. Robinson (1919–1983), Bishop of Woolwich, Honest to God (1963), chapter 6, "The New Morality": Christians should be guided by love alone, not by a code handed down from above.
  • Robinson drew on Bonhoeffer, Tillich and Bultmann; he gave evidence for the defence in the 1960 Lady Chatterley's Lover trial.
  • Fletcher's book three years later turned the idea into a method.
  • Your spec names both scholars: J A T Robinson and J Fletcher. Social, political and cultural influences: the "permissive society", existentialism, and churches losing authority.
Mission 5

Agape: a love that is chosen

  • Greek has several words for love (the New Testament mainly uses agape and philia). C. S. Lewis, The Four Loves (1960), describes four: storge (affection), philia (friendship), eros (romantic love), agape (self-giving love; Lewis calls it "charity").
  • Agape is the love of God shown in Jesus: unconditional, self-giving, for every neighbour, including enemies.
  • Fletcher: agape is an act of the will, not a feeling. It is not the same as liking someone.
  • It always seeks the neighbour's good, and it can be "calculating": it thinks hard about consequences.
Mission 5Read the source

Asked what the law requires, a lawyer gives the two great commandments, ending "…and your neighbour as yourself." He asks, "Who is my neighbour?" Jesus tells of a Samaritan who cares for a wounded Jewish traveller after a priest and a Levite pass by (Luke 10:25–36). Then:

> He said, "He who showed mercy on him." Then Jesus said to him, "Go and do likewise." (Luke 10:37, World English Bible, British Edition)

> Love is patient and is kind. (1 Corinthians 13:4)

> But now faith, hope, and love remain—these three. The greatest of these is love. (1 Corinthians 13:13)

Mission 5

Jesus and Paul: situationists?

  • Sabbath: Jesus defends his hungry disciples for picking grain, citing David eating the holy bread (Mark 2:23–26; 1 Samuel 21:1–6).

> "The Sabbath was made for man, not man for the Sabbath." (Mark 2:27)

  • He heals on the Sabbath, asking whether it is lawful to do good or harm (Mark 3:1–6).
  • The whole law hangs on love of God and neighbour (Matthew 22:37–40).
  • Paul: "Love therefore is the fulfilment of the law." (Romans 13:10)
  • But: Jesus also says he came not to abolish the law but to fulfil it (Matthew 5:17), and makes some rules stricter (divorce, Mark 10:2–12). Whether Jesus was a situationist is disputed.

Mission 5

The boss principle and four working principles

  • Pragmatism: a moral choice must work in practice, towards love's goal.
  • Relativism: rules are relative to the situation; but always relative to love, which is not relative. ("Relative", not "anything goes".)
  • Positivism: love is accepted by faith, as a free commitment, not proved by reason ("theological positivism"). Christians start from "God is love" (1 John 4:8).
  • Personalism: people, not laws or things, are at the centre. Ask "who is helped?", not "what is the rule?"
Mission 5

The six propositions

Fletcher's six propositions (paraphrase):

1. Only love is intrinsically good; nothing else is good in itself.

2. Love is the ruling norm of Christian decision; it replaces all laws.

3. Love and justice are the same: justice is love distributed to everyone affected.

4. Love wills the neighbour's good, whether we like them or not.

5. Only the end (love) justifies the means.

6. Love decides situationally, not by rules prescribed in advance.

Quick check
?A situationist says: "Agape cannot be shared only with those close to me; it must be weighed out fairly among everyone affected." Which of Fletcher's propositions is this?
Mission 5

Conscience: a verb, not a noun

  • Fletcher rejects conscience as a thing (noun): an inner voice, a faculty, or a store of rules.
  • Conscience is what we do (verb): the work of making a decision, creatively, in a real situation.
  • It looks forward (deciding what to do), not mainly backward (guilt over what was done).
  • So there is no ready-made answer inside us; each person must work out what love requires.
Mission 5

What kind of theory?

  • Teleological: judges an act by its end (telos): love.
  • Consequentialist: what makes an act right is its loving outcome for the people involved.
  • Relativist: the right act varies with the situation, though love itself is absolute.
  • Christian: Fletcher presents it as Christian ethics; love is the one absolute, accepted by faith.
  • Unlike utilitarianism, the good to be maximised is agape, not pleasure (comparison in a later lesson).
Mission 5Worked answer

Explaining the theory

Task: explain why Fletcher saw situation ethics as a middle way.

  • Point: Fletcher rejected legalism (rules before people) and antinomianism (no principles at all).
  • Scholar and source: He made agape the only absolute (Situation Ethics, 1966), drawing on Jesus: "The Sabbath was made for man" (Mark 2:27).
  • Develop: Rules remain useful guides from the community's experience, but love may set them aside, as the Samaritan put the wounded man's need first (Luke 10:37).
  • Link: So the theory is relativist in method but has one fixed norm, which is why Fletcher calls it a middle way.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Deontological (duty, the act), teleological (end, consequences) and character-based (virtues) approaches ask different questions.
  • Absolutism: some acts always wrong; relativism: it depends. Many theories mix them (natural law's telos; rule utilitarianism; proportionalism).
  • Fletcher: legalism, antinomianism, and situationism as the middle way, guided by agape.

Mission 2

  • Utilitarianism is teleological and consequentialist: the right act produces the greatest happiness of the greatest number. For Bentham, pleasure is the only good, measured by quantity.
  • The hedonic calculus: intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity, fecundity, purity, extent. Act utilitarianism judges each act afresh (relativist).

Mission 3

  • Mill: pleasures differ in quality; higher (mental) pleasures outweigh lower (bodily) ones, by the verdict of competent judges.
  • Harm principle: power over a person is justified only to prevent harm to others. Rule utilitarianism (strong and weak) follows rules that maximise happiness; critics say it collapses into act utilitarianism or becomes rule worship.

Mission 4

  • Preference utilitarianism (Hare, Singer) satisfies interests, and animals count. Evaluation: measurement, prediction, justice for minorities, no act wrong in itself; rule and preference forms reply in part.
  • Christian utilitarianism: Mill's golden rule and Paley's theology; critics cite Romans 3:8, intrinsic wrongs and the sanctity of life.

Mission 5

  • Fletcher's situation ethics (1966) is a middle way between legalism and antinomianism; Robinson's "new morality" (1963) is its setting.
  • Agape (self-giving love, willed not felt) is the only absolute: the boss principle; the four working principles are pragmatism, relativism, positivism and personalism; the six propositions explain love's role.
  • Conscience is a verb: the act of deciding. Fletcher drew on Jesus (Luke 10; Mark 2:27) and Paul (1 Corinthians 13; Romans 13:10).
