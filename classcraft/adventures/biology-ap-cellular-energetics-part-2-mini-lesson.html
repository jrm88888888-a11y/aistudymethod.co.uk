Mission 1

Mission 1: Two animals look almost identical. How can biologists tell whether they are truly close relatives?

Make a guess: Two animals look almost identical. How can biologists tell whether they are truly close relatives?

Mission 1

Same bones, different jobs

  • Homologous structures: the same basic structure, used for different functions.
  • Pentadactyl limb: one upper bone, two lower bones, a group of wrist bones, five digits.
  • Found in a human arm, a bat wing, a whale flipper.
  • No single job needs this layout. It was inherited from a common ancestor and modified.
  • Vestigial structures: reduced remnants of structures that worked in ancestors, e.g. tiny hip bones in whales. They are homologies too.
Mission 1

Same job, different origins

  • Analogous structures: the same function, different evolutionary origins.
  • They arise by convergent evolution: similar selection pressures favor similar forms in unrelated lineages.
  • Examples: streamlined body and fins of a shark (fish) and a dolphin (mammal); wings of birds and insects.
  • Say: "convergent: features became alike" — not: "the species are merging".
Quick check
?A hypothetical species of burrowing lizard has tiny limb bones under its skin that are not used for movement. Closely related lizard species that live above ground have fully formed legs with the same bones. Which reasoning best connects these observations to common ancestry?

  • Say: "P and Q share a more recent common ancestor" — not: "P evolved from Q".

?A biologist hypothesizes that a hypothetical rodent species, X, is more closely related to species Y than to species Z. Which investigation would best test this hypothesis?
Mission 1

Shared by all life

  • The genetic code is almost universal: the same codons code for the same amino acids in bacteria, plants and animals.
  • The code is not the only one possible: slightly different codes work (e.g. in mitochondria). Sharing one code is best explained by common ancestry of all life.
  • So a copy of a human gene, with its introns removed, can be put into a bacterium, and the bacterium makes a chain with the same amino acid sequence.
  • Core metabolic pathways, e.g. glycolysis and oxidative phosphorylation, are conserved across Archaea, Bacteria and Eukarya.
  • All eukaryotes share membrane-bound organelles, linear chromosomes and genes containing introns: evidence of one eukaryote common ancestor.
Quick check
?Biologists discover a single-celled organism whose cells contain a nucleus, mitochondria and an endoplasmic reticulum, each enclosed by membranes. Which conclusion is best supported?
Mission check

?The marsupial mole of Australia and the golden moles of southern Africa look remarkably alike: tiny or hidden eyes, no external ears and spade-like front claws for digging. DNA comparisons place the marsupial mole closest to other Australian marsupials, and the golden moles closest to other African placental mammals. Which conclusion is best supported?

Looks can converge. Inherited structure and molecular sequences show who is really related.

Mission 2

Mission 2: How can we trust the evidence that living things have changed over time?

Make a guess: How can we trust the evidence that living things have changed over time?

Mission 2

Fossils in order

  • Fossils: preserved remains or traces of organisms, usually in sedimentary rock.
  • Undisturbed layers: lower = older. So fossils show the order in which life forms appeared.
  • Simpler forms appear earlier; transitional forms link groups, e.g. Tiktaalik, part fish, part four-limbed animal.
  • The record is incomplete: fossilization is rare, and soft bodies seldom fossilize.
  • Evidence comes from many fields: geological, geographical, physical, biochemical and mathematical data.
  • Continuous change in the fossil record and genomic change over time show that all species have evolved and continue to evolve.
  • Like any evidence, fossil finds are trusted only after other scientists check them: peer review before publication, then independent repeats.
Mission 2

Worked example: dating a fossil

Three ways to date a fossil:

  • Age of the rock: volcanic layers above and below are dated with isotopes of long half-life. The fossil lies between those ages.
  • Carbon-14: half-life about 5,730 years; useful for remains up to about 50,000 years old.
  • Geographical data, e.g. the position of continents when the rock formed.

A bone has 12.5% of the carbon-14 it had at death.

  • 100% → 50% → 25% → 12.5%: 3 half-lives.
  • Age ≈ 3 × 5,730 = 17,190 years: about 17,000 years.
Quick check
?Carbon-14 has a half-life of about 5,730 years. A paleontologist finds a fossil bone in rock layers that are about 70 million years old. Which statement about dating this fossil is correct?
Mission 2

Selective breeding: evolution you can watch

  • Artificial selection: humans choose individuals with desired heritable traits and breed only from them, generation after generation.
  • Wild cabbage (Brassica oleracea) gave cabbage, kale, broccoli, cauliflower, kohlrabi and Brussels sprouts in a few thousand years.
  • All domestic dogs descend from wolves.
  • Selecting a narrow set of traits, often by mating relatives, reduces genetic variation in the breed.
  • Breeders cannot create new alleles. They select among variation that already exists.
Quick check
?Breeders of a dog breed mate only dogs that closely match a strict breed standard, and they often mate close relatives. Compared with a large population of mixed-breed dogs, which outcome is expected for the breed?
Quick check
?For many generations, farmers have propagated a hypothetical crop variety from cuttings of a few selected plants, so the plants in their fields are nearly genetically identical. A new fungal disease arrives in the region. Which prediction is best supported?
Mission check

?Whales were once thought to be most closely related to an extinct group of hoofed carnivores. DNA comparisons then placed whales closest to hippopotamuses, among the even-toed hoofed mammals. Soon after, fossils of early whales were found with an ankle bone of a shape found only in even-toed hoofed mammals. Why does this agreement strengthen confidence in the relationship?

No single fossil or sequence settles it. Confidence comes from independent evidence that keeps agreeing.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Burning sugar releases all its energy in one flash. How do your cells release it slowly enough to use?

Make a guess: Burning sugar releases all its energy in one flash. How do your cells release it slowly enough to use?

Mission 3

Why every cell respires

  • Respiration releases energy from carbon compounds and transfers it to ATP.
  • Cellular respiration uses energy from biological macromolecules to make ATP.
  • It runs in every living cell, day and night: animals, plants, fungi, bacteria.
  • Autotrophs respire too. Photosynthesis makes their sugar; respiration releases its energy.
  • Main substrates: glucose and fatty acids. Many other carbon compounds can be used.
  • Not the same as gas exchange. Breathing moves gases; respiration is a chemical process inside cells.
  • Respiration and fermentation are found in all forms of life.
  • Say: "respiration releases energy" — not: "respiration produces energy".
Quick check
?Which statement best describes what cellular respiration does in a cell?
Mission 3

Small steps, not one flash

  • Overall (aerobic): glucose + oxygen → carbon dioxide + water.
  • Burning releases the energy all at once, as heat and light.
  • Respiration releases the same total energy, but in many small steps.
  • Each step is catalyzed by its own specific enzyme, inside the cell.
  • At some steps, part of the energy is captured in ATP. The rest is released as heat.
  • Say: "energy is transferred to ATP" — not: "glucose is turned into energy".
Mission 3

Two routes from glucose

  • First stage, glycolysis: glucose → 2 pyruvate, in the cytoplasm. No oxygen needed.
  • With oxygen: pyruvate enters the mitochondria and is oxidized completely to carbon dioxide and water. Far more ATP.
  • In the mitochondria: pyruvate oxidation and the Krebs cycle, then the electron transport chain and oxidative phosphorylation.
  • Without O2: fermentation in the cytosol turns pyruvate into lactic acid (muscle) or alcohol and CO2 (yeast).
Mission 3

Glycolysis

  • Glycolysis takes place in the cytosol and uses no O2.
  • It releases energy in glucose to form ATP (from ADP + Pᵢ), NADH (from NAD⁺) and pyruvate.
  • It is a series of enzyme-catalyzed steps.
  • It is found in organisms of all three domains: Archaea, Bacteria and Eukarya.
  • A core pathway shared by every domain is evidence of common ancestry.
Quick check
?Which statement correctly describes glycolysis?
Mission check

?Yeast cells are growing in a glucose solution with no O2, producing ethanol and CO2. A compound is added that blocks one of the reactions of glycolysis. Which prediction is best supported?

Each enzyme-catalyzed step depends on the one before. Block one, and the energy stops flowing.

Mission 4

Mission 4: A plant takes in carbon dioxide and water and gives out oxygen. Which of the two did the oxygen come from?

Make a guess: A plant takes in carbon dioxide and water and gives out oxygen. Which of the two did the oxygen come from?

Mission 4

Photosynthesis in one line

  • carbon dioxide + water → glucose + oxygen
  • 6CO2 + 6H2O → C6H12O6 + 6O2 (light energy, absorbed by chlorophyll)
  • CO2 + H2O + light energy → carbohydrate + O2. Sugars are used in the plant's own cells or stored.
  • Light energy becomes chemical energy in the carbon compounds made.
  • Energy is transformed, not created.
  • Say: "photosynthesis transforms light energy" — not: "plants make energy".
Mission 4

Water is split for its hydrogen

  • CO2 has no hydrogen. Glucose has 12 H atoms. They come from water.
  • Light energy is used to split water.
  • Its hydrogen (as protons and electrons) reduces CO2 to glucose.
  • The oxygen left over is a by-product: it is released.
  • CO2's carbon and oxygen end up in glucose and in some new water, not in the O2.
  • Plants, algae and cyanobacteria (prokaryotes) all split water and release O2.
  • Say: "the oxygen released comes from water" — not: "the plant keeps the carbon of CO2 and releases its oxygen".

Mission 4

Inside a chloroplast

  • Envelope: outer and inner membrane.
  • Thylakoids: flattened membrane sacs. A stack is a granum (plural grana).
  • Stroma: the fluid around the thylakoids, inside the inner membrane.
  • Thylakoid membranes hold chlorophyll and electron carriers.
  • The chlorophyll is organized into two photosystems.
  • Light-dependent stage: thylakoid membranes. Light-independent stage: stroma.
  • Also called: light reactions in the grana; Calvin cycle (carbon fixation) in the stroma.
  • Chlorophyll absorbs light energy, boosting electrons to a higher energy level in both photosystems.
Quick check
?Electron micrographs of chloroplasts show grana. Which statement correctly describes grana?
Mission 4

It began in prokaryotes

  • Photosynthesis first evolved in prokaryotes, long before eukaryotic cells.
  • Cyanobacteria split water and released O2. Rocks show O2 building up when the only cells on Earth were prokaryotes.
  • This produced Earth's oxygenated atmosphere.
  • Chloroplasts descend from an engulfed cyanobacterium. So prokaryotic pathways were the foundation of eukaryotic photosynthesis.
Quick check
?A student claims that photosynthesis by cyanobacteria, which are prokaryotes, produced the oxygenated atmosphere of early Earth. Which of the following provides the best support for this claim?
Mission check

?Chloroplasts from a hypothetical alga are broken open and separated into two fractions: the thylakoid membranes, and the stroma with no thylakoids. Each fraction is placed in the light with water, ¹⁴C-labeled CO2, ADP, inorganic phosphate, and a dye that accepts electrons in place of NADP. No ATP or reduced NADP (NADPH) is added. Which results are predicted?

The O2 comes from water split in the thylakoid membranes; the carbon from CO2 is built into sugar in the stroma.

Mission 5

Mission 5: A leaf splits water to get electrons. Where do those electrons end up, and what do they make on the way?

Make a guess: A leaf splits water to get electrons. Where do those electrons end up, and what do they make on the way?

Mission 5

Photosystems catch light

  • Thylakoid membranes hold two photosystems: photosystem II (PSII) and photosystem I (PSI).
  • Each is an array of chlorophyll and accessory pigments, with a special chlorophyll a at the reaction center.
  • Absorbed energy is passed to the reaction center.
  • There an electron is raised to a higher energy level and emitted to an electron acceptor.
  • Say: "light excites an electron, which the chlorophyll loses" — not: "chlorophyll makes electrons".
Mission 5

The electron path: PSII → PSI → NADP

1. Light excites PSII. Its electron passes to an electron transport chain (ETC).

2. Carrier to carrier: each step is a redox reaction that releases energy.

3. The electron reaches PSI and is excited again by light.

4. It passes to NADP in the stroma.

  • You need not name the carriers in the chain.
Mission 5

Photolysis refills PSII

  • PSII has lost electrons. It must get them back.
  • Water is split: 2H2O → 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ + O2. This is photolysis.
  • Electrons replace those lost from PSII.
  • Protons (H⁺) stay inside the thylakoid: they add to the proton gradient.
  • Oxygen is a by-product (waste): it diffuses out of the leaf.
  • Say: "splitting water supplies electrons" — not: "splitting water supplies the energy". The energy comes from light.
Quick check
?During the light reactions, water molecules are split. What is the role of this process?
Mission 5

Reducing NADP

  • At the end of the chain, PSI passes two electrons to NADP, on the stroma side.
  • NADP also takes a hydrogen ion from the stroma: NADP is reduced to reduced NADP.
  • NADP⁺ + 2e⁻ + H⁺ → NADPH.
  • Reduced NADP and ATP carry the energy to the light-independent stage.
Mission 5

Chemiosmosis makes ATP

  • Energy released along the ETC pumps H⁺ from the stroma into the thylakoid space.
  • Photolysis adds more H⁺ inside; NADP reduction removes H⁺ outside.
  • Result: high [H⁺] inside, low outside: an electrochemical gradient.
  • H⁺ flow back to the stroma through ATP synthase. This drives ADP + Pᵢ → ATP, released into the stroma.
  • ATP made this way: photophosphorylation. The mechanism: chemiosmosis.
  • ETCs that build H⁺ gradients also run in mitochondria and across prokaryote plasma membranes.
  • Say: "ATP synthase is driven by H⁺ flowing down their gradient" — not: "the carriers make ATP".
Quick check
?Which statement best explains how the high concentration of H⁺ inside the thylakoid is established during the light reactions?
Quick checkRead the data
?In illuminated chloroplasts of a hypothetical alga, the pH of the thylakoid space was 5.0 and the pH of the stroma was 8.0. What is the H⁺ concentration in the thylakoid space as a multiple of the H⁺ concentration in the stroma?
Mission check

?A chemical is added to illuminated chloroplasts. It makes the thylakoid membrane freely permeable to H⁺ but does not affect the photosystems, the electron transport chain, or ATP synthase. Which prediction is best supported?

The electrons end on NADP⁺, making NADPH; on the way, their energy builds the H⁺ gradient that ATP synthase uses.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Homologous structures share an origin but differ in function; analogous structures share a function but arose separately by convergent evolution.
  • Fewer differences in DNA or protein sequences mean a more recent common ancestor.
  • A near-universal genetic code points to common ancestry of all life.

Mission 2

  • Fossils in rock layers show the order in which life changed.
  • Rock age, carbon-14 and geographical data date fossils.
  • Selective breeding changes heritable characteristics fast, showing evolution can be rapid; it reduces genetic variation.

Mission 3

  • Respiration releases the energy in carbon compounds in many small, enzyme-catalyzed steps; part is captured in ATP, the rest is heat.
  • Glycolysis in the cytosol forms ATP, NADH and pyruvate without O2, in organisms of all three domains.

Mission 4

  • Photosynthesis transforms light energy into chemical energy: water is split, its hydrogen reduces CO2 to sugar, and its oxygen is released.
  • Light-dependent stage (light reactions): thylakoid membranes. Light-independent stage (Calvin cycle): stroma.

Mission 5

  • Light excites electrons in PSII and PSI; they pass along an electron transport chain to NADP. Water splitting replaces PSII's electrons and releases O2.
  • Energy from the ETC builds an H⁺ gradient into the thylakoid space; H⁺ flow through ATP synthase makes ATP (photophosphorylation). Products: ATP and NADPH.
