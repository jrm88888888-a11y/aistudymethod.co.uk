Learning focus

Moral theory: virtue ethics

Virtue ethics asks not 'what should I do?' but 'what kind of person should I become?'

  • its origins in Aristotle — the virtues as a mean between extremes, aimed at eudaimonia (flourishing);
  • its modern development in the work of Anscombe, Foot, MacIntyre and Hauerwas; and
  • the challenge of ethical relativism and the Christian response.

G. E. M. Anscombe's 1958 essay is often credited with reviving virtue ethics in the twentieth century.

Learning focus

Free will and determinism

Moral responsibility depends on whether we are free:

  • Free will — its nature, and compatibilist vs incompatibilist views;
  • Determinism — the principle of causality, hard and soft determinism, internal and external causation; and
  • the influence of psychology, environment and genetics on moral choices.

Candidates evaluate the implications for moral responsibility and the Christian response.

Learning focus

Global rights

The nature of human rights is contested:

  • Christian and secular perspectives;
  • the contribution of Hobbes, Locke and Paine, and the sceptical views of Bentham;
  • the Universal Declaration of Human Rights (UDHR);
  • cultural and religious critiques; and
  • the issue of animal rights.

Bentham famously dismissed natural rights as 'nonsense upon stilts', while Locke grounded rights in life, liberty and property.

Learning focus

Sexual identity and gender justice

The specification treats several rights issues, to be studied across a spectrum of religious and secular perspectives:

  • same-sex relationships, and rights around marriage and parenthood;
  • the rights of women;
  • birth control and contraception; and
  • violations of rights, including female genital cutting and honour killings, and feminist responses.

These are sensitive topics; the exam expects balanced, well-evidenced evaluation of a range of views.

Learning focus

Global issues: war and peace

Contemporary warfare raises major ethical questions. The Just War tradition distinguishes:

  • Jus ad Bellum — the right to go to war;
  • Jus in Bello — right conduct within war; and
  • Jus post Bellum — justice after war.

Candidates also weigh non-combatant immunity, the challenge of weapons of mass destruction (WMD), and pacifism.

Learning focus

Justice, law and punishment

The specification lists the aims of punishment: protection, retribution, rehabilitation, deterrence and vindication. Candidates study:

  • religious perspectives, including biblical and various Christian views;
  • ethical perspectives from natural moral law, utilitarianism and virtue ethics;
  • the issue of capital punishment and diminished responsibility; and
  • the role of compassion and mercy.

Synoptic theme: Conscience, Freedom and Tolerance

The A2 synoptic assessment theme draws the unit together:

  • the nature and role of conscience, religious and secular views, and the notion of moral duty;
  • the role of the state — protecting the common good and personal liberty, and prisoners of conscience; and
  • the capacity for religion to promote both tolerant and intolerant attitudes.

Like all synoptic themes, this connects with at least one other unit you study.
