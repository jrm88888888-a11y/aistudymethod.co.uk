Mission 1

Basic needs: food, water and sanitation

  • 2024: 3.4 billion people lacked safely managed sanitation; 354 million practised open defecation.
  • Safely managed sanitation: WHO African Region 27%, Europe 80%.
  • 23.2% of children under five were stunted in 2024.
  • Without sanitation, faeces reach water; diarrhoea and undernutrition then feed each other and kill infants. Skilled care in childbirth cuts maternal deaths.

Mission 1

Classifying disease

  • Infectious (caused by a pathogen: virus, bacterium, parasite) v non-infectious (e.g. diabetes).
  • Communicable: passes between people, directly or by a vector or water. Noncommunicable (NCD): does not.
  • Contagious: spreads by direct contact or droplets (TB). Non-contagious: needs a vector or vehicle (malaria needs mosquitoes).
  • Endemic: "constant presence and/or usual prevalence" in an area. Epidemic: an increase "above what is normally expected". Pandemic: an epidemic "spread over several countries or continents".
Quick check
?Malaria is always present in much of Nigeria, spreads only through mosquito bites, and is caused by a parasite. How is it best classified?
Mission 1

Global distributions of five diseases

  • Malaria: 95% of cases and deaths in the WHO African Region (2024).
  • HIV: 41.0 million living with HIV in 2025, 64% in the WHO African Region.
  • TB: 10.6 million fell ill in 2025: South-East Asia Region 35%, Western Pacific 27%, Africa 25%; India about a quarter.
  • Diabetes: 200 million (1990) to 830 million (2022), rising faster in low- and middle-income countries.
  • CVD: 19.8 million deaths in 2022, about 32% of all deaths; over three quarters in low- and middle-income countries.
Mission 1

The epidemiological transition (Omran, 1971)

  • Omran's three stages: "The Age of Pestilence and Famine"; "The Age of Receding Pandemics"; "The Age of Degenerative and Man-Made Diseases".
  • Over time "pandemics of infection are gradually displaced by degenerative and man-made diseases".
  • It runs alongside the demographic transition: death rates fall first, then people live longer.
  • Driver: rising living standards (more food, clean water, sanitation) cut susceptibility to infection, so more people survive childhood.
Mission 1

The evidence

  • NCDs caused 58% of deaths worldwide in 2000 and 74% in 2023.
  • Eight of the ten leading causes of death in 2023 were NCDs.
  • In 2023, for the first time, communicable diseases caused less than half of all deaths in low-income countries.
  • Life expectancy 73.3 years, but healthy life expectancy (HALE) 62.8 (2023): many extra years are lived in poor health.
Mission 1

Diseases of poverty and of affluence

  • OCR: LIDCs have more communicable disease, ACs more noncommunicable disease.
  • Diseases of poverty (mostly communicable): unsafe water and sanitation, crowded housing, undernutrition, warm climates suiting vectors, young populations, weak health care.
  • Diseases of affluence (NCDs): older populations, tobacco, physical inactivity, harmful use of alcohol and unhealthy diets.
  • The label misleads: 82% of the 18 million NCD deaths before age 70 (2021) were in low- and middle-income countries.
Quick check
?In a country, the risk of dying of heart disease at each age has not changed, yet heart disease's share of all deaths has doubled. What best explains this?
Mission 1

Limits of the model

  • Omran's classical model is based on Western Europe (England and Wales, Sweden); he added an accelerated model (Japan) and a delayed model (Chile, Ceylon).
  • Many poorer countries now face infections and NCDs together: a double burden of disease.
  • Olshansky and Ault (1986) added a fourth stage: "the age of delayed degenerative diseases".
  • New and re-emerging infections (HIV, COVID-19) break the one-way pattern.
Mission check

?Over 40 years in one middle-income country, child deaths from diarrhoea fell sharply, life expectancy rose, and the share of deaths from heart disease, diabetes and dementia doubled. Good news or bad news?

Both. The world's rise from 58% to 74% is Omran's epidemiological transition: infections recede as living standards rise, people live longer, and NCDs take over, while the poorest countries and groups carry both burdens at once.

Mission 2

Cholera: a water-borne disease

  • Cholera is "an acute diarrhoeal infection caused by consuming food or water contaminated with the bacterium Vibrio cholerae".
  • Symptoms appear 12 hours to 5 days after infection. Most infected people have no symptoms but pass the bacteria in their faeces for 1–10 days: it spreads unseen.
  • Linked to unsafe water, poor sanitation and hygiene, conflict, displacement, floods and drought.
Mission 2

The Hägerstrand model: four phases

  • Torsten Hägerstrand modelled how innovations, and diseases, spread through space over time.
  • Primary phase: a few cases at the point of origin; strong contrast with places not yet reached.
  • Diffusion phase: rapid spread outwards; new centres appear away from the origin.
  • Condensing phase: growth slows; spread is about equal everywhere.
  • Saturation phase: few people left to infect; spread stops.
Mission 2

Types of diffusion and barriers

  • Expansion (contagious): spreads to neighbouring people and places. Hierarchical: jumps down a ladder of large to smaller places. Relocation: carriers move to a new place.
  • Physical barriers: mountains, seas, deserts, distance.
  • Socio-economic barriers: poverty, health services, culture, language, policy (e.g. border checks, vaccination).
  • Barriers can absorb spread (stop it) or be permeable (slow it).
Quick check
?Cases are appearing in many new towns far from where the disease began, and daily new cases are climbing fast. Which Hägerstrand phase is this?
Mission 2

The hazard: Haiti, January 2010

  • 12 January 2010: a magnitude 7.0 earthquake, 10 km SE of Léogâne, at a depth of about 13 km, close to the capital, Port-au-Prince.
  • It caused "population displacement, formation of camps, and overcrowding".
  • Haiti had reported no cholera outbreak "for more than a century": people had no immunity, and the health system had no recent experience of it.
Mission 2

Arrival: October 2010

  • "The first cholera outbreak was reported in Haiti in October 2010".
  • The first cluster (16–19 October) was near Mirebalais, inland, "next to a United Nations camp with deficient sanitation"; the next (20–28 October) ran along the Artibonite River.
  • Researchers concluded that contamination of the Artibonite and a tributary "downstream from a military camp triggered the epidemic".
Mission 2

Haiti's spread through the Hägerstrand lens

  • Relocation, then primary phase: bacteria were brought in; first cases beside the camp.
  • Expansion along the river: communes downstream and on the coastal plain had about 4.6–4.9 times the risk of other communes.
  • Diffusion phase: three further clusters in November; in Port-au-Prince the epidemic "exploded in Cite-Soleil, a slum located in a floodplain".
  • Barriers: the river carried it (no physical barrier); distance and better services slowed it in some places.
Mission 2

Did the earthquake cause the outbreak?

  • "Numerous media rapidly related the epidemic to the deadly earthquake".
  • But the first cases were inland, near Mirebalais, not in the earthquake zone.
  • After 6 weeks, "the IDP camps were still relatively free of cholera"; most had better food, safe water and sanitation than neighbouring wards.
  • So the hazard raised vulnerability (damage, crowding, a stretched state); the trigger was the bacterium's arrival.
Quick check

Mission 2

Environmental and human factors

  • Sanitation, 2010: 25% of Haitians had basic sanitation (rural 17%); the Dominican Republic, on the same island, had 83%.
  • Water: 64% had basic drinking water (2010). Faeces reached rivers people used.
  • Climate and relief: the largest northern epidemics were in cities on floodplains; the authors suggest La Niña weather "might have promoted the growth" of the bacteria.
  • Density and immunity: crowded slums such as Cité Soleil; no immunity after a century without cholera.
Quick check
?Haiti reported 820,000 cholera cases and 9,792 deaths from October 2010 to February 2019. What was the case fatality rate?
Mission 2

Impacts

  • Demographic: 9,792 deaths and 820,000 reported cases (2010–2019).
  • Socio-economic: sick adults cannot work and sick children miss school; families bear care costs. The UN later planned material help for the families most affected.
  • Trust: the UN Secretary-General said the outbreak had "cast a shadow upon the relationship between the United Nations and the people of Haiti".
Mission 2

Strategies: national and international

  • International, December 2016: the UN Secretary-General: "we apologise to the Haitian people … We are profoundly sorry for our role".
  • Track One: cut cases (rapid response, vaccination, water, sanitation, health systems). Track Two: material help for those most affected, dependent on voluntary funding.
  • The last confirmed case was in January 2019, at L'Estère in the Artibonite department.
  • Cholera returned in October 2022, centred on Port-au-Prince and Cité Soleil.
  • National: the Ministry of Health, WHO and partners set up an incident management team; the multisectoral plan covered case management, water and sanitation, and vaccination.
  • International limit: since October 2022 a global vaccine shortage has meant single-dose campaigns.
Mission check

?In October 2022 cholera returned to Port-au-Prince and Cité Soleil, amid violence that displaced thousands, fuel shortages and limited safe water and sanitation. Using Haiti's 2010 experience, why might it spread fast again?

In 2010 cholera arrived by relocation, beside a UN camp with deficient sanitation near Mirebalais, then spread by expansion diffusion down the Artibonite. The earthquake raised vulnerability, but low sanitation (25% in 2010), crowding and no immunity explain the speed; the same factors let it return in 2022.

Mission 3: Malaria killed an estimated 610,000 people in 2024. Why does it kill mostly in one continent, and mostly young children?

Mission 3

A biologically transmitted disease

  • Malaria is caused by Plasmodium parasites; P. falciparum and P. vivax are the most dangerous.
  • It spreads through bites of infected female Anopheles mosquitoes: the vector. Symptoms usually start 10–15 days after the bite.
  • Communicable, but not contagious: it needs the mosquito.
Mission 3

Global prevalence and distribution

  • 2024: an estimated 282 million cases and 610,000 deaths in 80 countries.
  • WHO African Region: 95% of cases and 95% of deaths.
  • Shares of the Region's malaria deaths: Nigeria 31.9%, DR Congo 11.7%, Niger 6.1%.
  • Prevalence: the share of people infected at one time. Incidence: new cases in a period.
Mission 3

Physical factors: heat, water and height

  • Temperature: a model validated with African data puts optimal transmission at 25 °C, falling sharply above 28 °C.
  • Water: Anopheles lay eggs on water "in marshy areas or near the banks of shallow creeks and streams"; egg to adult takes 10–14 days.
  • Relief: cool highlands limit transmission; in warmer years malaria reached higher altitudes in Ethiopia and Colombia.
  • So risk peaks in warm, humid lowlands close to still or slow water.
Mission 3

Seasonal incidence

  • Risk varies with the season, "being highest during the rainy season in tropical countries": rain creates pools for breeding.
  • WHO's seasonal malaria chemoprevention gives drugs "at designated time points during the period of greatest malarial risk".
Quick check
?Where would malaria transmission be most intense?
Mission 3

Socio-economic environment

  • Housing and poverty: open eaves and unscreened windows let mosquitoes in; nets and treatment cost money.
  • Health systems and conflict: testing and treatment need clinics; instability disrupts services.
  • Funding: US$3.9 billion was invested in 2024, less than half the US$9.3 billion target for 2025.
  • Urban spread: An. stephensi "thrives in urban settings, endures high temperatures and is resistant to many of the insecticides"; it has invaded 9 African countries.
Mission 3

Nigeria: prevalence and pattern

  • Malaria Indicator Survey 2021: 22.3% of children aged 6–59 months tested positive by microscopy; 39.6% by rapid diagnostic test (RDT).
  • Microscopy by zone: highest North West 29.8%, lowest South West 16.2% (by RDT the North West was 51.6%).
  • Trend: prevalence "fell from 42% in 2010 to 22% in 2021". Nigeria had 31.9% of the WHO African Region's malaria deaths in 2024.
  • Evaluate: always name the test and the survey dates (12 October to 4 December 2021); methods and seasons give different rates.
Quick check
?Using microscopy (2021), North West Nigeria had 29.8% prevalence in young children and South West had 16.2%. How many times higher was the North West?
Mission 3

Impact on health and well-being

  • Children under 5: about 76% of malaria deaths in the WHO African Region.
  • Infants, young children, pregnant women and people with HIV/AIDS are at particular risk; "partial immunity reduces the risk that malaria infection will cause severe disease". Young children have not yet built it.
  • Socio-economic: Gallup and Sachs (2001) found incomes in intensive-malaria countries were a third of those without, and growth 1.3% lower per person per year (1965–1990).
  • Evaluate: a cross-country correlation; poverty also limits protection, so causation runs both ways.
Mission 3

Direct strategies

  • Vector control: "The 2 core interventions are insecticide-treated nets (ITNs) and indoor residual spraying (IRS)".
  • Treatment: artemisinin-based combination therapies. Prevention: seasonal chemoprevention for young children.
  • Vaccines: RTS,S (2021) and R21 (2023). Nigeria introduced R21 in Kebbi and Bayelsa states in December 2024: 1 million doses, 846,200 from Gavi and 153,800 bought by the government.
Mission 3

Indirect strategies

  • Indirect strategies cut risk without targeting the parasite or mosquito directly.
  • Draining standing water and improving housing (screened windows, closed eaves).
  • Poverty reduction and education, so families can afford and use nets and seek early treatment.
  • Exam move: OCR asks for direct and indirect strategies by government and international agencies; give one of each and judge their success.
Mission 3

International agencies and NGOs

  • WHO (Global Malaria Programme): sets "evidence based global guidance", monitors trends and threats, and shapes research.
  • The Global Fund (a financing partnership): "57% of all international financing for malaria programs"; 196 million nets distributed in 2025.
  • Gavi (vaccine alliance): co-funded Nigeria's R21 doses.
  • NGOs: e.g. the Against Malaria Foundation: "100% of public donations buys long-lasting insecticidal nets (LLINs)".
  • Together these tools prevented an estimated 170 million cases and 1 million deaths in 2024.
Quick check
?Which role is played mainly by WHO rather than by a funding body such as the Global Fund?
Mission 3

Threats to progress

  • Drug resistance: partial resistance to artemisinin is confirmed in Eritrea, Rwanda, Uganda and Tanzania, and confirmed or suspected in at least 8 African countries.
  • Vector spread: An. stephensi, native to parts of South Asia and the Arabian Peninsula, has invaded 9 African countries.
  • Funding gap: investment below half the target.
Mission check

?Two children aged 2 have caught malaria for the first time. One lives in a cool highland town at 2,800 m; the other in a hot, humid lowland village beside a marsh, where few homes have nets. Which statement best explains which child faces the greater risk over the coming years?

Warm, wet lowlands and rainy seasons suit the parasite and Anopheles; poverty, weak services and funding gaps limit nets and treatment. So 95% of deaths are in the WHO African Region, and about three in four are children under 5, who have little partial immunity.

Mission 4

Seasons and disease

  • Many outbreaks follow the seasons: dry periods and monsoon rains change water, dust, vectors and crowding.
  • Rainy season: malaria risk is "highest during the rainy season in tropical countries" (WHO), because mosquitoes breed in standing water.
  • Dry season: some diseases peak when it is driest.
  • Seasonal timing lets health services plan vaccination and treatment.
Mission 4

Drought season: meningitis in the Sahel

  • The African meningitis belt stretches from Senegal to Ethiopia.
  • 2025: 21,526 suspected cases and 971 deaths reported by 24 of the 26 belt countries in WHO's network.
  • In Mali, epidemics begin around 15 February and recede in early May, as the wet season starts.
  • Cause: the dry winter Harmattan brings "dry air and strong dust winds" that damage the throat's lining, helping the bacteria invade.
Mission 4

Monsoon season: cholera in Dhaka

  • Dhaka, Bangladesh, 2004–24: 6,141 confirmed cholera cases in icddr,b surveillance.
  • 48% occurred in the pre-monsoon period.
  • Risk rose with temperature, and peaked at about 150 mm of rainfall.
  • Cholera spreads through unsafe water and poor sanitation, which rain and heat can worsen.

Mission 4

Climate change and emerging diseases

  • Emerging infectious disease: new to a place or host, or rapidly increasing.
  • Warming can let vectors (mosquitoes, ticks, flies) survive, breed and bite in new places and for longer seasons.
  • But hotter can also be too hot: some vectors decline.
  • Other drivers act too: land use, travel, trade, population density.
Mission 4

West Nile virus in Europe

  • Birds are the natural hosts; mosquitoes pass the virus to people and horses.
  • First isolated in Uganda (1937); introduced to the USA in 1999, it is now established "from Canada to Venezuela".
  • A 2024 study modelled Europe, 1901–2019: the area suitable for West Nile circulation increased notably, but stayed "largely unchanged" in a no-climate-change counterfactual.
  • It concluded that climate change "has also been a critical driver" alongside population changes.
Mission 4

Tsetse flies: winners and losers

  • Tsetse flies spread sleeping sickness (human African trypanosomiasis) in sub-Saharan Africa.
  • Mana Pools, Zambezi Valley, Zimbabwe: tsetse caught on cattle fell from about 50 per animal per afternoon (1990) to about 0.1 (2017).
  • Mean daily temperatures there rose about 0.9 °C since 1975; the hottest month (November) about 2 °C.
  • The model suggests warming may explain the collapse, while "higher, cooler" parts of Zimbabwe may have become more suitable.
Mission 4

Ticks moving north

  • Ixodes ricinus ticks spread Lyme borreliosis and tick-borne encephalitis (TBE).
  • Sweden, early 1980s–2008: the tick's range expanded north; reported as far north as 66° N.
  • North of 60° N, tick coverage rose from 12.5% (early 1990s) to 26.8% (2008).
  • ECDC: TBE cases in Europe's endemic regions rose by "almost 400% in the last 30 years"; "Wet summers and mild winters tend to increase tick population density".
Quick check
?Tsetse catches at Mana Pools fell about 500-fold as temperatures rose. What does this show about climate change and disease?
Mission 4

Zoonoses: when animal diseases jump

  • Zoonosis: "any disease or infection that is naturally transmissible from vertebrate animals to humans"; over 200 types are known.
  • Risks rise with close contact: farms, markets, pets and wildlife.
  • WHO: "Urbanization and the destruction of natural habitats increase the risk of zoonotic diseases by increasing contact between humans and wild animals".
Mission 4

Bird flu: the conditions

  • H5N1 spread in birds from Asia to Europe and Africa (from 2003), and to the Americas in 2021.
  • Main risk for people: exposure to infected animals or contaminated places "such as live bird markets"; also handling and preparing infected poultry at home.
  • 2024: H5N1 spread to dairy cattle in the USA; some farm workers in contact with infected cows were infected.
  • So far: "not demonstrated sustained human-to-human transmission". A pandemic needs that step.
Mission 4

Rabies: the conditions

  • Dogs cause up to 99% of human rabies cases.
  • Modelled deaths: 44,203 a year, probably under-reported; mainly in Asia and Africa.
  • Conditions: unvaccinated dogs, bites, and no access to post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP); PEP is often "inaccessible or unaffordable".
  • Control: mass dog vaccination is the most cost-effective strategy; culling free-roaming dogs "is not effective".
Mission check

Disease patterns follow seasons, shift with climate change (both spreading and shrinking vectors), and jump species when people, animals and vectors come into close contact.

Mission 5

Outdoor air pollution is a carcinogen

  • In 2013 IARC classified outdoor air pollution as carcinogenic to humans (Group 1).
  • Experts found "sufficient evidence" that it causes lung cancer.
  • Particulate matter was evaluated separately, and also classed Group 1.
  • For bladder cancer, they "noted a positive association": weaker evidence than for lung cancer.
Mission 5

The global scale

  • IARC: 223,000 lung cancer deaths worldwide in 2010 resulted from air pollution.
  • In 2019, 99% of the world's people lived where WHO air quality guideline levels were not met.
  • Average exposure to fine particles (PM2.5) was 35.7 µg/m³ in 2015–2019, against a WHO guideline of 5 µg/m³.
Quick check
?IARC found "sufficient evidence" for lung cancer but only "a positive association" for bladder cancer. What does that difference mean?
Mission 5

How particles may cause cancer

  • Cancer Research UK: particles may build up in the lungs and damage DNA, or cause inflammation that can lead to cancer.
  • A 2023 Nature study found PM2.5 levels associated with incidence in 32,957 cases of EGFR-driven lung cancer across four cohorts.
  • In mice, pollutants drew macrophages into the lung and released interleukin-1β, an inflammatory signal.
  • Cancer-linked EGFR mutations were found in 18% of healthy lung samples: pollution may promote existing mutated cells rather than create the mutation.
Mission 5

Case study: the United Kingdom, then

  • London's Great Smog, 5–9 December 1952: about 4,000 people were known to have died, "but it could be many more".
  • The Clean Air Acts of 1956 and 1968 followed, to prevent a repeat.
  • The mix changed: coal smoke declined, and traffic became a major source.
Mission 5

The United Kingdom, now

  • CRUK's latest figure: 50,218 new lung cancer cases in the UK (based on 2019 and 2021–2022 data).
  • Cancer Research UK: outdoor air pollution causes roughly 1 in 10 UK lung cancers.
  • Smoking causes around nine times more lung cancer cases than air pollution.
  • "It has not been proven that air pollution causes other types of cancer" (CRUK).

Mission 5

Uneven exposure inside the country

  • London, 2019: where the most deprived residents were likely to live, average NO₂ was 13% higher than in the least deprived areas.

  • It lasts up to 20 years, renewed each year; the invention must be new and inventive.
  • During that time the company sets prices with no copycat competitors, which is how it recovers research costs.
  • When the patent expires, generic makers can copy the medicine; AstraZeneca's life-cycle model names "Patent expiry and generic medicine entry".
Mission 11

Patents in practice

  • Farxiga (a diabetes, heart and kidney medicine) is approved in 126 countries.
  • In 2025 the company "saw increased generic competition for Farxiga".
  • Symbicort (asthma) suffered "generic erosion in the EU and Japan".
  • So each medicine's earnings rise, peak, then fall as patents expire market by market.

Mission 11

A breakthrough: the Oxford/AstraZeneca vaccine

  • Developed with the University of Oxford, the COVID-19 vaccine was authorised in the UK by the medicines regulator (MHRA) on 30 December 2020.
  • On 15 February 2021 WHO listed two versions for emergency use: one made by AstraZeneca-SKBio (Republic of Korea), one by the Serum Institute of India.
  • WHO listing is needed before vaccines can be supplied through COVAX, the global sharing scheme, so these versions could be rolled out worldwide.
  • A breakthrough in one place reached the world through partner manufacturers.
Mission 11

Manufacturing network

  • AstraZeneca runs 31 Operations sites in 15 countries.
  • About 16,900 employees work at those sites.
  • Alongside its own plants, licensed partners can add capacity, as with the vaccine in India and Korea.
  • Spreading production cuts risk and puts supply closer to large markets.

Mission 11

Global distribution flows

  • 2025 revenue by region:
  • United States: $25.5 billion (43%).
  • Emerging Markets: $15.3 billion (26%).
  • Europe: $12.7 billion (22%).
  • Established Rest of World: $5.2 billion (9%).
  • Medicines flow out to more than 125 countries; money flows back mainly from high-income markets.

Mission 11

Patents and access: the debate

  • The company's position: patents protect the returns that pay for risky research. AstraZeneca also runs a tiered pricing policy with four tiers based on gross national income.
  • Access campaigners' position: patents keep prices high in poorer countries for up to 20 years.
  • In June 2022 WTO members agreed a waiver of certain requirements on compulsory licensing for COVID-19 vaccines: governments can allow production without the patent holder's consent under set conditions.
  • Geography: where a patent is held, and where it is waived, shapes who gets medicines and when.
Mission 11

Impacts on places

  • Research hubs gain high-skill jobs and investment.
  • Manufacturing hosts (15 countries, plus licensed partners) gain jobs and supply security.
  • Markets gain medicines; prices vary by tier and patent status.
  • Exam move: for a TNC case study, link breakthrough → patent → manufacture → flow of products and profits, with named figures.
Mission check

A pharmaceutical TNC links places through breakthroughs (research), patents (time-limited control), manufacturing (own sites and partners) and flows (medicines out, revenue back), and those links decide who gets new medicines first.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • As living standards rise, infections recede and NCDs dominate: the epidemiological transition.
  • Classify diseases (infectious, communicable, contagious; endemic, epidemic, pandemic) and know where malaria, HIV, TB, diabetes and CVD concentrate.

Mission 2

  • Hägerstrand: primary, diffusion, condensing and saturation phases; spread is slowed or stopped by physical and socio-economic barriers.
  • Haiti: a 2010 earthquake raised vulnerability, but cholera's trigger was its arrival near a UN camp; poor sanitation and the river spread it fast.
  • Strategies: treatment, water and sanitation, vaccination and the UN's two tracks; weak infrastructure let it return in 2022.

Mission 3

  • Malaria is vector-borne: temperature near 25 °C, breeding water, low altitude and the rainy season raise transmission.
  • Poverty, housing, health systems, conflict and funding shape who is protected.
  • Nigeria: high prevalence (highest in the North West); direct strategies (nets, IRS, drugs, R21), backed by WHO, the Global Fund, Gavi and NGOs.

Mission 4

  • Seasonal: meningitis peaks in the dry, dusty Harmattan season (February–May in Mali); malaria peaks in rainy seasons; Dhaka's cholera peaks before the monsoon.
  • Climate change: West Nile suitability in Europe grew with warming; ticks spread north in Sweden; tsetse collapsed in the hot Zambezi Valley. Zoonoses need close contact (live bird markets, dairy cattle, unvaccinated dogs) and spread to people only under particular conditions.

Mission 5

  • IARC (2013): outdoor air pollution and particulate matter are Group 1 carcinogens; sufficient evidence for lung cancer, a positive association for bladder cancer.
  • UK: roughly 1 in 10 lung cancers from outdoor air pollution; national (Clean Air Acts, 2040 PM2.5 target), local (ULEZ) and global (WHO guidelines, UNECE Air Convention) solutions.

Mission 6

  • CVD (including CHD) causes about 32% of global deaths, over three quarters in low- and middle-income countries; in the UK over 8 million live with it and about 170,000 die each year, with early deaths highest in poorer northern areas.
  • Causes are mostly modifiable (blood pressure, smoking, obesity, inactivity, air pollution); impacts include £12 billion NHS and £29 billion economic costs.
  • Management: NHS Health Check (direct), Soft Drinks Industry Levy (indirect), statins; WHO HEARTS, SDG 3.4 and the BHF act at wider scales.

Mission 7

  • COVID-19 spread from one city to 197 countries and territories in three months, with reported cases doubling each week in late March; its centre moved from East Asia to Europe to the Americas.
  • WHO predicts and alerts, gathers data, coordinates research and runs support programmes, but cannot make governments act.

Mission 8

  • MSF in Équateur, DRC (2018): national level (ring vaccination with the Ministry and WHO) and local level (Bikoro treatment centre, safe burials, alert system in Wangata); outbreak over on 24 July 2018 after 54 cases and 33 deaths.
  • Physical barriers (remoteness, relief, rivers) had positive effects (containment) and negative effects (slow access, river and urban links raising the risk of spread).

Mission 9

  • HIV strategies combine screening, treatment (which also prevents) and education; funding and stigma decide who is reached.
  • Eradication is global (smallpox; polio nearly); elimination is national (China's malaria). Grassroots education and women carry much of the work at community level.

Mission 10

  • Prunus africana grows in Afromontane forest (18–26 °C, 890–2,600 mm, 900–3,400 m); its bark extract treats BPH and is the most widely exported African medicinal tree product.
  • Overharvest (vulnerable; CITES Appendix II since 1995; Cameroon ban 2007–10 and quota cut) threatens survival, genetic diversity across five regional lineages and Afromontane forests; quotas and domestication aim at sustainable use.

Mission 11

  • AstraZeneca: $58.7 bn revenue (2025); $14.2 bn invested in science; 31 Operations sites in 15 countries; sells in 125+ countries; US 43%, Emerging Markets 26%, Europe 22%, Established RoW 9%.
  • Patents (up to 20 years) protect returns; generics follow expiry (Farxiga, Symbicort). The Oxford/AstraZeneca vaccine reached COVAX through partners in Korea and India; WTO agreed a compulsory-licensing waiver for COVID-19 vaccines in 2022.
