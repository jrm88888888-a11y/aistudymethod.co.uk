Mission 1: Christians pray "Our Father". Does that mean they believe God is male?

Make a guess: Christians pray "Our Father". Does that mean they believe God is male?

Mission 1

Human words for God

  • Anthropomorphic language: describing God in human terms (Father, King, Judge, "the hand of God").
  • Christians believe God is spirit (John 4:24) and beyond all creatures, so no human word fits God exactly.
  • Classical theology treats such words as analogy (Thomas Aquinas) or metaphor: true in one way, not in another.
  • Mainstream teaching: God has no sex. Catholic teaching: God "is neither man nor woman: he is God" (Catechism of the Catholic Church 239).
  • Yet Christian worship has used mostly male words: Father, King, Lord, "he".
  • The question: if the words are not literal, do they still shape how people see God, and men and women?
Mission 1

God as personal, God as Father

  • Personal: God can be known, loved and spoken to; God acts with will and love, not as an impersonal force.
  • Jesus taught his followers to pray "Our Father in heaven" (Matthew 6:9).
  • Christians receive "the Spirit of adoption, by whom we cry, 'Abba! Father!'" (Romans 8:15).
  • In the Trinity, "Father" names a relationship: the Father of the Son (see Mission 3). For many Christians it is a revealed name, not just one image among many.
Mission 1

"Father": pastoral benefits and challenges

  • Benefits: intimacy and trust (Jesus' Abba); belonging as God's children; care, protection and provision; a model of good fatherhood.
  • Challenges: for people hurt by an absent or abusive father, the image may bring fear, not comfort.
  • It may suggest God is distant or stern, or that God is male.
  • Feminists argue it has been used to justify male authority in the Church and family.
  • Pastoral responses: stress the father of Luke 15 (who runs to forgive), use other biblical images alongside it, or teach clearly that "Father" is not about sex.

Female images of God in the Bible

> "As one whom his mother comforts, so I will comfort you." (Isaiah 66:13, WEBBE)

> "Can a woman forget her nursing child, that she should not have compassion on the son of her womb? Yes, these may forget, yet I will not forget you!" (Isaiah 49:15, WEBBE)

  • Jesus compares himself to "a hen [who] gathers her chicks" (Matthew 23:37).
  • "God created man in his own image… male and female he created them." (Genesis 1:27)
  • The medieval mystic Julian of Norwich (c. 1343–after 1416) wrote that God is truly our Mother as well as our Father (Revelations of Divine Love, ch. 59, paraphrase).

Mission 1

McFague: metaphors and models

  • Sallie McFague (1933–2019), American theologian; Metaphorical Theology (1982), Models of God (1987).
  • All talk of God is metaphor: a model of God both "is and is not" what it says (paraphrase).
  • Trouble comes when one metaphor is taken literally and becomes an idol: e.g. God as King.
  • The monarchical model (God as king and lord over the world): distant, controlling, and male. She argues it encourages domination and passivity, and fits badly with an age of nuclear and ecological threat.
  • So she proposes new models: God as Mother, Lover and Friend, and the world as God's body.
Mission 1

McFague: God as Mother

  • Mother as creator: God brings the world to birth, from God's own being, rather than making it as an object from outside (paraphrase).
  • Mother's love (agape in her sense): love that says "it is good that you exist" and wants all its children to live and flourish.
  • Mother as judge: justice as care for all she has borne; judgement falls on what harms the whole family, including the earth.
  • Ethic: care for others and for the planet; a mother does not dominate her children.
  • McFague does not say God is female: Mother is one partial model, used with others.
Mission 1

Daly: "if God is male, then the male is God"

  • Mary Daly (1928–2010), American feminist philosopher and theologian; Beyond God the Father (1973).
  • Her claim: "if God is male, then the male is God" (Beyond God the Father, ch. 1).
  • A male image of God, she argues, makes men seem god-like and legitimates patriarchy: male rule in Church, family and society.
  • The symbol of "God the Father" is not harmless picture-language: it shapes power.
  • Daly came to see Christianity as beyond reform and left it ("post-Christian").
Mission 1Diversity

Christian feminist and other responses

  • Reform from within: e.g. Rosemary Radford Ruether (1936–2022) and Elizabeth Johnson (She Who Is, 1992): recover female and wisdom (Sophia) images; keep Christianity.
  • Leave: Daly, and Daphne Hampson (b. 1944): Christianity is irredeemably patriarchal.
  • Inclusive language: some Protestant churches allow inclusive or female language for God in some prayers and hymns, alongside "Father".
  • Traditional: Catholic and Orthodox teaching, and many evangelicals, keep "Father" as the name Jesus revealed, while teaching God has no sex.
  • Some feminists also criticise "Mother": it may fix women's role as nurturing and motherhood.

Mission 1Evaluate

Is it valid to refer to God as Mother?

  • Claim: It is valid for Christians to refer to God as Mother.
  • For: The Bible itself uses maternal images (Isaiah 49:15, 66:13; Matthew 23:37), and Julian of Norwich did so.
  • For: If God has no sex, female words are as fitting (and as limited) as male ones.
  • For: McFague: male-only language has harmful effects; Mother expresses care for all.
  • Against: Jesus taught "Father", and the name expresses the Father–Son relation in the Trinity.
  • Against: McFague's "world as God's body" may blur God and creation (a risk of pantheism).
  • Against: "Mother" may itself fix a narrow idea of women.
  • Judgement criteria: Is God-language revealed or chosen? Does any image bring a sex into God? Which images help people to know God and treat each other justly?

Mission 2: Was Jesus fully God, or the first and greatest thing God made? Why did it matter so much?

Make a guess: Was Jesus fully God, or the first and greatest thing God made? Why did it matter so much?

Mission 2

The puzzle

  • Christians inherited strict monotheism: "The LORD is our God. The LORD is one." (Deuteronomy 6:4)
  • Yet the New Testament shows early Christians worshipping Jesus: in John's Gospel, Thomas calls him "My Lord and my God!" (John 20:28).
  • So: is Jesus God, or something less than God?
  • In the 4th century this became a crisis that split the Church and reached the emperor.
Mission 2

Arius: the Son is created

  • Arius (c. 256–336), a priest in Alexandria, Egypt; dispute from c. 318.
  • God alone is unbegotten, unchanging, without beginning.
  • So the Son (the Word, Logos) must have a beginning: created by God before time, the first and highest creature, through whom God made everything else.
  • Slogan of his party, condemned at Nicaea and attacked by Athanasius: "there was when he was not".
  • Texts he used: "the firstborn of all creation" (Colossians 1:15); "the Father is greater than I" (John 14:28); Proverbs 8:22 (Wisdom "created" in the Greek version).
  • His aim: to protect the oneness and changelessness of God.
Mission 2

Athanasius: only God can save

  • Athanasius (c. 296–373), deacon at Nicaea, then bishop of Alexandria from 328; exiled five times.
  • On the Incarnation (c. 318 or c. 335; date debated).
  • Humans, made in God's image, were sliding back into death and corruption through sin.
  • Only the Creator can re-create: a creature cannot restore the image of God.
  • "For He was made man that we might be made God" (On the Incarnation 54): sharing God's life (theosis, deification).
  • So if the Son is a creature, we are not saved. The Son must be fully God.
Mission 2Read the source

The Word before the world

> "In the beginning was the Word, and the Word was with God, and the Word was God." (John 1:1, WEBBE)

> "The Word became flesh and lived amongst us." (John 1:14, WEBBE)

> "who, existing in the form of God, didn't consider equality with God a thing to be grasped, but emptied himself, taking the form of a servant" (Philippians 2:6–7, WEBBE)

  • Pre-existence: the Son exists before his human birth: "before Abraham came into existence, I AM" (John 8:58).

Mission 2

Nicaea, 325

  • Emperor Constantine called the first ecumenical (worldwide) council to end the dispute: roughly 250–300 bishops at Nicaea (ancient counts vary; tradition says 318).
  • The creed of Nicaea: the Son is "begotten, not made", "of one substance with the Father".
  • Key word homoousios: of the same substance (being) as the Father; not homoiousios, "of similar substance".
  • So the Son is co-equal with the Father and has no beginning.
  • Arius was condemned and exiled. Arian views stayed strong for decades; the Council of Constantinople (381) confirmed Nicaea (see Mission 3).

Mission 3: Christians pray to Father, Son and Holy Spirit. How can that still be one God?

Make a guess: Christians pray to Father, Son and Holy Spirit. How can that still be one God?

Mission 3

One God, three persons

  • The Trinity: one God in three persons: Father, Son and Holy Spirit.
  • Christians believe each person is fully God, yet there is one God, not three.
  • "Hear, Israel: The LORD is our God. The LORD is one." (Deuteronomy 6:4)
  • Three views the Church rejected:
  • Tritheism: three separate gods.
  • Modalism: one person appearing in three modes (associated with Sabellius, 3rd century).
  • Subordinationism: the Son and Spirit as lesser than the Father.
Mission 3Read the source

Biblical basis

The word "Trinity" is not in the Bible. Christians find the pattern in it:

> "baptising them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit" (Matthew 28:19, WEBBE)

> "The grace of the Lord Jesus Christ, God's love, and the fellowship of the Holy Spirit be with you all." (2 Corinthians 13:14, WEBBE)

  • At Jesus' baptism, the Spirit descends "as a dove" and the Father's voice speaks (Matthew 3:16–17).
  • "yet to us there is one God, the Father, of whom are all things … and one Lord, Jesus Christ, through whom are all things" (1 Corinthians 8:6): Paul places Jesus inside the Jewish confession of one God.
  • "I and the Father are one." (John 10:30)

Q1 In Matthew 28:19, how many "names" are there?

Show answer

Mission 3

The Son and the Spirit as God

  • Son: "the Word was God" (John 1:1); "My Lord and my God!" (John 20:28).
  • Pre-existence: "the glory which I had with you before the world existed" (John 17:5).
  • Spirit: lying to the Holy Spirit is lying "to God" (Acts 5:3–4); "Where could I go from your Spirit?" (Psalm 139:7).
  • Christians read the Spirit as personal: teaches and reminds (John 14:26); not just a force.
  • Early Christians worshipped and baptised in the threefold name before the doctrine was defined.
  • The doctrine explains what Christians already did in prayer.

Mission 3

From Nicaea to Constantinople

  • Nicaea (325): the Son is homoousios (of one substance) with the Father (see Mission 2).
  • Cappadocian Fathers: Basil of Caesarea (c. 330–379), Gregory of Nazianzus (c. 329–390), Gregory of Nyssa (c. 335–c. 395).
  • Their formula: one ousia (being, substance), three hypostaseis (persons).
  • Constantinople (381) completed the creed: the Spirit is "the Lord and Giver of life", who "proceeds from the Father", and "with the Father and the Son together is worshipped and glorified".
  • This Nicene Creed is shared by Catholic, Orthodox and most Protestant churches.
Mission 3

Co-equal and co-eternal

  • Co-equal: no person is greater or lesser in being; all share the one divine nature.
  • Co-eternal: no person had a beginning; there was never a time when the Father was without the Son and the Spirit.
  • The Athanasian Creed (Latin, 5th–6th century): none of the three is before or after another, none greater or less (paraphrase).
  • But a relation of origin: the Father begets, the Son is begotten, the Spirit proceeds.
  • "The Father is greater than I" (John 14:28): read as Jesus speaking in his humanity, or of the Father as source, not of a greater being.
Mission 3

The filioque

  • Latin filioque: "and the Son". The West added it to the creed: the Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son.
  • First used in Spain (Council of Toledo, 589); spread in the Frankish church; sung at Mass in Rome from 1014.
  • West (Augustine, On the Trinity): the Spirit is the bond of love between Father and Son; "the Spirit of his Son" (Galatians 4:6).
  • East (Photius, 9th century): the Father is the one source (monarchia) of Son and Spirit; John 15:26 says the Spirit "proceeds from the Father".
  • East also objected that a council's creed was changed without an ecumenical council.
  • A cause of the East–West Schism (1054), with papal authority.
  • Today: the Catholic Church omits the filioque when it uses the Greek creed; Lambeth Conferences (1978, 1988) asked Anglicans to consider omitting it.

Mission 3Evaluate

"The doctrine of the Trinity is consistent with monotheism."

  • For: there is one divine being (ousia); the persons are not three beings but three relations within one God.
  • For: the New Testament writers were Jewish monotheists who placed Jesus inside the one God (1 Corinthians 8:6).
  • Against: Jewish and Muslim critics: worshipping three persons compromises God's oneness.
  • Against: popular explanations slide into tritheism or modalism; perhaps no coherent middle exists.
  • Against: "person" today means a separate centre of consciousness, making three persons sound like three gods.
  • Also debated: is the Trinity necessary to understand the God of Christianity, or a later philosophical layer?
  • Judge it by: What does "one" mean (number, being, unity)? Does it fit the texts? Can a mystery be consistent without being explained?
Mission check

?A youth leader says: "God is like one actor who plays three parts: first the Father in the Old Testament, then the Son in the Gospels, then the Spirit at Pentecost." Which assessment would most Christian theologians (Catholic, Orthodox and Protestant) give?

For mainstream Christian teaching, the doctrine keeps two truths together: one God, and three distinct, co-equal, co-eternal persons; losing either is seen as the error.

Mission 4: When Jesus died on the cross, did God suffer too, or can God not suffer at all?

Make a guess: When Jesus died on the cross, did God suffer too, or can God not suffer at all?

Mission 4

What "impassible" means

  • Impassibility: God cannot be acted upon or changed by anything outside God, and so does not undergo suffering or changing emotions (Latin pati, to suffer or undergo).
  • Linked to immutability: God does not change.
  • It does not mean God is cold or indifferent: classical theologians held that God loves perfectly, but without passion in the sense of being moved from outside.
  • Passibility: God can be affected and can suffer.
  • The modern question: can a God who cannot suffer really love a suffering world?
Mission 4Read the source

Texts on both sides

> "For I, the LORD, don't change" (Malachi 3:6, WEBBE)

> "In all their affliction he was afflicted, and the angel of his presence saved them." (Isaiah 63:9, WEBBE)

  • Other texts for unchanging: "God is not a man… that he should repent" (Numbers 23:19); "the Father of lights, with whom can be no variation" (James 1:17).
  • Other texts for feeling: God "was sorry that he had made man… and it grieved him in his heart" (Genesis 6:6); "My heart is turned within me, my compassion is aroused" (Hosea 11:8).

Mission 4

Why classical theology held impassibility

  • From the early Church to the Reformation, most theologians read the feeling texts as anthropomorphic: true of God's acts, not of changes in God.
  • Perfection: a perfect being lacks nothing; change would be gain or loss.
  • Reliability: an unchanging God is faithful (Malachi 3:6).
  • Thomas Aquinas: love and joy in God are acts of will, not passions; God "loves without passion" (Summa Theologica I, q. 20, a. 1).
  • The early Church rejected patripassianism (the view that the Father himself suffered on the cross).
  • Cyril of Alexandria (d. 444): the divine Word "suffered impassibly": he suffered in his own flesh, while remaining unchanged as God (paraphrase).
Mission 4

Moltmann: the context

  • Jürgen Moltmann (1926–2024), German Reformed theologian; Theology of Hope (1964), The Crucified God (1972; English 1974).
  • As a teenage soldier he saw Hamburg destroyed by bombing (1943); a prisoner of war 1945–48, he came to faith reading the Bible in the camp, and felt understood by the Christ who cried out on the cross.
  • After Auschwitz, he argued, Christians cannot speak of an untouched God watching from heaven.
  • He recalls Elie Wiesel's account of a boy hanged in Auschwitz: a prisoner asks "Where is God?", and Wiesel hears an inner answer: here, on the gallows (paraphrase of Night, 1958).
  • Moltmann's reply: God is in the suffering, not above it.
Mission 4

Moltmann: the crucified God

  • The cross is an event within the Trinity: between the Father and the Son.
  • The Son suffers dying and abandonment by the Father. The Father suffers the grief of the Son's death. The suffering differs, so this is not patripassianism.
  • From this event the Spirit of love goes out to the godforsaken.
  • A God who cannot suffer, he argues, also cannot love: love means being open to being hurt (paraphrase).
  • So God chooses to suffer, out of love: suffering is not forced on God from outside.
  • Result: God is in solidarity with all victims; no one is outside God's reach.
Mission 4Read the source

The cry from the cross

> "At the ninth hour Jesus cried with a loud voice, saying, 'Eloi, Eloi, lama sabachthani?' which is, being interpreted, 'My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?'" (Mark 15:34, WEBBE)

  • Jesus quotes Psalm 22:1.
  • Moltmann: the heart of the cross; real godforsakenness, endured by God the Son.
  • Classical reading: the Son, in his human nature, takes on human abandonment; Psalm 22 ends in trust and praise.

Show answer

Mission 4Evaluate

Does God suffer in or through Jesus?

  • Claim: God suffers, not only in Jesus' humanity, but in God's own life.
  • For (Moltmann): It makes sense of texts where God grieves (Genesis 6:6; Hosea 11:8; Isaiah 63:9).
  • For: It answers Auschwitz with God's solidarity; love that cannot suffer may not be love.
  • Against (classical): a suffering God changes and lacks some good; the feeling texts are anthropomorphic.
  • Against: Moltmann may come close to patripassianism, or make suffering part of God's nature.
  • Implications of a suffering God: comfort and solidarity for sufferers; but questions about God's power, perfection and ultimate victory over evil.
  • Judgement criteria: Which set of biblical texts interprets the other? Can love be perfect without being affected? Does a suffering God help or hinder hope?

Mission 5

Two key terms

  • Atonement: the reconciling of God and humanity. The English word, popularised by William Tyndale's New Testament (1526, Romans 5:11), means "at-one-ment": making one again.
  • Soteriology: the study of salvation (Greek sōtēria, salvation + logos, study). What are we saved from, to, and how?
  • The problem: sin separates humans from God (Isaiah 59:2). Christians believe God offers forgiveness through Jesus' death and resurrection.
  • The creeds say Christ died "for us" but give no single theory of how this works. Several models grew up.
  • Your spec stresses they are "not mutually exclusive": many Christians hold more than one.

Mission 5

Sacrifice

  • Background: the Day of Atonement (Leviticus 16). Sin is confessed over a goat sent into the wilderness; blood is offered for cleansing.
  • "It is the blood that makes atonement by reason of the life." (Leviticus 17:11)
  • Hebrews presents Jesus as both priest and offering, "once for all" (Hebrews 10:10).
  • John the Baptist: "Behold, the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world!" (John 1:29).
  • Sacrifice language means: a life given to restore the relationship that sin broke.
  • Catholic and Orthodox Christians link this to the Eucharist, which makes present the one sacrifice of Christ.
Mission 5

Christus Victor: victory

  • Gustaf Aulén (1879–1977), Swedish Lutheran bishop: Christus Victor (lectures 1930; English 1931).
  • He called this the "classic" view: held by the early Church Fathers and, he argued, by Luther.
  • Humanity is held captive by hostile powers: sin, death and the devil. On the cross God, in Christ, fights and defeats them.
  • "through death he might bring to nothing him who had the power of death, that is, the devil" (Hebrews 2:14).
  • Early ransom forms: Origen said the ransom was paid to the devil; Gregory of Nyssa pictured Christ's humanity as bait hiding the "hook" of his divinity.
  • The resurrection is the victory. The Orthodox Easter hymn: Christ is risen, "trampling down death by death".
  • Aulén: atonement is God's own act from start to finish, not humanity's offering to God.

Mission 5

Anselm: satisfaction

  • Anselm of Canterbury (c. 1033–1109), Cur Deus Homo ("Why God Became Man", 1098).
  • He rejected the idea that the devil had any rights over humanity to be bought off.
  • Sin robs God of the honour owed by every creature. "Every wish of a rational creature should be subject to the will of God." (I.11, trans. Deane)
  • Repaying what was taken is "the satisfaction which every sinner owes to God" (I.11).
  • God cannot just overlook this; but humans cannot pay, since they already owe God everything.
  • Only God can pay; only a human ought to. So "it is necessary for the God-man to make it" (II.6).
  • Christ's free, sinless death gives God more honour than sin took away. This is the substitution in which only the divine-human Jesus could act.
Mission 5

Substitution: Christ in our place

  • Penal substitution: Christ bears the punishment for sin in place of sinners. Developed by the Reformers (Calvin, Institutes II.16) and central to many evangelicals.
  • "He was pierced for our transgressions. He was crushed for our iniquities." (Isaiah 53:5)
  • "For him who knew no sin he made to be sin on our behalf" (2 Corinthians 5:21); Christ became "a curse for us" (Galatians 3:13).
  • Difference from Anselm: Anselm's satisfaction is an alternative to punishment (honour restored); penal substitution is punishment, borne by another.
  • Your spec names both: Jesus as a substitute for humanity, and only the divine-human Jesus as the sacrifice by God for humanity.
Mission 5

Moral example

  • Peter Abelard (1079–1142), Commentary on Romans (c. 1133–37).
  • The cross does not change God (who already loves); it changes us. Christ's love, shown to the death, kindles love in us and frees us from fear (paraphrase).
  • "Greater love has no one than this, that someone lay down his life for his friends." (John 15:13)
  • Jesus is the moral exemplar: how to live, and how to die, faithful and forgiving (Luke 23:34).
  • Aulén called this the "subjective" type: the change happens in the human heart.
  • Developed in liberal theology (e.g. Hastings Rashdall, The Idea of Atonement in Christian Theology, 1919) and by John Hick.

Mission 5

Wrath, love and forgiveness

  • Assumption 1: humans need divine forgiveness; sin is real and serious (Romans 3:23).
  • Assumption 2: God is just. "The wrath of God is revealed from heaven against all ungodliness" (Romans 1:18).
  • Assumption 3: God is love. "God commends his own love towards us, in that while we were yet sinners, Christ died for us." (Romans 5:8)
  • How do wrath and love fit? Substitution: love provides what justice requires (1 John 4:10). Victory: wrath falls on the powers, love frees the captives. Example: God's love needs no payment, only our response.
  • Critique: if the Father punishes the Son, is God cruel? Some feminist theologians called this a picture of "divine child abuse"; Steve Chalke and Alan Mann wrote "cosmic child abuse" (The Lost Message of Jesus, 2003).
  • Replies: the Trinity acts together: "God was in Christ reconciling the world to himself" (2 Corinthians 5:19). The Son gives himself freely (John 10:18). "Wrath" is God's settled opposition to evil, not a temper.
Mission 5Diversity

Who stresses which model?

  • Eastern Orthodox: Christus Victor and theosis (sharing God's life); sin as sickness and death more than legal guilt.
  • Catholic: satisfaction and sacrifice (Anselm, Aquinas); the Mass as the sacrifice made present.
  • Reformed and evangelical: penal substitution as the heart of the gospel (with victory and example as results).
  • Liberal Protestant: moral example; doubts about punishment language.
  • Many theologians today combine them: the cross as sacrifice for sin, victory over evil, and example of love.
Mission 5Evaluate

Are the theories contradictory?

  • Claim: The theories of the atonement contradict each other.
  • For: God changes (satisfaction, substitution) versus only we change (moral example).
  • For: Anselm rejected any payment to the devil; the ransom form of Christus Victor needs one.
  • For: Satisfaction instead of punishment (Anselm) versus punishment itself (penal substitution).
  • Against: Each develops a New Testament image; Paul uses several side by side (Romans 3:25; 5:8; Colossians 2:15).
  • Against: They answer different questions: what is removed (guilt), defeated (evil), awakened (love).
  • Judge it by: Are they models (pictures) or literal mechanisms? Does each fit God as both just and loving? Does any make God cruel?
  • Spec issues: "the extent to which the three theories … are contradictory"; "suggest that the Christian God is cruel".
Quick check
?A critic says: "If God demands a death before he will forgive, God is cruel." Which reply comes from inside the Christus Victor tradition?
Mission check

?A hospice chaplain tells a dying man who fears death: "On the cross and in the resurrection, death itself was beaten; it no longer has the last word." Which model is she mainly using?

The same cross, several pictures: sacrifice, victory, satisfaction, substitution, example. Christians choose which to stress, often depending on the need in front of them.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Words like Father and King are anthropomorphic: analogies or metaphors. Mainstream Christian teaching holds that God has no sex.
  • "Father" brings intimacy, trust and belonging, but can hurt those with painful experiences of fathers and has been used to support male authority.
  • McFague (Models of God, 1987): God as Mother, Lover, Friend; the world as God's body.
  • Daly (Beyond God the Father, 1973): "if God is male, then the male is God".

Mission 2

  • Arius: the Son is the first, highest creature ("there was when he was not"). Athanasius: only God can re-create and save, so the Son is fully God.
  • Nicaea (325): the Son is homoousios, "of one substance with the Father", "begotten, not made".

Mission 3

  • Christians believe in one God in three co-equal, co-eternal persons; not three gods, not three modes, not ranked.
  • Biblical basis: the threefold name (Matthew 28:19; 2 Corinthians 13:14), the divinity of Son and Spirit, and Jewish monotheism (Deuteronomy 6:4).
  • Paul places Jesus inside the one God (1 Corinthians 8:6).
  • Nicaea (325) and Constantinople (381) defined it; the West's filioque helped divide East and West.

Mission 4

  • Impassibility: God is not changed or made to suffer by anything outside God; classical theology read the Bible's "feeling" texts as anthropomorphic.
  • Moltmann (The Crucified God, 1972): after Auschwitz, God suffers in the cross: the Son dies forsaken, the Father grieves; a God who cannot suffer cannot love.

Mission 5

  • Atonement = reconciliation of God and humanity; soteriology = the study of salvation.
  • Models: sacrifice; victory (Aulén, Christus Victor); satisfaction and substitution (Anselm; penal substitution); moral example (Abelard).
  • The theories rest on assumptions about sin, forgiveness, and God's wrath and love; many Christians combine them.
