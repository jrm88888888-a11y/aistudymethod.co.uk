Before you start

You need from the core lesson

  • Rate = change in concentration per unit time, in mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹.
  • Particles react only if they collide with E ≥ Ea and the right orientation.
  • K = products over reactants, each concentration raised to its coefficient.
  • Only temperature changes K.

Mission 1

Elementary steps

  • Most reactions happen in a series of elementary steps: single collisions or break-ups.
  • The sequence of steps is the mechanism.
  • The steps must add up to the overall equation.
  • They must also fit the rate equation found by experiment.
  • The slowest step is the rate-determining step (RDS).
Mission 1

Intermediates and transition states

  • An intermediate forms in one step and is used up in a later one.
  • It sits in an energy minimum, so it can, in principle, be detected.
  • A transition state sits at an energy maximum: bonds partly made, partly broken.
  • A transition state cannot be isolated.
  • Intermediates cancel when the steps are added, so they never appear in the overall equation.
Quick check
?Which statement correctly distinguishes a reaction intermediate from a transition state in a multistep reaction?
Mission 1

Molecularity

  • Molecularity: the number of reacting particles in one elementary step.
  • Unimolecular: one particle. (CH₃)₃CBr → (CH₃)₃C⁺ + Br⁻.
  • Bimolecular: two. (CH₃)₃C⁺ + OH⁻ → (CH₃)₃COH.
  • Termolecular: three at once. Rare, because three particles seldom collide together.
  • Count particles, not species: 2X → X₂ is bimolecular. Never apply it to an overall equation.
Quick check
?Which statement about molecularity is correct?
Mission 1

How the mechanism sets the rate equation

  • The rate equation contains the reactants of the RDS and of any fast step before it.
  • If the slow step is first, the orders match the particles in that step.
  • SN1 example: slow first step, so rate = k[(CH₃)₃CBr]. OH⁻ reacts later: zero order.
  • Intermediates never appear: replace one by the reactants that formed it.
  • Species that react only after the RDS do not appear.
Mission 1

When the slow step is not first

  • Overall: P + 2Q → PQ₂.
  • Step 1: P + Q ⇌ PQ (fast). Step 2: PQ + Q → PQ₂ (slow).
  • The intermediate PQ is made from P and Q, so [PQ] is proportional to [P][Q].
  • Rate = k[PQ][Q] becomes rate = k[P][Q]²: third order overall.
  • The slow step is bimolecular, but the reaction is third order. The two need not match.
Quick check
?A reaction has the overall equation 2A + B → C + D. A student states that its rate equation must be rate = k[A]²[B]. Which response is correct?
Quick check
?The overall equation for a reaction is 2X + Y → X₂Y. The experimental rate equation is rate = k[X][Y]. Which mechanism is consistent with both?
Mission 1

Energy profile of a multistep reaction

  • One maximum (transition state) per step; one minimum per intermediate.
  • Each step's Ea runs from the species entering that step up to its own maximum.
  • A later step starts from the intermediate's dip, not from the reactants.
  • The step with the largest Ea (usually also the highest peak) is rate-determining.
  • From kinetic data: decide which step is slow, then draw its climb as the larger one.

Mission 2

The rate equation

  • rate = k[A]ᵐ[B]ⁿ. m and n are the orders with respect to A and B.
  • Overall order = m + n.
  • Orders are found by experiment. They need not match the coefficients.
  • Usually only reactants appear, plus sometimes a catalyst. Products do not normally appear.

Mission 2

Units of k

  • k = rate ÷ (concentration terms). Work out units by cancelling, not memorizing.
  • Second order: (mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹) ÷ (mol dm⁻³)² = mol⁻¹ dm³ s⁻¹.
  • Zero: mol dm⁻³ s⁻¹. First: s⁻¹. Third: mol⁻² dm⁶ s⁻¹.
  • k depends on temperature, not on concentration.

Mission 3

Concentration–time graphs

  • Zero order: straight line sloping down. The rate is constant.
  • First order: a curve. [A] falls by the same fraction in equal times.
  • Second order: steeper at first, then flattens more. Each halving takes longer than the last.
  • The rate at any moment is the gradient of the tangent (made positive).
Mission 3

Rate–concentration graphs

  • Zero order: a horizontal line. Rate does not depend on [A].
  • First order: a straight line through the origin. Gradient = k.
  • Second order: a curve rising more and more steeply from the origin.
  • Rate against [A]² is a straight line for second order.
  • Don't carry the concentration–time shape across: these are different graphs.

Mission 4

k rises with temperature

  • k is constant only at one temperature. It rises as T rises.
  • Arrhenius equation: k = Ae^(−Ea/RT) (in the data booklet).
  • e^(−Ea/RT) is the fraction of collisions with E ≥ Ea.
  • Ea in J mol⁻¹, R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹, T in kelvin.
Mission 4

The straight-line form

  • Take natural logs: ln k = −Ea/RT + ln A (in the data booklet).
  • That is y = mx + c, with y = ln k and x = 1/T.
  • Gradient = −Ea/R, so Ea = −gradient × R.
  • y-intercept = ln A.
  • The line slopes down: higher T means smaller 1/T and larger ln k.
Quick check
?The rate constant of a reaction varies with absolute temperature according to the Arrhenius equation, k = Ae^(−Ea/RT). A student plots ln k (y-axis) against 1/T (x-axis) for one reaction over a range of temperatures. Which describes the graph?
Mission 4

Ea and A from a graph (worked example)

  • Two points on the line (k in s⁻¹): (3.00 × 10⁻³ K⁻¹, −6.00) and (3.40 × 10⁻³ K⁻¹, −9.60).
  • Gradient = (−9.60 − (−6.00)) ÷ (0.40 × 10⁻³) = −9.00 × 10³ K.
  • Ea = 9.00 × 10³ × 8.31 = 7.48 × 10⁴ J mol⁻¹ = 74.8 kJ mol⁻¹.
  • The intercept is far off the graph, so use a point: ln A = −6.00 + 9.00 × 10³ × 3.00 × 10⁻³ = 21.0.
  • A = e²¹·⁰ = 1.3 × 10⁹ s⁻¹ (A has the units of k).
Calculate
1An Arrhenius plot has ln k on the y-axis and 1/T / 10⁻³ K⁻¹ on the x-axis. Two points on the line are (3.10, −4.20) and (3.30, −6.70). Calculate Ea, in kJ mol⁻¹. R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.
Mission 4

Two temperatures, no graph

  • Write ln k = −Ea/RT + ln A at T₁ and T₂, then subtract: ln A cancels.
  • ln(k₂/k₁) = (Ea/R)(1/T₁ − 1/T₂).
  • Use natural logs and kelvin. Ea comes out in J mol⁻¹.
  • Convert to kJ mol⁻¹ only at the end.
  • Not a booklet equation: write the linear form twice and subtract. With more than two points, use the graph.
Quick check
?The rate constant of a first-order reaction is 2.00 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹ at 25.0 °C and 8.00 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹ at 45.0 °C. Using ln k = −Ea/RT + ln A and R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹, what is the activation energy of the reaction?
Mission 4

What A means

  • A, the Arrhenius factor, accounts for the frequency of collisions with the proper orientation.
  • A stricter orientation requirement means a smaller A.
  • A is not the fraction with enough energy: that is e^(−Ea/RT).
  • Find A from the intercept, or from one point: ln A = ln k + Ea/RT.
Quick check
?A first-order reaction has Ea = 100 kJ mol⁻¹, and its rate constant at 127 °C is 3.0 × 10⁻³ s⁻¹. Using k = Ae^(−Ea/RT) and R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹, what is the Arrhenius factor, A?
Quick check
?Reactions 1 and 2 have the same Arrhenius factor, A. Reaction 1 has the larger activation energy. Both are plotted as ln k against 1/T on the same axes. Which statement is correct?
Mission check

?A student times a cricket's chirps at several temperatures. Which treatment of the data gives an apparent activation energy?

Yes, roughly. Chirping is driven by chemical reactions in the cricket's body. Over a limited range of temperatures, ln(chirp rate) against 1/T is close to a straight line. Its gradient gives an apparent Ea of roughly 50 kJ mol⁻¹ for the whole process, not for one reaction. The idea comes from chemist Keith Laidler (1972).

Mission 5

The reaction quotient, Q

  • Q has the same form as K, but uses concentrations at any moment.
  • Q < K: too little product. Net forward reaction until Q = K.
  • Q > K: too much product. Net backward reaction.
  • Q = K: at equilibrium; no net change.
  • Divide amounts by volume first. Moles work only if the volumes cancel.
Mission 5

Q in action (worked example)

  • CH₃COOH + C₂H₅OH ⇌ CH₃COOC₂H₅ + H₂O, all in one liquid mixture; K ≈ 4.0.
  • One mixture holds 1.0 mol acid, 1.0 mol ethanol, 1.5 mol ester and 1.5 mol water.
  • Two terms on top and two below, so the volume cancels: Q = (1.5 × 1.5) ÷ (1.0 × 1.0) = 2.25.
  • Q < K: net forward reaction; more ester forms.

Mission 5

When K is very small

  • Very little reactant reacts, so [reactant]eqm ≈ [reactant]initial.
  • Then solve for the product directly. No quadratic needed.
  • Z₂(g) ⇌ 2Z(g), K = 4.0 × 10⁻⁸, [Z₂]initial = 0.10 mol dm⁻³.
  • [Z]² = K × [Z₂] = 4.0 × 10⁻⁸ × 0.10 = 4.0 × 10⁻⁹, so [Z] = 6.3 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³.
  • Check: only 3.2 × 10⁻⁵ mol dm⁻³ of the 0.10 mol dm⁻³ Z₂ reacted. The approximation holds.
Calculate
1XY(g) ⇌ X(g) + Y(g) has K = 2.5 × 10⁻⁷ at temperature T. A vessel at T starts with [XY] = 0.40 mol dm⁻³ and no X or Y. Calculate [X] at equilibrium, in mol dm⁻³.
Mission 5

K and ΔG⦵

  • ΔG⦵ = −RT ln K (in the data booklet). ΔG⦵ in J mol⁻¹, T in kelvin.
  • ΔG⦵ < 0 → K > 1, products favoured. ΔG⦵ > 0 → K < 1. ΔG⦵ = 0 → K = 1.
  • Rearranged: K = e^(−ΔG⦵/RT).
  • Worked: K = 1.0 × 10³ at 298 K. ΔG⦵ = −8.31 × 298 × ln(1.0 × 10³) = −1.71 × 10⁴ J mol⁻¹ = −17.1 kJ mol⁻¹.
Quick check
?For a reaction at 227 °C, ΔG⦵ = −8.00 kJ mol⁻¹. Use ΔG⦵ = −RT ln K, with R = 8.31 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. What is the value of K at this temperature?

Recap

The big ideas

  • The rate equation reflects the slow step and any fast steps before it. Orders come from experiment.
  • Intermediates sit in dips; transition states sit at peaks. The largest climb is the slow step.
  • Orders 0, 1, 2: doubling gives rate × 1, × 2, × 4. Units of k come from cancelling.
  • ln k against 1/T: gradient −Ea/R, intercept ln A.
  • Q < K: net forward. Q > K: net backward. ICE rows follow the coefficients.
  • ΔG⦵ = −RT ln K: negative ΔG⦵ means K > 1.
