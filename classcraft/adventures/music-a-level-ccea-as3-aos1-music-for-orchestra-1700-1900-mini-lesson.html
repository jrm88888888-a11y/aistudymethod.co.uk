Music for Orchestra, 1700–1900

This is one of the three compulsory Areas of Study in CCEA AS 3 (Responding to Music). It asks you to listen to and appraise orchestral music across two centuries — the Baroque, Classical and Romantic periods — and to write in detail about style, form, harmony, texture and the use of orchestral resources.

Context · genres & periods

The orchestra and its genres

Across 1700–1900 the orchestra grew from a small Baroque band into a huge Romantic ensemble. Two genres dominate this Area of Study:

  • The concerto — a solo instrument (or small group) set against the orchestra. A solo concerto pits one soloist against the tutti; a concerto grosso contrasts a small group (the concertino) with the full band (the ripieno).
  • The symphony — a large multi-movement work for full orchestra, usually in four movements, that grew in scale and ambition through the Classical and Romantic eras.

Listen for the story of the orchestra: Baroque = strings plus harpsichord continuo; Classical = added pairs of woodwind, horns and timpani; Romantic = a vastly expanded body with a large brass and percussion section and valved horns able to play in every key.

Style · Baroque (c.1700–1750)

Baroque orchestral style

Baroque music is built on the basso continuo — a bass line played by cello or bassoon with a chord-playing instrument (harpsichord or organ) improvising harmony above it. Other fingerprints to listen for:

  • Terraced dynamics — the volume changes in sudden steps (loud tutti, quiet solo), not gradual swells.
  • Ritornello form — a recurring orchestral tutti theme (the ritornello) returns in different keys, alternating with contrasting solo episodes. This is the standard shape of a fast concerto movement.
  • Sequences (a phrase repeated at higher or lower pitch), driving walking bass lines, and busy ornamentation (trills, acciaccaturas).
  • Often contrapuntal (polyphonic) texture with independent interweaving lines.

Exam anchor: if a fast movement keeps bringing back the same tutti idea between solo passages, name the form as ritornello, not rondo.

Style · Classical (c.1750–1810)

Classical orchestral style

The Classical style prizes balance, clarity and elegance. Textures become mostly homophonic — a clear tune over an accompaniment — and the continuo fades away as the orchestra fills in its own harmony. Key features:

  • Periodic phrasing — tidy, balanced phrases in question-and-answer pairs (an antecedent phrase answered by a consequent), often four bars plus four bars.
  • Alberti bass — a broken-chord accompaniment (lowest, highest, middle, highest) that keeps the harmony gently moving.
  • Graduated dynamics — real crescendos and diminuendos, including the famous "Mannheim crescendo".
  • Sonata form as the standard first-movement design (see the next screen).

Compare: Baroque terraced dynamics jump in steps; Classical dynamics swell and fade. Hearing a smooth crescendo already rules out a purely Baroque style.

Form · sonata form

Sonata form in three stages

Sonata form is the great Classical structure. It has three main sections:

  • Exposition — a first subject in the tonic key, a bridge (transition) that modulates, then a contrasting second subject in a related key (the dominant, or the relative major if the movement is minor).
  • Development — the themes are broken up, combined and pushed through many keys, building tension.
  • Recapitulation — both subjects return, but now both in the tonic, often rounded off by a coda.

Harmony · cadences

The four cadences

A cadence is the pair of chords that punctuates the end of a phrase. Learn all four by sound and by chord numbers:

  • Perfect — V–I. Sounds finished and conclusive, like a full stop.
  • Plagal — IV–I. The gentle "Amen" cadence, also conclusive but softer.
  • Imperfect — ends on V (often I–V, ii–V or IV–V). Sounds unfinished, like a comma or question.
  • Interrupted — V–vi. A surprise: the ear expects V–I but lands on the minor chord vi instead.

Bonus colour: a tierce de Picardie is a major chord ending a piece that was otherwise in a minor key — a bright final surprise common in Baroque and earlier music.

Elements · texture

Describing texture

Texture is how many layers of sound there are and how they relate. Use the correct term when you appraise:

  • Monophonic — a single unaccompanied line (or everyone in unison).
  • Homophonic — one clear melody with chordal accompaniment. The Classical default.
  • Polyphonic / contrapuntal — two or more independent melodic lines woven together. Common in the Baroque.
  • Imitation — one part states an idea and another copies it shortly after; a fugue is a strict imitative form.

Style · Romantic (c.1810–1900)

Romantic orchestral style

The Romantic orchestra is bigger, richer and more expressive. Composers stretch the earlier forms and reach for intense emotion. Listen for:

  • Chromaticism — notes from outside the key colouring the melody and harmony, creating longing and instability.
  • A huge dynamic range, from the barest whisper to overwhelming climaxes, and expressive rubato (flexible timing).
  • A greatly expanded orchestra: more brass and percussion, valved horns and trumpets, and richly divided strings.
  • Long, singing melodies and adventurous modulation to distant keys.

Continuity and change: a Romantic concerto still uses the Classical inheritance of sonata design and soloist-versus-orchestra contrast — but drenched in chromatic harmony and a far wider palette of colour.

Appraising · putting it together

Listening like an examiner

When you appraise an unfamiliar extract from this Area of Study, run a quick mental checklist:

  • Dynamics — terraced (steps) suggests Baroque; graduated swells suggest Classical or later.
  • Continuo — a chugging harpsichord underneath everything points firmly to the Baroque.
  • Harmony — clear tonic-and-dominant simplicity = Classical; heavy chromaticism and remote modulation = Romantic.
  • Resources — a small string band = early; prominent trombones, tuba, harp and large percussion = late Romantic.

Always justify. Never just name a period — say why: "Romantic, because of the chromatic harmony, wide dynamic range and expanded brass."

Recap

The big ideas to know

Periods: Baroque (continuo, terraced dynamics, ritornello) · Classical (homophony, Alberti bass, periodic phrasing, sonata form) · Romantic (chromaticism, expanded orchestra, wide dynamics, remote modulation)

Genres: concerto (solo concerto & concerto grosso) · symphony

Forms: ritornello (Baroque concerto) · sonata form (exposition–development–recapitulation) · variation · sonata rondo

Cadences: perfect V–I · plagal IV–I · imperfect (ends on V) · interrupted V–vi · tierce de Picardie

Texture: monophonic · homophonic · polyphonic/contrapuntal · imitation/fugue
