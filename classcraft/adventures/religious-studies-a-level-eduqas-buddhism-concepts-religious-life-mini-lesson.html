Make a guess: If even a happy day counts as suffering, is Buddhism a gloomy religion?

Mission 1

Three marks of existence

  • The Buddha taught three marks (Pali tilakkhana) of conditioned existence:
  • anicca: impermanence; dukkha: suffering, unsatisfactoriness; anatta: not-self (see Mission 2).
  • The classic summary (Dhammapada 277–279): all conditioned things (sankhara) are impermanent; all conditioned things are dukkha; all dhammas are not-self.
  • Note the shift: dhammas is wider than sankhara. For Theravada, nibbana is unconditioned: not impermanent, not dukkha, but still not-self.
  • Eduqas calls the marks the three lakshanas.
Mission 1

Translating dukkha

  • No English word fits. Common translations:
  • suffering (the usual one); unsatisfactoriness; pain, ill; stress (Thanissaro Bhikkhu).
  • "Suffering" is too strong for a mild let-down; "unsatisfactoriness" is too weak for grief or torture. Dukkha covers both.
  • A traditional explanation (folk etymology, not certain): du- (bad) + kha (axle-hole): a wheel that fits badly, so the ride always jars.
  • The Buddhist point is not that every moment hurts, but that nothing conditioned can give lasting, secure satisfaction.
Mission 1Read the source

The first truth at the Deer Park

Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta ("Setting the Wheel of the Dhamma in Motion"), Samyutta Nikaya 56.11 (paraphrase): the noble truth of dukkha is this: birth, ageing, sickness and death are dukkha; sorrow, grief, pain and despair are dukkha; being joined with what is unpleasant, being parted from what is pleasant, and not getting what one wants are dukkha. In brief, the five clinging-aggregates are dukkha.

And Dhammapada 278 (Müller, 1881):

> "'All created things are grief and pain,' he who knows and sees this becomes passive in pain; this is the way that leads to purity."

  • "Passive in pain" renders a Pali word meaning turns away from it, grows disenchanted: seeing dukkha is a step to freedom, not despair.

Q1 Which items on the list can no one avoid, however lucky?

Show answer

Birth, ageing, death; parting from what is pleasant. Sickness for almost everyone.

Q2 Why does the list end with the five aggregates?

Show answer

Dukkha is not just bad events: it is built into the conditioned, grasping personality itself.

  • At the end of the sermon Kondañña, one of the five listeners, sees that whatever is subject to arising is subject to ceasing (paraphrase): anicca in one line.
  • This is the sutta your spec names for dukkha and anicca: the first truth gives dukkha; Kondañña's insight gives anicca.
Mission 1

Three kinds of dukkha

  • Dukkha-dukkha: the dukkha of pain itself: physical and mental pain, illness, grief.
  • Viparinama-dukkha: the dukkha of change: pleasant things end, so even happiness carries loss.
  • Sankhara-dukkha: the dukkha of conditioned existence: everything depends on causes that shift, so nothing gives a secure footing. The subtlest kind; often seen only through insight meditation.
  • Source: e.g. Dukkhata Sutta (Samyutta Nikaya 38.14); explained by Buddhaghosa, Visuddhimagga XVI.
  • Eduqas's glosses: painful experience; changing nature of existence; the contingent nature of existence.

Mission 1

Anicca: gross and subtle

  • Gross (obvious) impermanence: seasons turn, bodies age, buildings crumble, kingdoms fall, everyone dies.
  • Subtle or momentary impermanence: every bodily and mental event arises and ceases moment by moment. What looks solid is a fast series, like the frames of a film or a flame renewed each instant.
  • The Abhidhamma and its commentators, including Buddhaghosa (5th century CE, Visuddhimagga, "The Path of Purification"), analyse experience into momentary events.
  • In Buddhaghosa's account of insight, the meditator comes to see formations breaking up moment by moment (Visuddhimagga XXI, paraphrase).
  • Anicca is not bad news in itself: change also makes growth, healing and awakening possible.
Mission 1

How the marks link

  • The Buddha's own chain (e.g. Samyutta Nikaya 22.15, paraphrase): what is impermanent is dukkha; what is dukkha is not-self.
  • Anicca → dukkha: change is not painful by itself; dukkha comes from craving and clinging to what changes.
  • Dukkha → anatta: what is unstable and beyond our control cannot be a lasting self ("this is mine, this I am, this is my self").
  • So the three marks are one insight from three angles, not three separate facts.

Mission 1

Is Buddhism pessimistic?

  • The charge: the first teaching is suffering; even pleasure is counted as dukkha; the goal is to end rebirth. Arthur Schopenhauer (1788–1860, The World as Will and Representation, 1818/1844) admired Buddhism as agreeing with his pessimism. Many 19th-century Western readers took nibbana to mean annihilation.
  • The reply: Walpola Rahula (1907–1997), What the Buddha Taught (1959): Buddhism is "neither pessimistic nor optimistic" but realistic (short phrase).
  • Like a doctor, the Buddha names an illness in order to cure it: the third truth says dukkha can end.
  • Joy has a place: calm and happiness in meditation; "Nirvana the highest happiness" (Dhammapada 204, Müller).
Mission 1Evaluate

Do the three marks describe reality?

  • Claim: The three marks are an accurate account of reality.
  • For: Science finds change everywhere: cells, stars, even "solid" matter.
  • For: Experience confirms that pleasures fade and that we cannot control body or mind.
  • Against: Some things seem lasting in human terms (mathematical truths, love that endures); "all is dukkha" seems overstated.
  • Against: If all is impermanent, what about nibbana? Theravada answers: nibbana is unconditioned, so outside the first two marks. Critics say the system then has one exception.
  • Judgement criteria: Is the claim descriptive or a tool for practice? Does it fit experience in meditation as well as ordinary life?
  • Your spec's issues: the three lakshanas "as representative of reality" and as "the most important teachings".

Mission 2

What anatta denies

  • Anatta (Sanskrit anatman): not-self, the third mark of existence (see Mission 1).
  • In the Brahmanical thought of the Buddha's day (e.g. the Upanishads), the atman is an eternal, unchanging self, the true owner of experience, one with ultimate reality (Brahman).
  • The Buddha taught that no such self is found in any part of experience: nothing permanent, independent and fully under our control.
  • Anatta does not say people do not exist. "Ruth" is a real, changing person; what is denied is a fixed core behind her.
Mission 2

The five khandhas

  • A person is analysed into five khandhas ("heaps", "aggregates"; Sanskrit skandhas):
  • rupa: form: the body and the physical world it meets.
  • vedana: feeling: each experience as pleasant, unpleasant or neutral.
  • sanna: perception: recognising and labelling ("red", "friend").
  • sankhara: mental formations: will, habits, intentions; this is where karma is made.
  • vinnana: consciousness: awareness through the six senses (the mind counts as the sixth).
  • Buddhists teach that all five are impermanent and depend on each other, and that none of them, and nothing beyond them, is a self.
Mission 2Read the source

Nagasena's chariot

The Questions of King Milinda (Milinda Panha), Book II, chapter 1, section 1 (paraphrase). The Greek king Milinda (Menander I, 2nd century BCE) meets the monk Nagasena.

  • Nagasena: "I am called Nagasena, but that is only a name, a way of speaking. No permanent individual is found here."
  • The king objects: then who keeps the precepts, who meditates, who reaches nibbana? Is the hair Nagasena? The body? Feeling, perception, formations, consciousness? All together? Something apart from them? To each, Nagasena says no. "Then 'Nagasena' is an empty sound!"
  • Nagasena asks how the king came. "By chariot." Is the pole the chariot? The axle? The wheels? The frame? The yoke? All together? Something apart? No to each. "Then 'chariot' is an empty sound!"
  • The king: "No: 'chariot' is the name used because of all these parts." Nagasena: "Just so: because of the body and the five khandhas, I am called Nagasena."
  • He quotes the nun Vajira (Samyutta Nikaya 5.10): as "chariot" is said when the parts come together, so "a being" is said when the khandhas are present.

Q1 Does Nagasena say that he does not exist?

Show answer

No: "Nagasena" is a correct name for a real, changing process; there is just no separate owner.

Mission 3

The Wheel of Life

  • Samsara: the cycle of birth, death and rebirth, driven by karma and craving, with no first beginning that can be found.
  • Tibetan Buddhists paint it as the Wheel of Life (Sanskrit bhavachakra, "wheel of becoming"), often at a monastery entrance.
  • From the centre out: hub (three poisons) → ring of beings rising and falling → six realms → rim of twelve links (see Mission 5).
  • The wheel is gripped by Yama, lord of death: all of samsara is subject to impermanence and death.
  • Outside the wheel, in the upper corner, a figure points to the moon or a Dhamma wheel: release is possible.
  • Your spec: rebirth "with reference to the iconography of the bhavachakra".
Mission 3

The hub: three poisons

  • At the centre, three animals chase each other, each biting the next one's tail:
  • cockerel: greed, craving (Pali lobha or raga);
  • snake: hatred, aversion (dosa; Sanskrit dvesha);
  • pig: delusion, ignorance (moha).
  • Delusion is often called the root: not seeing anicca and anatta, beings grasp and push away.
  • The Buddha's Fire Sermon (Samyutta Nikaya 35.28, paraphrase): everything is burning with the fire of passion, the fire of aversion, the fire of delusion.
  • Their opposites are the wholesome roots: generosity, loving-kindness, wisdom.
Mission 3

Rising and falling

  • Around the hub, a ring split in two:
  • light half: beings rising to higher rebirths through skilful (kusala) actions;
  • dark half: beings falling to lower rebirths through unskilful (akusala) actions.
  • In the painting, and in Buddhist teaching, karma moves beings up and down the wheel, but never off it. Even the highest rebirth is still samsara.
  • Leaving the wheel needs nibbana: uprooting the three poisons, not just earning good karma.
Mission 3

The six realms

  • As Buddhists describe them:
  • Gods (devas): long, blissful lives; risk pride and complacency; they fall when merit runs out.
  • Titans (asuras): powerful but driven by envy; endless conflict with the gods.
  • Humans: a mix of pleasure and pain; the best realm for liberation.
  • Animals: driven by instinct, fear and ignorance; little chance to practise.
  • Hungry ghosts (pretas): huge bellies, tiny throats: craving that can never be satisfied.
  • Hell beings: intense pain, linked to hatred and cruelty. Hells are not eternal: beings leave when the karma is used up.
  • A common Tibetan pairing: gods–pride, titans–jealousy, humans–desire, animals–ignorance, hungry ghosts–greed, hells–anger.
  • Many Buddhists read the realms both as places of rebirth and as states of mind we pass through now: craving can be the ghost realm, rage the hell realm.

For many Buddhists, the wheel works on two scales at once: lives across time, and moods within a day. Either way, the poisons at the hub keep it turning.

Mission 4

Karma is intention

  • Karma (Pali kamma) means action: in Buddhist teaching, intentional action.
  • The Buddha (Anguttara Nikaya 6.63, paraphrase): it is intention (cetana) that I call karma; having intended, one acts by body, speech and mind.
  • Karma is not fate. It is what you are doing now, and it shapes what you will become.
  • Contrast: the Jains of the Buddha's day treated karma as a kind of matter that sticks to the soul even through unintended acts. The Buddha put intention at the centre.
Mission 4

Seeds and fruits

  • In Buddhist teaching, an intentional act plants a seed; its fruit (vipaka) ripens later: in this life, the next, or a later one.
  • Kusala (skilful, wholesome) acts spring from generosity, kindness and wisdom; they bring happiness and good rebirths. Merit (punna; Sanskrit punya) is their store of good results.
  • Akusala (unskilful, unwholesome) acts spring from greed, hatred and delusion (see Mission 3); they bring suffering and lower rebirths.
  • Which acts make karma? Intentional acts of body, speech and mind. A truly accidental act makes little or none.
  • An arhat's acts make no new karma: with no craving left, there is no seed to sprout. Old karma can still ripen in the arhat's body until death.
Mission 4Read the source

The Dhammapada on karma

From Chapter 1, "The Twin-Verses" (Dhammapada 1, Müller, 1881):

> "All that we are is the result of what we have thought: it is founded on our thoughts, it is made up of our thoughts. If a man speaks or acts with an evil thought, pain follows him, as the wheel follows the foot of the ox that draws the carriage."

Verse 2 (paraphrase): with a pure thought, happiness follows "like a shadow". From Chapter 9, "Evil" (Dhammapada 127, Müller, 1881):

> "Not in the sky, not in the midst of the sea, not if we enter into the clefts of the mountains, is there known a spot in the whole world where a man might be freed from an evil deed."

Show answer

Q2 What does verse 127 rule out?

Show answer

Escaping karma by place or hiding; there is no outside judge to avoid, only results.

Mission 4

Rebirth without a soul

  • Rebirth is not a soul moving house (reincarnation of an atman). With anatta (see Mission 2), there is no traveller.
  • Buddhists explain that what continues is a stream of causes: the khandhas of one life condition the khandhas of the next, shaped by karma and craving.
  • The Questions of King Milinda, Book II, chapter 2 (paraphrase): Milinda asks whether the one reborn is the same or another. Nagasena: neither the same nor another.
  • His lamp: a flame burns all night. The flame of the last watch is not the same flame as the first, nor a different one: it depends on it.
  • His milk: milk becomes curds, then butter, then ghee: not the same, yet not unconnected.

Mission 4Evaluate

Do karma and rebirth describe reality?

  • Claim: The teachings of karma and rebirth are an accurate account of reality.
  • For: They give a moral order without a judging God: actions have results.
  • For: Some Buddhists point to meditators' reports and to Ian Stevenson's (1918–2007) studies of children's claimed past-life memories (Twenty Cases Suggestive of Reincarnation, 1966). Critics dispute his method.
  • Against: No known mechanism carries karmic results across lives; without a self, it is hard to say who receives them.
  • Against: Some modern Buddhists, e.g. Stephen Batchelor (b. 1953, Buddhism Without Beliefs, 1997), are agnostic about rebirth and say the path works without it.
  • Judgement criteria: What kind of evidence fits a claim about many lives? Is karma within one life enough for practice? Does dropping rebirth change the Four Noble Truths?
  • Your spec's issue: these teachings "as representative of reality".

Mission 5

Everything depends on conditions

  • Dependent origination (Pali paticcasamuppada; Sanskrit pratityasamutpada): Buddhists teach that nothing arises on its own; everything arises in dependence on conditions.
  • The Buddha's formula (e.g. Samyutta Nikaya 12.21, paraphrase): when this exists, that comes to be; with the arising of this, that arises. When this does not exist, that does not come to be; with the cessation of this, that ceases.
  • It explains samsara (how suffering and rebirth keep arising) and nibbana (how they can cease) without a first cause or a creator.
  • Majjhima Nikaya 28 (paraphrase): whoever sees dependent origination sees the Dhamma.
  • Your spec writes pratityasamutpada.
Mission 5

The twelve links

  • The best-known form has twelve links (nidanas, "causes"), each a condition for the next:
  • (1) ignorance (avijja) → (2) formations (sankhara: karmic intentions) → (3) consciousness (vinnana) → (4) name-and-form (namarupa: mind and body) → (5) six sense bases → (6) contact (phassa) →
  • (7) feeling (vedana) → (8) craving (tanha) → (9) grasping (upadana) → (10) becoming (bhava) → (11) birth (jati) → (12) ageing and death (jaramarana), with sorrow and despair.
  • Read backwards, it traces dukkha to its root, ignorance. The Buddha also teaches it in reverse: with the cessation of ignorance, formations cease … and so all dukkha ceases.
Mission 5

The rim of the Wheel of Life

  • On the bhavachakra (see Mission 3), the twelve links are pictured around the rim, clockwise:
  • (1) a blind person with a stick; (2) a potter shaping pots; (3) a monkey swinging from branch to branch; (4) people in a boat; (5) a house with six windows; (6) a couple embracing;
  • (7) an arrow in an eye; (8) a person drinking; (9) a person picking fruit; (10) a pregnant woman; (11) a woman giving birth; (12) a person carrying a corpse.
  • Each image is a memory aid: e.g. the monkey is a restless consciousness grabbing one object after another.
  • Details vary between paintings (e.g. link 10 is sometimes a couple).

Mission 5

Three lives or this moment?

  • Three-lives reading (Buddhaghosa, 5th century CE, Visuddhimagga XVII): links 1–2 belong to the past life; links 3–10 to the present; links 11–12 to the future. This is the classic Theravada interpretation.
  • Present-moment reading: the Thai reformer Buddhadasa Bhikkhu (1906–1993) taught that the whole chain can turn within one moment of experience: "birth" is the birth of the sense of "I" and "mine".
  • Many Mahayana teachers stress mutual dependence: all things condition each other (a step towards emptiness).
  • The readings can be combined: the same pattern runs across lives and within a single moment.
Mission 5

Where the chain can be broken

  • Ignorance is the root: wisdom (seeing anicca, dukkha, anatta) ends it at its source.
  • In daily practice, many teachers point to the gap between feeling (7) and craving (8).
  • Feeling arises from past conditions; you cannot simply stop it. But craving need not follow: a mindful person can notice a pleasant or painful feeling without grasping at it or pushing it away.
  • Where that gap is held, grasping, becoming and the rest do not arise.
  • This links the chain to the three poisons: rooting out greed, hatred and delusion is cutting the links that feed the wheel.
  • Your spec: "the importance of rooting out of greed, hatred and delusion".

Mission 5Read the source

The poisoned arrow

Cula-Malunkyaputta Sutta, Majjhima Nikaya 63 (paraphrase):

  • The monk Malunkyaputta threatens to leave unless the Buddha answers: Is the world eternal or not? Finite or infinite? Is the soul the same as the body or different? Does a Tathagata (an awakened one) exist after death, not exist, both, or neither?
  • The Buddha replies with a parable: a man is shot with a poisoned arrow. He refuses the surgeon until he knows who shot him, the man's clan, name, height and skin colour, his village, and what the bow, string, shaft and feathers were made of. He would die before he found out.
  • These questions are left undeclared because answering them does not lead to calm, insight and nibbana. What the Buddha has declared: dukkha, its origin, its cessation and the path.

Q1 What does the arrow stand for, and the surgeon?

Show answer

Dukkha, here and now; the Buddha and his teaching as the cure.

Q2 Is the existence of God among the ten questions?

Show answer

No: they concern the world, the soul and the Tathagata. Your spec extends the lesson to God and other metaphysical questions.

Mission 5Evaluate

Is it legitimate to set aside questions about God?

  • Claim: Buddhism is right to set aside questions about the existence of God.
  • For: Like the arrow, such questions do not help end suffering; speculation can harden into views to cling to.
  • For: Dependent origination explains arising without needing a first cause.
  • For: Pali texts present gods, even Great Brahma, as beings within samsara; in the Brahmajala Sutta (Digha Nikaya 1) Brahma wrongly believes he created others.
  • Against: Questions of ultimate origin and meaning are basic human questions; setting them aside is not answering them.
  • Against: Buddhism still makes large claims (rebirth, karma, nibbana) that are hard to verify, so its "practical" stance is not neutral.
  • Against: Theistic philosophers argue that a chain of dependent things needs an uncaused ground (a cosmological argument).
  • Judgement criteria: Is the Buddha's silence agnostic, or a quiet denial of a creator? Does the path work without an answer? Is it fair to compare Buddhism with theistic religions on this point?
  • Your spec's issue: "the legitimacy of rejecting questions about the existence of God".

Mission 6

Two goals: better rebirth and nirvana

  • Nirvana (Pali nibbana): the end of greed, hatred and delusion, and so of rebirth. The ultimate goal.
  • Better rebirth: a happier human life or a heaven realm, gained by merit (punya): giving, keeping precepts, devotion. A proximate goal.
  • Many lay Buddhists in Theravada countries aim mainly at better rebirth; many monastics aim at nirvana.
  • Anthropologist Melford Spiro, Buddhism and Society (1970), called these nibbanic and kammatic Buddhism (plus "apotropaic": protective ritual).
  • Most Buddhists see them as stages on one road, not rivals: a good rebirth gives the chance to practise; merit supports the path.
Mission 6

The arhat, "worthy one"

  • Arhat (Pali arahant): "worthy one": a person who has reached nirvana in this life by following the Buddha's teaching.
  • Greed, hatred and delusion are destroyed; no new karma is made; at death there is no further rebirth (parinirvana).
  • Standard canonical description (paraphrase): birth is ended, the holy life has been lived, what had to be done is done.

> "There is no suffering for him who has finished his journey, and abandoned grief, who has freed himself on all sides, and thrown off all fetters." (Dhammapada 90, Müller 1881)

  • In Theravada the arhat is the ideal of the Buddha's disciple; the Buddha himself is also an arahant, but one who found the path unaided.
Mission 6

The ten fetters

  • Progress is measured by breaking ten fetters (samyojana) that bind beings to rebirth:
  • Lower five: 1 belief in a permanent self · 2 doubt (in the Buddha, Dhamma, Sangha) · 3 clinging to rites and rituals as liberating in themselves · 4 sensual desire · 5 ill will.
  • Higher five: 6 craving for fine-material existence · 7 craving for immaterial existence · 8 conceit ("I am") · 9 restlessness · 10 ignorance.
  • "Fetters" in Müller's Dhammapada 90 are these ties.
Mission 6

The four stages of awakening

  • 1 Stream-winner (sotapanna, "stream-enterer"): breaks fetters 1–3. At most seven more lives, none in the lower realms; unshakeable confidence in the Three Refuges.
  • 2 Once-returner (sakadagami): fetters 4–5 weakened. One more human birth.
  • 3 Non-returner (anagami): fetters 4–5 broken. Reborn in the Pure Abodes, a heaven realm, and reaches nirvana there.
  • 4 Arhat: fetters 6–10 broken. Full awakening in this life.
  • Each stage has a path moment and a fruit: "four pairs, eight kinds of person", the noble Sangha honoured in the refuge chant.
  • Your spec names the four stages of Awakening: stream-winner, once-returner, non-returner, arhat (worthy one).
Mission 6Read the source

The first step

> "Better than sovereignty over the earth, better than going to heaven, better than lordship over all worlds, is the reward of the first step in holiness." (Dhammapada 178, Müller 1881)

  • "The first step in holiness" = the fruit of stream-entry (sotapatti-phala).

Arhat and bodhisattva

  • Mahayana holds up the bodhisattva: one who vows to reach full Buddhahood for the sake of all beings, through the perfections.
  • Some Mahayana texts call the arhat's goal lesser: liberation for oneself only.
  • Theravada replies: arhats teach and serve; the Buddha sent the first arahants out "for the welfare of the many, out of compassion for the world" (Vinaya, Mahavagga I.11, paraphrase). Ending one's own greed and hatred is good for others.
  • Theravada also honours the bodhisattva as the path the Buddha himself took (the Jataka tales), but as rare.
  • Your spec issue: "the relative legitimacy of concepts of bodhisattva and arhat": weigh the "lesser goal" charge against the Theravada reply above.
Mission 6Evaluate

Better rebirth or nirvana?

  • Claim: For most Buddhists, better rebirth matters more in practice than nirvana.
  • For: Most lay merit-making (dana, festivals, chanting) aims at a happier rebirth (Spiro).
  • For: Nirvana seems remote; many Buddhists hope to meet the future Buddha Maitreya and awaken then.
  • Against: Merit and better rebirth are means: every Buddhist hopes to reach nirvana in the end; the refuge chant honours the noble disciples.
  • Against: Stream-entry (Dhammapada 178) is ranked above heaven, and lay meditation movements make awakening a present aim.
  • Judgement criteria: Is "importance" measured by belief or by practice? Lay or monastic? Theravada or Mahayana?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Three marks: anicca, dukkha, anatta. Dukkha has three kinds: pain, the dukkha of change, and the dukkha of conditioned existence.
  • Anicca works at a gross level (ageing, death) and a subtle, momentary level; Buddhists teach that craving for what changes is what turns anicca into dukkha.

Mission 2

  • Anatta: no permanent, independent self in the five khandhas (form, feeling, perception, formations, consciousness) or beyond them.
  • The Buddha rejects eternalism and annihilationism; Nagasena's chariot shows "person" is a valid name for parts in relation, with no owner.

Mission 3

  • The Wheel of Life (bhavachakra): three poisons at the hub (cockerel greed, snake hatred, pig delusion), rising and falling beings, six realms, twelve links, all gripped by Yama; a figure outside points to release.

Mission 4

  • In Buddhist teaching, karma is intentional action of body, speech and mind; it plants seeds whose fruits ripen later. Accidents make little or none; an arhat makes none.
  • Rebirth is "neither the same nor another" (Milinda): a stream of causes, not a soul.

Mission 5

  • Dependent origination: Buddhists teach that everything arises from conditions; twelve links run from ignorance to ageing and death, pictured on the rim of the Wheel of Life.
  • Classic Theravada reads the links across three lives; others read them within one moment. Craving (link 8) follows feeling, and that gap is where practice works.
  • The poisoned arrow (MN 63): questions that do not lead to the end of dukkha are set aside, a stance your spec extends to the existence of God.

Mission 6

  • Better rebirth (through merit) is a near goal; nirvana is the ultimate goal; Spiro called these kammatic and nibbanic Buddhism.
  • Four stages break ten fetters: stream-winner (1–3), once-returner (4–5 weakened), non-returner (4–5 broken), arhat (all ten).
  • Mahayana prefers the bodhisattva ideal; Theravada replies that arhats serve "the welfare of the many".
