Mission 1

Mission 1: Forty-eight countries voted for the Universal Declaration of Human Rights. Did that bind any of them?

Make a guess: Forty-eight countries voted for the Universal Declaration of Human Rights. Did that bind any of them?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR A-level Geography (H481)

  • 2.2.3-1.a what is meant by human rights
  • 2.2.3-1.a norms, intervention and geopolitics as concepts for understanding the complexity of human rights
Mission 1

What are human rights?

  • Human rights: rights every person holds because they are human, not because a state grants them.
  • Civil and political: life, no torture, fair trial, free speech, vote.
  • Economic, social and cultural: health, education, work.
  • A right is a claim against power: usually the state must respect, protect and fulfil it.
Mission 1

The UDHR, 1948

  • Adopted on 10 December 1948 as General Assembly Resolution 217(III): 48 in favour, 8 abstaining.
  • Described in the drafting debate as "a common standard of achievement for all peoples and all nations".
  • The General Assembly "may make recommendations" (UN Charter, Art. 10): a resolution is not a treaty.
  • Its rights were "later enshrined in a legally binding international agreement" (Amnesty International, on torture).
Quick check
?Why is the UDHR described as a statement of intent rather than international law?
Mission 1

A framework for foreign policy

  • The EU's treaty says its action on the international scene is guided by "the universality and indivisibility of human rights" and "respect for the principles of the United Nations Charter" (TEU Art. 21).
  • So the UDHR's standards shape aid, trade and diplomacy, not only law.
  • This is a norm at work: an expected standard of behaviour that states use to judge each other.
Mission 1

Norms

  • A norm is a shared expectation of how states should behave. It can exist before, or without, a binding law.
  • Responsibility to Protect (R2P): "unanimously adopted in 2005 at the UN World Summit".
  • Para. 138: "Each individual State has the responsibility to protect its populations from genocide, war crimes, ethnic cleansing and crimes against humanity".
  • Norms change the question from "may outsiders act?" to "has this state failed its people?"
Mission 1

Intervention and geopolitics

  • Intervention: action by outside actors to change what happens inside a state: diplomacy, sanctions, aid, prosecution, force.
  • R2P para. 139: collective action "through the Security Council … on a case-by-case basis".
  • Security Council decisions need "the concurring votes of the permanent members" (Charter Art. 27(3)): a veto.
  • Since 2011 Russia cast 19 vetoes, 14 on Syria; China 8 of 9 on Syria. The US cast 14 since 2020, all but two on Israel/Palestine (Security Council Report).
  • Geopolitics: states' power and interests decide where rights are enforced.
Quick check
?A draft resolution to act on mass killings gains 13 of 15 votes but is vetoed by a permanent member allied to the government accused. Which concept best explains the outcome?
Mission check

?In 2007 the General Assembly adopted the UN Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples; four states voted against: Australia, Canada, New Zealand and the United States. What did that vote do?

The UDHR bound no one in 1948. It works as a norm and a foreign-policy framework; binding force comes from treaties such as the ECHR and the Geneva Conventions, and even those depend on enforcement.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why are forced labour, maternal deaths and executions concentrated in such different places?

Make a guess: Why are forced labour, maternal deaths and executions concentrated in such different places?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR A-level Geography (H481)

  • 2.2.3-1.b spatial patterns of forced labour, maternal mortality and capital punishment
  • 2.2.3-1.b factors influencing global variation in forced labour, maternal mortality and capital punishment
Mission 2

Three violations, three measures

  • Forced labour: "all work or service which is exacted from any person under the menace of any penalty and for which the said person has not offered himself voluntarily" (ILO Convention No. 29). Measured per 1,000 people.
  • Maternal mortality: deaths from complications of pregnancy and childbirth, during or soon after; the MMR = deaths per 100,000 live births.
  • Capital punishment: executions by the state; Amnesty counts are minimum figures.
  • Rights at stake: freedom from slavery and servitude; health; life.
Mission 2

Forced labour: the pattern

  • 27.6 million people in forced labour on any given day in 2021: 3.5 per 1,000.
  • Numbers: Asia and the Pacific 15.1 million; Europe and Central Asia 4.1m; Africa 3.8m; Americas 3.6m; Arab States 0.9m.
  • Prevalence: Arab States 5.3 per 1,000; Europe and Central Asia 4.4; Americas 3.5; Asia and the Pacific 3.5; Africa 2.9.
  • By income: highest in low-income countries (6.3 per 1,000), then high-income (4.4).
Quick check
?Which statement about the forced-labour pattern is correct?
Mission 2

Forced labour: the factors

  • Private economy: 86% of cases are imposed by private actors; 14% (3.9 million) by states.
  • State-imposed: 55% involve abuse of compulsory prison labour; 27% abuse of conscription.
  • Migration: adult migrant workers' forced-labour prevalence is "more than three times higher" than non-migrants'.
  • Vulnerability: "the poor and socially excluded, workers in the informal economy, irregular or otherwise unprotected migrant workers, and people subject to discrimination".
  • So the map follows labour systems and migration rules, not wealth alone.
Mission 2

Maternal mortality: the pattern

  • About 260,000 women died during and following pregnancy and childbirth in 2023; global MMR 197.
  • Sub-Saharan Africa ~70% of deaths (182,000); southern Asia ~17% (43,000).
  • Highest MMRs (2023): Nigeria 993; Chad 748; South Sudan 692.
  • Lowest: Norway 1.3; UK 8.3.
  • Lifetime risk: 1 in 66 in low-income countries; 1 in 7,933 in high-income countries.
Mission 2

Maternal mortality: the factors

  • Skilled care: ~99% of births have a trained attendant in high- and upper-middle-income countries; 73% in low-income.
  • Conflict and fragility: 37 such countries had 25% of live births but 61% of maternal deaths; MMR 504 in conflict-affected settings and 368 in fragile ones, v 99 in settings that are neither.
  • Health systems: poor quality of care, too few trained staff, shortages of supplies.
  • Society: income, education, and "harmful gender norms" that give women's health low priority.
Quick check
?In 2023 the MMR was 346 per 100,000 live births in low-income countries and 10 in high-income countries. How many times higher is the low-income MMR?
Mission 2

Capital punishment: the pattern

  • At least 1,518 executions recorded in 15 countries in 2024; executing countries at their lowest number on record.
  • Iran 972+, Saudi Arabia 345+, Iraq 63+: about 91% of known executions.
  • China executes "thousands" but keeps figures secret; they are excluded from the total.
  • For 2025, Amnesty records at least 2,707.
  • End of 2024: 113 countries abolitionist for all crimes; 145 abolitionist in law or practice; 54 retentionist.
Mission 2

Capital punishment: the factors

  • Politics and secrecy: China, North Korea and Viet Nam restrict information.
  • Drug policy: at least 52% of Iran's recorded executions in 2024 were for drug-related offences.
  • Regional law: ECHR protocols abolish the death penalty "in all circumstances" (Vilnius, 2002); Belarus is the only European country still using it.
  • Not just wealth or democracy: the USA carried out 25 executions in 2024.
Quick check
?Why does the map of executions show almost none in Europe?
Mission 2

Comparing the three maps

  • Maternal mortality tracks poverty, health systems and conflict.
  • Forced labour tracks labour markets, migration rules and state coercion: high in some high-income regions.
  • Capital punishment tracks law and politics: retained by rich and poor states alike.
  • Data limits: forced labour is survey-based; executions are minimums; MMRs are modelled. Compare with care.
Mission check

?Country X is high-income. It has abolished the death penalty, has an MMR below 10, and relies on migrant workers with employer-tied visas in construction and domestic work. Which violation is most likely to be significant there?

Each violation has its own drivers, so each has its own map: health systems and conflict for maternal deaths, labour and migration systems for forced labour, law and politics for executions.

Mission 3: Nearly half of the 2.4 million elected members of India's rural local councils are women. Can reserving places close a development gap?

Make a guess: Nearly half of the 2.4 million elected members of India's rural local councils are women. Can reserving places close a development gap?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR A-level Geography (H481)

  • 2.2.3-2.a economic, political and social factors in gender inequality: education, reproductive health services, employment
  • 2.2.3-2.a case study of women's rights in one country: issues, consequences, changing norms and strategies
Mission 3

Unequal rights within countries

  • In many states, groups defined by gender or ethnicity have had fewer rights than the dominant group: to vote, own land, study, work or use services.
  • In some post-colonial states, groups dispossessed or excluded under colonial rule still have fewer rights than the dominant group.
  • Rights gaps show up as development gaps: in health, education and income.
Mission 3

The global pattern of gender inequality

  • The Gender Inequality Index (GII) combines reproductive health, empowerment and the labour market, from 0 (equal) to 1.
  • 2023: Denmark 0.003 (rank 1); Yemen 0.838 (rank 172); world 0.455.
  • Maternal deaths per 100,000 live births, 2023 (WHO estimates): world 197; Australia 2.3; Afghanistan 520.5.
Mission 3

Why gender inequality varies

  • Economic: where work is informal or farm-based and unpaid care falls on women, fewer women earn wages.
  • Political: laws on property, marriage and work; women's share of seats; conflict and changes of regime.
  • Social: norms about early marriage, girls' schooling and mobility.
  • These shape educational opportunity (in Afghanistan 3.7 million children are out of school, 60% of them girls), reproductive health services and employment.
Quick check
?A government bars women from working in many jobs. Which kind of factor is this?
Mission 3

India: the issues

  • Reproductive health: maternal deaths fell from 128.5 (2015) to 80.5 (2023) per 100,000 live births (WHO estimates), still far above Australia's 2.3.
  • Representation: the Constitution (Article 243D) reserves at least one-third of seats in rural local councils for women; 21 states and 2 union territories reserve 50%.
  • Employment: a study of 495 villages found "no evidence of changes in young women's labor market opportunities" where women led councils.
Mission 3

India: reserved seats, what changed

  • On 25 November 2025, 1,206,013 of 2,424,108 elected representatives in rural local councils were women: 49.75%.
  • From 1998, one-third of West Bengal's village council leader posts were reserved for women at random.
  • Women leaders invested more "in infrastructure that is directly relevant to the needs of rural women (water, fuel, and roads)".
  • In villages with women leaders, the gender gap in aspirations "closed by 20% in parents and 32% in adolescents".
Mission 3

India: consequences, limits and new strategies

  • Consequences of under-representation: before reservation, rural women's priorities (water, fuel) got less investment.
  • Limits: some seats are run by male relatives as proxies; a government panel set up in September 2023 has recommended "exemplary penalties" (news report).
  • New strategy: in September 2023 Parliament passed an amendment reserving one-third of seats in the Lok Sabha and state assemblies for women, effective after the next census is published, for 15 years.
Quick check
?Why is the random reservation of seats in West Bengal so useful to researchers?
Mission check

?In 2023 India reserved one-third of national and state assembly seats for women, from after the next census. Which prediction does the village-council evidence best support?

Reserving places can narrow some gaps, not all. In India's village councils, women leaders changed spending and the aspirations gap closed by 20–32%, but young women's job opportunities did not change, and some seats are run by proxies.

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR A-level Geography (H481)

  • 2.2.3-3.a human rights violations as a cause of conflict (e.g. access to education, discrimination)
  • 2.2.3-3.a violations as a consequence of conflict, addressed through geopolitical intervention
  • 2.2.3-3.a flows of people, money, ideas and technology in geopolitical intervention
Mission 4

A spectrum of intervention

  • Geopolitical intervention: action by outside actors to change what happens inside another state.
  • From least to most forceful: development aid → sanctions and embargoes → military aid (arms, training) → indirect military action (supporting one side) → direct military action.
  • National sovereignty: a state's right to run its own affairs. The UN Charter rests on "the sovereign equality of all its Members".
  • Its limit: non-intervention "shall not prejudice the application of enforcement measures under Chapter VII".
Mission 4

Aid and embargoes

  • Development aid: OECD donors gave $174.3 billion in 2025; aid "fell in 2025 by 23.1% in real terms".
  • Embargoes: bans on trade in some or all goods. UN sanctions on North Korea, begun under resolution 1718 (2006) and widened in 2016–2017, include an arms embargo, a "luxury goods" ban and bans on its exports of coal, iron, textiles and seafood.
  • Embargoes aim to change behaviour without force, but can also hurt ordinary people.
Quick check
?A government sends weapons and military trainers to an allied state fighting rebels, but sends no troops. What kind of intervention is this?
Mission 4

Who promotes intervention?

  • IGOs: the UN Security Council can authorise sanctions or force under Chapter VII.
  • Governments: act alone or in alliances, using aid, trade and arms.
  • NGOs: Amnesty International, "a global movement of more than 10 million people", says it is "independent of any political ideology, economic interest or religion".
  • Human Rights Watch works "in close to 100 countries", refuses government funding, and uses "satellite imagery to track the destruction of villages and city blocks".
  • NGOs document violations and lobby; they do not use force.
Mission 4

Libya 2011: intervention without consensus

  • 2005: UN members endorsed the responsibility to protect populations "from genocide, war crimes, ethnic cleansing and crimes against humanity".
  • 17 March 2011: Security Council Resolution 1973 authorised states "to take all necessary measures" to protect civilians in Libya, and set a no-fly zone.
  • Vote: 10 for, 0 against, 5 abstentions (China, Russia, Brazil, Germany, India); it excluded "an occupation force".
  • NATO's Operation Unified Protector enforced it until 31 October 2011.
Quick check
?What does the Resolution 1973 vote show about intervention?
Mission 4

Violations as a cause of conflict

  • A UN–World Bank study names "grievances related to exclusion—from access to power, natural resources, security and justice" as being "at the root of many violent conflicts today".
  • Denying a group education, jobs or citizenship, or discrimination in law, builds grievances.
  • When peaceful protest is repressed, grievances can turn into armed conflict.
Mission 4

Violations as a consequence of conflict

  • War brings killings, sexual violence, forced displacement and blocked aid.
  • End of 2025: 117.8 million people forcibly displaced worldwide (UNHCR).
  • Sudan: 9.1 million displaced within the country and 2.8 million refugees at the end of 2025.
  • Intervention responds with humanitarian aid, sanctions, investigations and, rarely, force (e.g. Libya 2011).
Mission 4

Flows in intervention

  • People: refugees and displaced people (117.8 million in 2025); peacekeepers and aid workers moving in.
  • Money: aid budgets ($174.3 billion from OECD donors in 2025); frozen assets and sanctions.
  • Ideas: human rights norms and the responsibility to protect (2005) justify intervention.
  • Technology: satellite imagery used by HRW to document destruction; arms flows in military aid.
Quick check
?An NGO publishes satellite images showing villages burned in a conflict, and donor states then fund aid for people who have fled. Which flows are involved?
Mission check

?A government's forces attack civilians. NGOs publish evidence, but a Security Council resolution authorising force fails. Which response can still go ahead without Council authorisation?

Intervention is a spectrum, from aid and conditions to force, promoted by IGOs, governments and NGOs. Consensus is rare, and every step tests the balance between human rights and national sovereignty.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Over a million Rohingya refugees live in camps in Bangladesh. Who is responsible for protecting their rights?

Make a guess: Over a million Rohingya refugees live in camps in Bangladesh. Who is responsible for protecting their rights?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR A-level Geography (H481)

  • 2.2.3-3.b human rights promoted and protected by institutions, treaties, laws and norms
  • 2.2.3-3.b case study of human rights governance in one area of conflict: roles of the UN, a national government and an NGO; local consequences
Mission 5

Four tools of human rights governance

  • Institutions: bodies that act, e.g. UNHCR, the International Court of Justice ("principal judicial organ of the United Nations").
  • Treaties: binding agreements, e.g. the Genocide Convention; the 1951 Refugee Convention ("well-founded fear of being persecuted").
  • Laws: national rules that apply treaties, or fail to.
  • Norms: shared expectations, e.g. R2P: "Each individual State has the responsibility to protect its populations".
Quick check
?Which is an example of a norm rather than a treaty, law or institution?
Mission 5

The conflict area: Rakhine State, 2017

  • From 25 August 2017, the Myanmar military, police and local militias launched "clearance operations" in Rakhine "in response to attacks by the Arakan Rohingya Salvation Army" (MSF).
  • By 12 December 2017, more than 647,000 Rohingya had fled into Bangladesh.
  • MSF surveys estimated at least 6,700 Rohingya killed by violence between 25 August and 24 September, including at least 730 children under five.
  • UN investigators found most Rohingya had become "de facto stateless, arbitrarily deprived of nationality".
Mission 5

Role 1: the United Nations

  • UNHCR works with the Government of Bangladesh: 1,208,018 Rohingya refugees registered (31 Oct 2026).
  • ICJ: The Gambia filed a case against Myanmar under the Genocide Convention on 11 November 2019.
  • On 23 January 2020 the Court ordered provisional measures; Myanmar has filed regular reports since (its twelfth on 21 November 2025).
  • In 2022 the Court ruled it has jurisdiction; 11 states have intervened; the Court was due to deliver its judgment on 19 November 2026.
  • Members "undertake to comply" with ICJ decisions; enforcement passes to the Security Council (Charter Art. 94).
Mission 5

Role 2: national governments

  • Myanmar (government of the conflict area): the respondent at the ICJ; it challenged the Court's jurisdiction and lost (22 July 2022).
  • Bangladesh (host): "not a party to the 1951 Refugee Convention" but has ratified "the main human rights instruments".
  • Bangladesh manages the response "through directives, letters and administrative instruments", not a refugee law.
  • Since 2021, about 35,000 refugees were relocated to Bhasan Char, an island, "to decongest the camps".
Quick check
?Bangladesh is not a party to the 1951 Refugee Convention. What does this mean for the Rohingya in Cox's Bazar?
Mission 5

Role 3: an NGO, Médecins Sans Frontières

  • Relief: MSF "has run a medical facility and a clinic" for Rohingya refugees and the local community.
  • MSF expanded "water, sanitation and medical activities for the refugee population".
  • Evidence: MSF's six mortality surveys covered areas with 608,108 people, giving independent data on deaths in Myanmar.
  • NGOs have no power to enforce law; their strengths are presence, speed and testimony.
Mission 5

Local consequences

  • 1,174,975 refugees live in Cox's Bazar's 33 "highly congested camps"; 33,043 on Bhasan Char (31 Oct 2026).
  • The largest camp, Camp 15, holds 67,957 people.
  • 155,679 new arrivals have been biometrically identified; they receive family cards, not individual refugee identity documents.
  • In 2025, UN agencies appealed for US$84 million for an estimated 150,000 additional new arrivals.
  • The host district shares health services: MSF's facility treats the local community too.
Quick check
?Of 1,208,018 Rohingya refugees in Bangladesh, 33,043 live on Bhasan Char. What percentage is that, to 1 d.p.?
Mission 5

Evaluating the governance

  • Institutions acted: UNHCR registration; the ICJ's binding order (2020); its judgment was due on 19 November 2026.
  • Treaties have gaps: no Refugee Convention duty in Bangladesh; enforcement of ICJ rulings depends on the Security Council.
  • Norms strained: R2P assigns protection to the state, here the state accused.
  • Outcome so far: more than nine years after August 2017, more than 1.2 million people remain in camps and new arrivals continue.
Mission 5

Exam move: structure a case-study answer

  • Actor → role → action → consequence, for the UN, a national government and an NGO.
  • Use one figure per actor (e.g. 1,208,018 registered; ICJ order of 23 Jan 2020; MSF's 6,700).
  • End with a judgement: which actor changed outcomes most, and for whom?
Mission check

?Rwanda hosts 140,377 forcibly displaced people, mostly from DR Congo and Burundi; 72,540 live in Mahama camp. The figures are published by "Government, MINEMA, UNHCR" (30 Sep 2026). Who has the primary responsibility to protect their rights in Rwanda?

In Bangladesh too, the host government holds primary responsibility, with UNHCR supporting and MSF providing care and evidence. The ICJ addresses Myanmar's duties as the state where the violence happened. Responsibility is layered, and gaps appear where layers are missing.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Most of Rwanda's MPs are women, yet it is rated "Not Free". Has human rights governance there succeeded?

Make a guess: Most of Rwanda's MPs are women, yet it is rated "Not Free". Has human rights governance there succeeded?

Specification: what this mission covers

OCR A-level Geography (H481)

  • 2.2.3-4.a short-term (e.g. NGO relief) and long-term (e.g. changes in law) consequences of human rights governance
  • 2.2.3-4.a case study of human rights governance in one LIDC: issues, strategies, opportunities, inequality and injustice
Mission 6

What counts as success?

  • An intervention can be judged by many indicators:
  • Health (child mortality), life expectancy, education (enrolment);
  • Gender equality (women in decision-making), free speech (media freedom);
  • Refugees (numbers fleeing or hosted), GDP per head.
  • Different actors pick different indicators, so they reach different verdicts.
Mission 6

Rwanda: the LIDC case

  • Rwanda is a low-income country (World Bank): an LIDC in OCR's terms.
  • 1994, the year of the genocide: life expectancy at birth 12.2 years; under-5 mortality 389 per 1,000.
  • 2024: life expectancy 68.0 years; under-5 mortality 37.7 per 1,000.
  • GDP per head (constant 2015 US$): 187 (1994) → 1,116 (2025).
Mission 6

Rwanda: strategy through law

  • The 2003 constitution (revised 2015) commits to "women occupying at least thirty percent (30%) of positions in decision-making organs".
  • "At least thirty percent (30%) of Deputies must be women"; the same for Senators.
  • Of 80 deputies, 24 are women chosen by local councils.
  • Outcome: women hold 63.8% of lower-house seats and 53.8% of Senate seats.
Quick check
?Women hold 63.8% of the 80 seats in Rwanda's lower house. About how many women deputies is that?
Mission 6

Rwanda: inequality and injustice

  • Freedom House (an NGO) rates Rwanda Not Free: 21/100 (political rights 7/40; civil liberties 14/60).
  • It reports self-censorship by journalists, and that 2018 penal-code changes criminalised cartoons and writings that "humiliate" leaders.
  • It finds women legislators have "little practical ability to engage in politics outside the RPF".
  • It reports that the Twa are unrecognised as a marginalised community.
  • Opportunity and injustice sit side by side.
Mission 6

Short-term relief v long-term change

  • Short-term: relief meets urgent needs. Rwanda hosts 140,377 forcibly displaced people (Sep 2026), 72,540 in Mahama camp, with the government and UNHCR.
  • NGO relief example: MSF ran a medical facility and clinic for Rohingya refugees and the local community in Bangladesh.
  • Long-term: law changes structures: Rwanda's 30% quotas are written into the constitution.
  • Relief saves lives now; law changes who holds power for decades.
Quick check
?Which is a long-term consequence of human rights governance?
Mission check

?Country Z (a low-income country) has, over 20 years, halved child mortality and doubled GDP per head; women now hold 40% of seats under a legal quota. Over the same period, independent media were closed and its freedom score fell. A donor government calls its aid programme a success; a human rights NGO calls it a failure. Which judgement is best supported?

Like Rwanda, Country Z shows mixed results: big gains in health, growth and women's representation alongside losses in free speech. Success depends on the indicator, and on whose values choose it.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Human rights belong to people as humans; the UDHR (1948, 48 for, 8 abstaining) set a standard but is a statement of intent, not law.
  • Norms (R2P), intervention and geopolitics (the veto) explain why rights are protected unevenly.

Mission 2

  • Forced labour: 27.6 million; highest rate in the Arab States (5.3 per 1,000); driven by private exploitation, state coercion and unprotected migration.
  • Maternal mortality: 260,000 deaths (2023), 70% in sub-Saharan Africa; driven by skilled care, conflict, health systems and gender norms. Capital punishment: ≥1,518 executions in 15 countries (2024), 91% in Iran, Saudi Arabia and Iraq; driven by law, politics and drug policy.

Mission 3

  • Rights gaps by gender and ethnicity show up in health and education.
  • Economic, political and social factors explain the GII pattern; India's reserved seats changed some outcomes, not all.

Mission 4

  • Geopolitical intervention ranges from development aid and embargoes to military aid and direct military action; IGOs, governments and NGOs (Amnesty, HRW) promote it, but rarely agree.
  • Rights violations can cause conflict and result from it; intervention involves flows of people, money, ideas and technology.

Mission 5

  • Rights are governed by institutions, treaties, laws and norms; in the Rohingya case the UN (UNHCR, ICJ), two national governments (Myanmar, Bangladesh) and an NGO (MSF) each play different roles.
  • Local consequences: 1.2 million refugees in congested camps, relocation to Bhasan Char, continuing arrivals; protection depends on a host state with no refugee law.

Mission 6

  • Success can be measured by health, life expectancy, education, gender equality, free speech, refugees or GDP.
  • Rwanda (LIDC): life expectancy 12.2 (1994) → 68.0 (2024), women 63.8% of deputies under a 30% quota, yet "Not Free" (21/100). Relief is short-term; law change is long-term.
