Mission 1

The church is its people

  • church (small c): a building. Church (capital C): the community of believers.
  • The New Testament word ekklesia means an assembly: people called together.
  • A local church (in Catholic and Anglican life, a parish) is a community in one place.
  • It is the centre of Christian identity: where believers worship, are baptised, married and buried, and belong.
Mission 1

What a local church does

  • The first Christians met for teaching, fellowship, breaking bread and prayer, and shared with anyone in need (Acts 2:42–47, paraphrase).
  • Today a local church offers:
    • Worship: Sunday services, the sacraments, festivals;
    • Rites of passage: baptism, confirmation, marriage, funerals;
    • Learning: Bible study, preparation for the sacraments, groups for new Christians;
    • Fellowship: belonging, friendship, support in hard times;
    • Service: help for anyone in need, believer or not.
Mission 1

Nurturing families

  • Sunday schools and children's groups: Bible stories, prayer, friendship. (Begun in 1780 by Robert Raikes in Gloucester, to teach poor children to read.)
  • Youth clubs: safe places to meet and talk about faith and life.
  • Parent and toddler groups: support for parents, especially new or isolated ones.
  • Visiting the elderly and sick: at home, in hospital or care homes; Catholics bring Holy Communion to the housebound.
  • Preparation for baptism and marriage; support for the bereaved.
  • Why: Christians see the family as the first place faith is learned; the church acts as a wider family.

Mission 1

Food banks

  • A food bank gives emergency food to people in crisis.
  • Many are run by churches, often in the network of the charity Trussell (founded by Christians; its first food bank opened in Salisbury in 2000).
  • How it works: a professional (such as a doctor or social worker) refers someone; they receive several days' food, a welcome, and help to find longer-term support.
  • Volunteers come from many churches together, and from people of other faiths and none.
  • Why: "I was hungry and you gave me food… you did it to me" (Matthew 25:35–40, paraphrase); love of neighbour.
  • Some Christians also campaign on the causes of poverty, not only its effects.
Mission 1

Street pastors

  • Street pastors: trained volunteers from local churches who patrol town centres late on Friday and Saturday nights.
  • Begun in London in 2003 by the Rev Les Isaac (Ascension Trust).
  • They care, listen and help: water, flip-flops for people who can't walk in their shoes, foil blankets, sweeping up broken glass, staying with vulnerable people until help arrives.
  • They work alongside police and councils; they do not preach unless someone asks about their faith.
  • Why: to be Christ's presence on the streets; love of neighbour in practice (Luke 10:25–37, paraphrase).

Mission 1

Ecumenism and outreach

  • Ecumenism locally: churches of different denominations work and pray together.
    • Joint services, Good Friday walks of witness, the Week of Prayer for Christian Unity;
    • shared buildings in some towns;
    • joint projects: food banks, night shelters, street pastors.
  • Outreach: reaching beyond the congregation.
    • Courses exploring faith (e.g. Alpha); holiday clubs; carol services in public places;
    • debt advice (e.g. Christians Against Poverty), community cafés, warm spaces.
  • How this helps the believer: belonging, support, a way to live out faith. How it helps the area: services, friendship, people who notice those in need.
Mission 1Evaluate

"The local church is still the heart of its community today."

  • For: food banks, street pastors, warm spaces and toddler groups serve everyone, not only members.
  • For: churches mark the big moments of life (baptisms, weddings, funerals) and gather the community at Christmas and in times of tragedy.
  • Against: most people rarely attend; many towns have councils, charities and community centres that do the same work.
  • Against: in some places churches have divided communities rather than united them.
  • Judge it by: Heart for whom: believers or everyone? Measured by attendance, or by service? Does it differ between villages, cities and Northern Ireland?

Mission 2

Mission and evangelism

  • Mission (Latin mittere, "to send"): the whole task the Church is sent to do: sharing God's love in word and action.
  • Evangelism (Greek euangelion, "good news"): sharing the Gospel so that others may come to faith in Jesus.
  • So all evangelism is mission, but not all mission is evangelism: a food bank is mission; explaining the Gospel is evangelism.
  • Church growth: more people joining churches, and new churches being started.
  • Catholics often say evangelisation: bringing the Gospel into every part of life and society.
  • Catholic teaching counts the witness of a Christian life as the first step of evangelisation, completed by words.
Mission 2Read the source

The Great Commission

After his resurrection, Jesus tells the eleven disciples:

> "Go and make disciples of all nations, baptising them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, teaching them to observe all things that I commanded you. Behold, I am with you always, even to the end of the age." (Matthew 28:19–20)

  • "But you will receive power when the Holy Spirit has come upon you. You will be witnesses to me in Jerusalem, …" (Acts 1:8): from Jerusalem outwards to the whole world.
  • Jesus sends the eleven to "go into all the world" and preach the Good News to the whole creation (Mark 16:15, paraphrase).
  • Jesus: "I am the way, the truth, and the life" (John 14:6, phrase); Paul is "not ashamed of the Good News", the power of God for salvation (Romans 1:16, paraphrase); "preach the word… in season and out of season" (2 Timothy 4:2, paraphrase).

Mission 2Diversity

Salvation and the urgency of mission

  • How Christians understand salvation shapes how they see mission.
  • Exclusivist (many evangelical Christians): salvation only through conscious faith in Jesus (John 14:6; Acts 4:12). So evangelism is urgent.
  • Catholic teaching: salvation comes through Christ; the Church is his instrument; God's grace can reach people who, through no fault of their own, do not know Christ. But the Church is still sent to proclaim him to all.
  • Liberal or pluralist Christians: God is at work in many faiths; mission stresses dialogue and justice more than conversion.
  • Most Christians agree that mission includes both words and loving action; they differ on the balance.
Mission 2

Locally, nationally, globally

  • Locally: welcoming newcomers; courses for adults exploring faith or wanting to be baptised or received; youth work; food banks and street pastors (see Mission 1); inviting friends.
  • Nationally: Christian radio and online media; national youth festivals and pilgrimages; church leaders speaking on public issues; joint campaigns by Churches Together.
  • Globally: missionary societies and orders (e.g. the Church Mission Society; for Catholics, Missio, the Pope's mission charity) support churches, schools and clinics worldwide.
  • Reverse mission: many churches in Britain are now led or planted by Christians from Africa, Asia, Latin America and Eastern Europe.

Mission 2

Evangelism today and new forms of church

  • Evangelism in practice: courses exploring faith (e.g. Alpha, started at Holy Trinity Brompton, London); sharing faith with friends; school and university Christian Unions; street preaching; online videos and podcasts.
  • New forms of church:
    • Messy Church: crafts, a short celebration and a meal for families (from 2004);
    • café church: worship and discussion over coffee;
    • church plants: new congregations in schools, pubs or warehouses;
    • online church.
  • The Church of England and the Methodist Church call these Fresh Expressions (from the report Mission-shaped Church, 2004).
  • Reasons: to reach people who would never come to a traditional service; changing Sunday habits; to fit the needs of families and young adults.
  • Church growth worldwide is strongest in Africa, Asia and Latin America; in Britain, Pentecostal, Black-majority and migrant churches are among those growing.

Mission 2Evaluate

"Showing love in action is a better way to spread Christianity than preaching."

  • For: Jesus served the sick and poor; "faith without works is dead" (James 2:17, paraphrase); actions build trust where words may be resented.
  • Against: the Great Commission commands teaching (Matthew 28:19–20); "how will they believe in him whom they have not heard?" (Romans 10:14, paraphrase).
  • Against: good works alone may be seen as simple kindness; people need to hear why Christians act.
  • Judge it by: What does "spread" mean: more believers, or more love? Can word and action be separated? Which reaches people in Britain today?

Mission 3

One Church, worldwide

  • The worldwide Church is all Christians in every country: Catholic, Orthodox, Protestant, Pentecostal and more.
  • Christians call it the Body of Christ: many parts, one body.
  • "When one member suffers, all the members suffer with it." (1 Corinthians 12:26)
  • So a church in Birmingham cares about a church in Nigeria or Pakistan as family.
  • This mission: two jobs of the worldwide Church: reconciliation and standing with the persecuted.
  • (Its charity and aid agencies: a later mission.)
Mission 3

Reconciliation

  • Reconciliation: restoring friendship after a quarrel, hurt or war.
  • Christians believe God reconciled people to himself through Jesus, and then sent them to do the same:
  • "God… reconciled us to himself through Jesus Christ, and gave to us the ministry of reconciliation" (2 Corinthians 5:18).
  • "Blessed are the peacemakers, for they shall be called children of God." (Matthew 5:9)
  • Three levels: people with God; people with each other (communities, nations); churches with each other.
  • Why the Church does it: Jesus' teaching, Jesus' example on the cross, and the belief that Christians are one body.
Mission 3

The Ecumenical Movement

  • Ecumenism (from Greek oikoumene, "the whole inhabited world"): churches working towards unity.
  • Its aim is reconciliation between divided churches, not one identical church.
  • Jesus prayed for his followers "that they may all be one… that the world may believe" (John 17:21).
  • Christians see division as a wound; unity makes their witness believable.
  • Milestones: Edinburgh Missionary Conference (1910) · World Council of Churches (1948) · Catholic Church commits to ecumenism at the Second Vatican Council (decree Unitatis Redintegratio, 1964).
  • Local examples (Taizé, Iona, Corrymeela, Churches Together): see the pilgrimage mission.
Mission 3

The World Council of Churches

  • The World Council of Churches (WCC): founded in Amsterdam, 1948; offices in Geneva.
  • A fellowship of about 350 churches: Protestant, Anglican, Orthodox and others.
  • The Catholic Church is not a member but works closely with it (joint working group; members on its Faith and Order commission).
  • Main purpose: visible unity of the churches, a shared witness, and service to the world.
  • In practice: dialogue on beliefs (e.g. baptism, Eucharist); the Week of Prayer for Christian Unity, prepared jointly with the Catholic Church; work for peace and justice in conflict zones; help for refugees.
  • This is reconciliation through ecumenism: churches reconciled with each other, then working together for reconciliation in the world.

Mission 3

Peacemakers after conflict

  • Coventry Cathedral: bombed in 1940. The words "Father forgive" were written on the ruined wall. Its Community of the Cross of Nails now links partners worldwide, including in Germany, working for reconciliation.
  • South Africa: after apartheid, Archbishop Desmond Tutu chaired the Truth and Reconciliation Commission (from 1996): victims told their stories; offenders confessed.
  • Mozambique: the Catholic Community of Sant'Egidio hosted talks that ended a long civil war (peace agreement, Rome, 1992).
  • Corrymeela does this work between Catholics and Protestants in Northern Ireland.
  • Pattern: truth, forgiveness, justice and rebuilding trust, not pretending nothing happened.
Mission 3

Persecution in the past

  • Persecution: hostility or ill-treatment because of a person's faith.
  • From the start: Stephen, the first Christian martyr, was stoned (Acts 7).
  • Jesus warned: "If they persecuted me, they will also persecute you." (John 15:20)
  • Roman Empire: Nero blamed Christians for the fire of Rome (AD 64); the Great Persecution under Diocletian (from AD 303); ended by the Edict of Milan (AD 313).
  • A martyr ("witness") dies rather than deny the faith. The early writer Tertullian (c. AD 197) wrote that "the blood of Christians is seed" (Apology 50): persecution made the Church grow.
Mission 3

The persecuted Church today

  • Many Christians today face persecution, in some countries severely.
  • Forms: violent attacks on churches and villages; prison or death for leaving another religion; blasphemy laws; loss of jobs or schooling; surveillance; being disowned by family.
  • Places often named by monitoring groups: North Korea, parts of Nigeria and the Sahel, Pakistan, Iran, Afghanistan, Eritrea; Iraq and Syria, where many ancient churches lost most of their members after 2014.
  • Causes differ: hostile governments, armed extremist groups, nationalism, local pressure.
  • In 2019 a review for the UK Foreign Secretary, led by the Bishop of Truro, urged the UK to do more for persecuted Christians.

Remember those in chains

"Remember those who are in bonds, as bound with them, and those who are ill-treated, since you are also in the body." (Hebrews 13:3)

  • "Love your enemies, bless those who curse you, do good to those who hate you, and pray for those who mistreat you and persecute you" (Matthew 5:44).
  • "Bless those who persecute you; bless, and don't curse." (Romans 12:14)

Mission 3

How churches respond

  • Pray: e.g. the International Day of Prayer for the Persecuted Church (November); in Catholic churches, Red Wednesday (Aid to the Church in Need).
  • Practical help: agencies such as Open Doors (founded by Brother Andrew, Dutch), Aid to the Church in Need (Catholic) and Release International send Bibles, rebuild churches, support families of prisoners, give trauma care.
  • Speak up: campaign, write to prisoners and politicians, raise cases in Parliament.
  • Forgive: many persecuted Christians publicly forgive their attackers.
  • Different emphases: some Christians stress endurance and sharing Christ's sufferings ("Blessed are those who have been persecuted for righteousness' sake", Matthew 5:10); others stress campaigning for justice and religious freedom for everyone, not only Christians.
  • Some Christians warn against using persecution to stir hatred of other religions: most people of every faith are not persecutors.

Mission 3Evaluate

"The most important work of the worldwide Church is reconciliation."

  • Reasons for: Christians believe God's own work is reconciling the world (2 Corinthians 5:18–19); Jesus blessed peacemakers (Matthew 5:9); divided churches weaken witness (John 17:21); reconciliation can end wars and save lives (Mozambique, South Africa).
  • Reasons against: persecuted Christians need protection and aid first (Hebrews 13:3; 1 Corinthians 12:26); feeding the hungry and helping the poor are equally commanded (Matthew 25); some argue that spreading the Gospel (mission) is the Church's first task (Matthew 28:19).
  • Another view: these are not rivals: supporting victims, seeking justice and making peace are all reconciliation.
  • Judge it by: What did Jesus teach most? What does the world most need now? Can reconciliation happen without justice for victims?
Mission check

Reconciliation heals what is broken; support for the persecuted treats suffering Christians as one body. Both come from the same belief: one Church, one Lord, love even for enemies.

Mission 4

A different kind of pilgrimage

  • A pilgrimage is a journey made for a spiritual purpose.
  • Some pilgrims go to a holy site: a place of Jesus' life, a saint's tomb, a reported vision.
  • Others go to a living community: people who share prayer, work and meals.
  • Many of these communities are ecumenical: Christians of different denominations together.
  • Ecumenism: working for unity between Christian churches. It does not mean merging them into one.
  • The word comes from Greek oikoumene, "the whole inhabited world".
Mission 4

Iona: Columba's island

  • Iona: a small island off the west coast of Scotland.
  • AD 563: the Irish monk Columba arrives with 12 companions and founds a monastery.
  • From Iona, monks took Christianity to the Picts and to northern England (Aidan founded Lindisfarne, 635).
  • Later a Benedictine abbey; ruined after the Scottish Reformation (1560).
  • Many call Iona a "thin place": a Celtic idea of a place where heaven and earth feel close.
Mission 4

The Iona Community today

  • Founded 1938 by George MacLeod, a Church of Scotland minister from Govan, Glasgow.
  • Ministers and unemployed craftsmen rebuilt the abbey together: faith joined to work and to the poor.
  • Today: an ecumenical community; members live all over Britain and beyond and keep a shared Rule:
    • daily prayer and Bible reading;
    • accounting for their use of money and time;
    • action for justice and peace.
  • Guests stay for a week: worship twice a day, shared meals and chores, talks on faith and justice.
  • A weekly pilgrimage walk round the island stops at places from Iona's history for prayer.
  • Significance: all of life (work, politics, the environment) is part of following Christ.

Mission 4

Taizé: a parable of community

  • Taizé: a village in Burgundy, eastern France.
  • 1940: Brother Roger (1915–2005), a Swiss Protestant, settles there and shelters refugees from the war, including Jews.
  • From 1949 brothers commit for life to celibacy, simplicity and community.
  • Today: about a hundred brothers from Catholic and Protestant backgrounds, from many countries.
  • They accept no gifts for themselves; they live by their own work.
  • Brother Roger called the community a "parable of community": a small sign that divided Christians can live as one.
Mission 4

A week at Taizé

  • Every week, thousands of pilgrims, mostly young people, arrive from all over the world.
  • Prayer three times a day in the Church of Reconciliation:
    • short chants, repeated many times, in many languages;
    • a Bible reading;
    • a long silence.
  • Bible introduction by a brother, then small-group sharing across languages.
  • Practical work: cooking, cleaning, washing up. Simple food and simple living.
  • Friday: prayer around the cross; Saturday: a candlelit vigil of the resurrection.
  • Significance: trust in God, reconciliation, and taking prayer home to ordinary life.

Mission 4

Corrymeela

  • Founded 1965 by Ray Davey, a Presbyterian chaplain at Queen's University Belfast.
  • A residential centre at Ballycastle, on the north coast of County Antrim, Northern Ireland.
  • During the Troubles (late 1960s–1998) it brought Catholic and Protestant families, young people and groups together.
  • Today: work on sectarianism, racism, refugees and peace-building; members from many churches.
  • Significance: reconciliation (restoring broken relationships) as the heart of the Christian message (2 Corinthians 5:18, paraphrase).

Mission 4

Churches Together

  • Churches Together: national and local partnerships of churches (in England since 1990; also in Wales, Scotland and Ireland).
  • Members: Anglican, Catholic, Methodist, Baptist, Orthodox, Pentecostal and other churches.
  • Locally they:
    • hold joint Good Friday walks of witness and services;
    • keep the Week of Prayer for Christian Unity (18–25 January);
    • run shared projects: food banks, night shelters, school chaplaincy.
  • Churches keep their own beliefs and worship, but act together where they can.
Mission 4Diversity

Contrasting pilgrimages, differing views

  • Lourdes: Mary's reported visions; prayer for healing; processions, baths, Mass; mainly Catholic.
  • Iona: community life; justice and peace; simple shared worship; ecumenical.
  • Many Protestants who avoid shrines welcome communities like these: no relics or Marian devotion; Bible, prayer and service at the centre.
  • Catholics also go: popes have welcomed Taizé, and Catholic brothers belong to it.
  • Some Christians are wary:
    • some fear that a focus on unity blurs real differences in belief;
    • some evangelicals question repetitive chant (Matthew 6:7, paraphrase);
    • some think the Iona Community's political campaigning goes beyond the Church's role.

Mission 4Evaluate

"A week in a Christian community is worth more than a visit to a holy site."

  • For: a week of shared life can change habits, not just feelings.
  • For: meeting Christians of other churches lives out John 13:34–35.
  • For: the focus stays on Christ and on service, not on a place or object.
  • Against: holy sites link pilgrims to real events of faith: Jesus' life, the apostles, the saints.
  • Against: shrines offer the sacraments and a welcome for the sick that communities may not.
  • Against: people need different things; both kinds of pilgrimage are prayer, so ranking them may miss the point.
  • Judge it by: What is pilgrimage for? Does the place matter, or the people? What lasts after the journey home?

Mission 5

Agape: love that gives

  • Agape (Greek): selfless, unconditional love that seeks the good of others, expecting nothing back.
  • Christians see it in Jesus: healing, feeding, forgiving, and giving his life.
  • The Two Greatest Commandments: love God "with all your heart… soul… mind, and… strength", and "You shall love your neighbour as yourself." (Mark 12:30–31)
  • The Good Samaritan shows who my neighbour is: anyone in need, even an enemy. "Go and do likewise." (Luke 10:37)
  • Christian charity work begins here: love of God shown as love of neighbour.

Mission 5

Christian Aid

  • What it is: the aid and development agency of many British and Irish churches (Protestant, Anglican, Orthodox and others); founded 1945 to help refugees in post-war Europe; named Christian Aid in 1964.
  • What it does: emergency relief (floods, earthquakes, war); long-term development (farming, health, income); works through local partner organisations; campaigns on the causes of poverty (debt, unfair tax, climate change).
  • It helps people of any faith or none, and does not try to convert them.
  • Christian Aid Week (May): door-to-door collections by local church volunteers.
  • Why: love of neighbour (Mark 12:31); Christ in the needy (Matthew 25:40); every person made in God's image; justice (Micah 6:8). Its motto: "We believe in life before death."
Mission 5

Tearfund

  • What it is: a UK evangelical Christian relief and development agency, founded 1968 (originally the Evangelical Alliance Relief Fund).
  • How it works: mainly through local churches in poor communities. Churches are trained to look at their own needs and resources, then act together (church and community mobilisation).
  • What it does: disaster response; clean water, sanitation and hygiene; food and livelihoods; peace-building; campaigns on climate change and waste.
  • Integral mission: care for the whole person, body and spirit; where welcome, local churches share their faith as well as practical help.
  • Why: following Jesus, who preached good news to the poor and healed the sick (Luke 4:18); "let's not love in word only… but in deed and truth" (1 John 3:18).
Mission 5

CAFOD and Caritas

  • CAFOD (Catholic Agency for Overseas Development): official aid agency of the Catholic Church in England and Wales, founded 1962; grew out of Family Fast Days started by Catholic women in 1960.
  • Family Fast Day: eat a simple meal and give what you save (Lent and autumn).
  • Works with local Catholic and other partners: emergencies, health, water, farming, education; campaigns for justice.
  • Helps people regardless of religion.
  • Caritas Internationalis: the worldwide confederation of national Catholic charities (CAFOD is the member for England and Wales; SCIAF for Scotland, Trócaire for Ireland).
  • Why: Catholic social teaching: human dignity, the common good, solidarity, and a preferential option for the poor.

Mission 5Diversity

Different responses to the teaching on charity

  • Body and soul: many evangelicals (e.g. Tearfund) stress meeting spiritual and physical needs together; Christian Aid and CAFOD give help with no religious message attached. Catholic teaching says charity must never be used to win converts: love is freely given (Benedict XVI, Deus Caritas Est 31, 2005, paraphrase).
  • Charity or justice? Some focus on direct giving; others say the causes of poverty must be changed too: unfair trade, debt, climate (campaigns by Christian Aid and CAFOD).
  • Faith and works: Catholics teach that salvation is God's gift of grace, received in a faith that must show itself in works of love; many Protestants stress salvation by grace through faith alone, with good works as its fruit (Ephesians 2:8–10). Both see charity as essential to Christian life.
  • "The least of these": most read it as anyone in need; some scholars read it as Jesus' followers in need.
  • Public or private? Jesus said give in secret (Matthew 6:3–4); others value public campaigns and events that raise awareness.
Mission check

?After an earthquake, a Christian student says: "We should send help only through agencies that also preach the Gospel. Otherwise it isn't Christian work." Which reply best fits the Christian teaching in this mission?

Christians differ on whether to share faith in words alongside help, but most see practical love for anyone in need as serving Christ.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The local church is a community of believers, the centre of worship and belonging, serving its whole area: food banks, street pastors, family support, visiting.

Mission 2

  • Mission is the Church being sent to share God's love in word and action; evangelism is sharing the Gospel (Matthew 28:19–20).
  • Mission happens locally, nationally and globally, including new forms of church; Christians differ on how urgent conversion is.

Mission 3

  • Reconciliation: restoring relationships with God, between people and between churches (2 Corinthians 5:18; Matthew 5:9); e.g. Coventry, South Africa, Sant'Egidio.
  • The World Council of Churches (1948) works for unity, shared witness and service; the Catholic Church cooperates but is not a member.
  • Christians have been persecuted from Stephen to today; churches respond with prayer, practical help, campaigning and forgiveness (Hebrews 13:3; Matthew 5:44).

Mission 4

  • Iona: Columba's island (563); the ecumenical Iona Community (1938) joins prayer to justice and peace.
  • Taizé: Brother Roger's ecumenical community (1940); chants, silence, Bible sharing and work, mostly with young people.
  • Corrymeela and Churches Together put John 13:34–35 into practice through reconciliation and shared action.

Mission 5

  • Agape and the Two Greatest Commandments (Mark 12:30–31) motivate Christian charity.
  • So does the Sheep and the Goats (Matthew 25:31–46): Christians read it as teaching that serving people in need is serving Christ.
  • Christian Aid (1945), Tearfund (1968), CAFOD (1962) and Caritas: relief, development and campaigning, for people of any faith.
