Mission 1

Mission 1: Two atoms of the same element can have different masses. What is different inside them?

Make a guess: Two atoms of the same element can have different masses. What is different inside them?

Mission 1

Inside the atom

  • A tiny, positively charged nucleus, with electrons around it.
  • Nucleus: protons (charge +e) and neutrons (no charge). Together: nucleons.
  • Nucleus about 10⁻¹⁵ to 10⁻¹⁴ m across; atom about 10⁻¹⁰ m: 10 000 to 100 000 times larger.
  • A proton or neutron has about 1840 times an electron's mass. Nearly all the atom's mass is in the nucleus.
Mission 1

Nuclear notation

  • ᴬZ X. Example: ⁵⁶26Fe.
  • Z = proton number (atomic number): 26 protons.
  • A = nucleon number (mass number): 56 nucleons.
  • Neutrons: N = A − Z = 56 − 26 = 30.
  • Z fixes the element. Every iron nucleus has 26 protons.
Quick check
?A nucleus of potassium-40 is written in nuclear notation as ⁴⁰19K. Which statement about this nucleus is correct?
Quick check
?Predict first. Carbon-12 (¹²6C) and carbon-14 (¹⁴6C) are isotopes of carbon. Which statement about these two nuclides is correct?
Mission 1

Isotopes

  • Isotopes: same proton number, different neutron number.
  • Same element. Same number of electrons, so the same chemistry.
  • Different mass: extra neutrons add mass.
  • Carbon: ¹²6C (6 neutrons), ¹³6C (7), ¹⁴6C (8).
  • Some isotopes are unstable: carbon-14 is radioactive; carbon-12 is not.
Mission check

?Which pair are isotopes of the same element?

Same element means the same number of protons. Atoms of one element differ in mass because their nuclei hold different numbers of neutrons.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Blow an atom up to the size of a stadium. How big is the nucleus at its heart?

Make a guess: Blow an atom up to the size of a stadium. How big is the nucleus at its heart?

Mission 2

The plum-pudding model

  • 1897: Thomson discovers the electron. Atoms must contain positive charge too.
  • Plum-pudding model: positive charge spread evenly through the whole atom, electrons dotted inside.
  • Charge spread out → only weak electric fields anywhere in the atom.
  • Prediction: fast alpha particles pass through with only tiny deflections.
Mission 2

The experiment

  • Geiger and Marsden, in Rutherford's Manchester laboratory (1909–1913).
  • A narrow beam of alpha particles aimed at a very thin gold foil.
  • In a vacuum, so air does not stop the alpha particles.
  • A zinc sulfide screen flashes where an alpha particle hits. Counted through a microscope at many angles.
Quick check
?Predict first. Which statement about the results of the Geiger–Marsden–Rutherford alpha-particle scattering experiment is correct?
Mission 2

Results and conclusions

  • Almost all went straight through → the atom is mostly empty space.
  • Some were deflected through small angles → they are repelled by positive charge (plum pudding allows this too).
  • A very few were deflected by more than 90° → the positive charge and nearly all the mass are concentrated in a tiny core. Plum pudding cannot do this.
  • Rutherford (1911): the nuclear model. A small, dense, positive nucleus.
  • Bouncing back needs a strong electric repulsion from something much more massive than an alpha. Electrons are far too light.
Mission 2

The nuclear atom

  • Nucleus: protons and neutrons. Positive. Tiny.
  • Electrons around it, held by the electric attraction of the nucleus.
  • A proton or neutron has about 1840 times the mass of an electron.
  • So over 99.9% of an atom's mass is in the nucleus.
  • (The neutron was discovered in 1932.)
Mission 2

How small is the nucleus?

  • Atom: about 10⁻¹⁰ m across.
  • Nucleus: about 10⁻¹⁵ to 10⁻¹⁴ m across.
  • Atom ÷ nucleus: roughly 10⁴ to 10⁵ times.
  • Stadium-sized atom (about 200 m) → nucleus a few millimetres: about a pea on the centre spot.
  • Almost all the volume is empty; almost all the mass is in the pea.
Quick check
?A gold nucleus has radius 7.0 × 10⁻¹⁵ m and a gold atom has radius 1.4 × 10⁻¹⁰ m. Roughly what fraction of the atom's volume does the nucleus occupy?
Mission 2

How models changed

  • Early 1800s: Dalton, solid indivisible atoms.
  • 1897–1904: Thomson, electron discovered → plum pudding.
  • 1909–1911: alpha scattering → Rutherford's nuclear model.
  • 1913: Bohr adds energy levels. 1932: Chadwick finds the neutron.
  • Each model was replaced when new evidence did not fit it, after other scientists checked the results.
Mission check

?A gold nucleus is about 1.4 × 10⁻¹⁴ m across and a gold atom is about 2.9 × 10⁻¹⁰ m across. In a scale drawing the nucleus is a dot 1 mm across. How wide must the atom be drawn?

The nucleus is about 10⁴ to 10⁵ times smaller than the atom: a pea in a stadium, holding nearly all the mass.

Mission 3

Mission 3: An electron and a proton feel the same force between charged plates. Which swerves more, and by how much?

Make a guess: An electron and a proton feel the same force between charged plates. Which swerves more, and by how much?

Mission 3

The force on a charge in a field

  • F = EQ, the same everywhere in a uniform field.
  • Positive charge: force along the field (towards the negative plate).
  • Negative charge: force against the field (towards the positive plate).
  • Between parallel plates: E = V/d. 400 V across 2.0 cm → E = 2.0 × 10⁴ V m⁻¹.
  • Electron or proton there: |F| = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 2.0 × 10⁴ = 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ N. Same size, opposite directions.
Mission 3

Same force, different acceleration

  • Constant force → constant acceleration: a = F/m.
  • Electron: a = 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ / 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ = 3.5 × 10¹⁵ m s⁻².
  • Proton: a = 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ / 1.67 × 10⁻²⁷ = 1.9 × 10¹² m s⁻².
  • The electron's mass is about 1/1800 of the proton's, so its acceleration is about 1800 times greater.
  • Gravity is negligible: the electron's weight is about 10⁻²⁹ N, some 10¹⁴ times smaller than 3.2 × 10⁻¹⁵ N.
Quick check
?Two horizontal parallel plates, 2.5 cm apart in a vacuum, have a potential difference of 150 V between them. What is the magnitude of the acceleration of an electron between the plates? Gravity is negligible. Take e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C and me = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg.
Mission 3

Accelerating through a potential difference

  • Work done by the field = QV = gain in kinetic energy.
  • From rest: ½mv² = eV (electron or proton).
  • Electron gun: a heated filament releases electrons by thermionic emission. They are accelerated from the cathode to the anode in a vacuum and pass through a hole as a beam.
  • Worked example: electron through 2000 V.
  • v = √(2eV/m) = √(2 × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × 2000 / 9.11 × 10⁻³¹) = 2.65 × 10⁷ m s⁻¹.
  • That is about 9% of the speed of light, so ½mv² is still accurate.
Quick check
?Two parallel plates, 2.0 cm apart in a vacuum, have a potential difference of 500 V between them. An electron is released from rest at the negative plate and accelerates across the gap. What is its speed when it reaches the positive plate? Take e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C and me = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg.
Mission check

?A proton and an alpha particle are placed, one at a time, at the same point in a uniform electric field. The alpha particle has twice the charge of the proton and about four times its mass. Gravity is negligible. What is the ratio (acceleration of the alpha particle) / (acceleration of the proton)?

Same field, acceleration = charge × E ÷ mass. The electron and proton feel equal forces, but the electron has about 1/1800 of the mass: about 1800 times the acceleration, so it swerves far more.

Mission 4

Mission 4: How much energy must an electron have for its wavelength to be smaller than a proton?

Make a guess: How much energy must an electron have for its wavelength to be smaller than a proton?

Mission 4

λ from the accelerating p.d.

  • Electron accelerated from rest through p.d. V: eV = ½mv².
  • So mv = √(2meV), and λ = h/mv:
  • λ = h/√(2meV).
  • Higher V → shorter λ. λ ∝ 1/√V.
  • Valid while v ≪ c (up to a few tens of kV).
Mission 4

Worked example: choosing the anode voltage

  • Wanted: electrons with λ = 5.0 × 10⁻¹¹ m, below the size of an atom.
  • Rearrange: V = h²/(2meλ²).
  • V = (6.63 × 10⁻³⁴)² ÷ (2 × 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ × 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ × (5.0 × 10⁻¹¹)²).
  • V = 600 V (603).
  • For λ ≈ 10⁻¹⁰ m, the size of an atom: about 150 V.
Quick check
?An electron and a proton have the same kinetic energy, and both move at speeds much less than c. The mass of the proton is about 1840 times the mass of the electron. How do their de Broglie wavelengths compare?
Mission 4

Probing inside nucleons

  • To see detail of size d, you need a wavelength about d or smaller.
  • A nucleon is about 10⁻¹⁵ m across. So λ ≲ 10⁻¹⁵ m.
  • p = h/λ ≥ 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ ÷ 10⁻¹⁵ = 6.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ kg m s⁻¹.
  • At this momentum the electron moves at almost c. Its energy is about pc ≈ 2 × 10⁻¹⁰ J ≈ 1 GeV.
  • So probing nucleons needs high-energy accelerators. In the late 1960s, GeV electrons revealed point-like charges inside protons: quarks.
Quick check
?A team wants to resolve detail of about 10⁻¹⁶ m, a tenth of the size of a nucleon. At these energies, an electron's energy ≈ momentum × c. Roughly what electron energy do they need?
Mission check

?An electron microscope needs electrons with a de Broglie wavelength of 2.5 × 10⁻¹¹ m. Through what potential difference must the electrons be accelerated from rest? Take h = 6.63 × 10⁻³⁴ J s, m = 9.11 × 10⁻³¹ kg and e = 1.60 × 10⁻¹⁹ C.

Shorter wavelength needs more momentum, so more energy. Atom-sized λ needs about 150 V; proton-sized λ needs about 1 GeV.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Antimatter is real. How could you tell an antiparticle from its ordinary twin?

Make a guess: Antimatter is real. How could you tell an antiparticle from its ordinary twin?

Mission 5

Antimatter

  • Every particle has an antiparticle: the same mass, the opposite charge.
  • electron e⁻ ↔ positron e⁺ · proton p ↔ antiproton p̄ · neutron n ↔ antineutron n̄ · neutrino ν ↔ antineutrino ν̄.
  • Antiparticles are real: positrons come out of β⁺ decay, and hospitals use them in PET scans.
  • A particle and its antiparticle can annihilate: their mass becomes photons.
Quick check
?Predict first. The table shows the charge and mass of four particles that can be emitted when nuclei decay. One entry in the table is incorrect. Which row contains the incorrect entry?
Mission 5

Particles from nuclear decay

  • α particle (α, ⁴2He): 2 protons + 2 neutrons, a helium-4 nucleus. Charge +2e, mass ≈ 4 u.
  • Electron (e⁻, β⁻): charge −e, mass ≈ 0.00055 u.
  • Positron (e⁺, β⁺): charge +e, the same mass as the electron.
  • Neutrino (ν) and antineutrino (ν̄): no charge, negligible mass.
Mission 5

Reading off an antiparticle

  • Same: mass.
  • Opposite: charge (and other conserved quantities you will meet later).
  • Symbol: a bar over the particle's symbol: p̄, n̄, ν̄. Exception: e⁺ for the positron.
  • The antineutron is neutral, like the neutron, but it is not the same particle.
Quick check
?A proton has charge +e and mass 1.0073 u. Which describes the antiproton?
Mission check

?A beam of electrons enters the region between two oppositely charged parallel plates with velocity v, as shown, and follows track 3. A beam of positrons then enters with the same velocity. The figure shows four possible tracks, numbered 1 to 4 (not drawn to scale). Which track do the positrons follow?

An antiparticle has the same mass as its particle but the opposite charge. In a field it bends by the same amount, the opposite way: a mirror-image track.

Mission 6

Mission 6: Protons, electrons and neutrinos: what decides which family a particle belongs to?

Make a guess: Protons, electrons and neutrinos: what decides which family a particle belongs to?

Mission 6

Fundamental or composite?

  • Fundamental particle: no internal structure, as far as any experiment shows.
  • Fundamental: quarks and leptons.
  • Composite: made of smaller particles. Protons and neutrons are made of quarks.
  • Quarks are never found alone: they are always bound inside composite particles (quark confinement).
Mission 6

The particle families

  • Hadrons: made of quarks; feel the strong interaction.
  • Baryons: three quarks (proton, neutron). Antibaryons: three antiquarks (antiproton, antineutron).
  • Mesons: a quark and an antiquark, e.g. pions (π).
  • Kaons (K) are mesons too.
  • Leptons: fundamental; do not feel the strong interaction. Electron, neutrino.
  • Photons: a class of their own in the standard model.
Quick check
?A particle is made of an up quark and a down antiquark. How is it classified?
Mission 6

Leptons

  • Leptons are fundamental and feel no strong interaction.
  • Examples: electron and neutrino, and their antiparticles.
Mission 6

How particle physics is done

  • The quark model is symmetrical: quarks come in pairs, matching the pairs of leptons.
  • When the bottom quark was found (1977), the pattern demanded a partner: the top quark. It was found in 1995.
Mission check

?A beam from an accelerator contains antineutrons, negative pions (π⁻), positrons and protons. Which of these particles never feel the strong interaction?

Family is decided by what a particle is made of, not by mass, charge or lifetime, and an antiparticle stays in its partner's family. Quarks or antiquarks inside: a hadron, which feels the strong interaction. Fundamental: a lepton, which does not.

Mission 7

Mission 7: Protons and neutrons are built from the same two kinds of quark. Why is only one of them charged?

Make a guess: Protons and neutrons are built from the same two kinds of quark. Why is only one of them charged?

Mission 7

The quarks

  • Up (u): charge +⅔e, baryon number +⅓.
  • Down (d): charge −⅓e, baryon number +⅓.
  • Antiquarks (ū, d̄): every number reversed. ū: −⅔e, d̄: +⅓e, baryon number −⅓.
Mission 7

Building baryons

  • Three quarks. Add the charges.
  • Proton uud: ⅔ + ⅔ − ⅓ = +1.
  • Neutron udd: ⅔ − ⅓ − ⅓ = 0.
  • Antiproton ūūd̄: −1. Antineutron ūd̄d̄: 0.
  • Baryon number: 3 × ⅓ = +1 (antibaryons: −1).
Quick check
?An antibaryon is made only of up and down antiquarks and has a charge of −2e. What is its quark content?
Mission 7

Building mesons

  • A quark and an antiquark.
  • Baryon number: ⅓ − ⅓ = 0.
  • π⁺ = ud̄: ⅔ + ⅓ = +1.
  • π⁻ = dū: −⅓ − ⅔ = −1. The π⁻ is the antiparticle of the π⁺.
Quick check
?A meson is built from up and down quarks and antiquarks only. Which charge can it not have?
Mission check

?Quarks combine only in certain ways. Which of these combinations can not exist as a particle?

Quark charges are fractions: up +⅔, down −⅓. A proton (uud) totals +1; a neutron (udd) totals 0.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Nucleus: protons + neutrons (nucleons). ᴬZ X: Z protons, A nucleons, A − Z neutrons.
  • Isotopes: same Z, different numbers of neutrons; same chemistry, different mass.

Mission 2

  • Alpha scattering: most pass straight through, a very few bounce back → a tiny, dense, positive nucleus.
  • The nucleus (protons and neutrons) holds over 99.9% of the mass and is 10⁴–10⁵ times smaller than the atom.

Mission 3

  • In a uniform field F = EQ (positive charges along E, negative against it), so a = EQ/m is constant; light particles accelerate most.
  • Through a potential difference V, a charge gains kinetic energy QV.

Mission 4

  • λ = h/√(2meV): a higher accelerating p.d. gives a shorter wavelength (λ ∝ 1/√V, for v ≪ c).
  • Resolving structure inside nucleons needs λ ≲ 10⁻¹⁵ m, so GeV electrons.

Mission 5

  • Every particle has an antiparticle with the same mass and the opposite charge.
  • Pairs to know: e⁻/e⁺, p/p̄, n/n̄, ν/ν̄; a bar marks the antiparticle.

Mission 6

  • Hadrons (made of quarks; feel the strong interaction): baryons (qqq), antibaryons (q̄q̄q̄), mesons (qq̄). Leptons: fundamental, no strong interaction.

Mission 7

  • u: +⅔e; d: −⅓e; each quark has baryon number +⅓; antiquarks have everything reversed.
  • Proton uud (+1), neutron udd (0); antiproton ūūd̄, antineutron ūd̄d̄; π⁺ ud̄, π⁻ dū.
