Mission 1

Most English speakers aren't native speakers. Whose English is it?

Make a guess: Most English speakers aren't native speakers. Whose English is it?

Language diversity · global EnglishScreen 1

How English went global

  • Settlement: from the 1600s, speakers moved to North America, then Australia and New Zealand; English became the main first language there.
  • Colonial rule: in India, Nigeria and Singapore, English became a language of government, law and education alongside local languages.
  • Globalisation: since 1945, business, science, aviation, film and the internet made it the main international lingua franca.
  • David Crystal (English as a Global Language, 2nd ed. 2003) argues a language goes global through the power of its speakers, not because it is simpler.

Pidgins, creoles and new Englishes

  • A pidgin is a contact language with no native speakers: simple grammar, vocabulary mostly from one language.
  • A creole develops when children learn a pidgin as their first language; its grammar and vocabulary expand.
  • Tok Pisin, an official language of Papua New Guinea, is now a creole: gras (hair, from 'grass'), haus sik (hospital), bilong (of, from 'belong').
  • New Englishes coin forms: Indian English prepone (bring forward), the Singapore English particle lah.

Whose English? Attitudes and debates

  • English as a lingua franca (ELF): English between speakers with different first languages; success means being understood, not sounding native (Jennifer Jenkins, 2000).
  • Robert Phillipson (Linguistic Imperialism, 1992) argues English's dominance is kept up by structural and cultural inequalities with other languages.
  • Crystal (2003) sees a global language as useful for international contact while warning about threats to other languages.
  • Prescriptive attitudes persist: Singapore's government launched the Speak Good English Movement (2000) to steer speakers from Singlish to standard English.

Mission checkScreen 9

?Most English speakers aren't native speakers. Whose English is it?

English now has many owners. It spread through settlement, colonial rule and globalisation, and today most speakers use it as an additional language, developing their own norms. But ownership is also a question of power: attitudes still rank some varieties above others, which is why linguists debate it.

Language diversity · sociolect, age and ethnicityScreen 1

Sociolect and the social variables

  • A sociolect is the variety used by a social group; an idiolect is one person's own mix.
  • Social variables: class, age, gender, ethnicity, occupation and friendship networks all shape speech.
  • Age-grading: teenagers use most slang and non-standard forms, then often move towards the standard at work.
  • Change in progress: if young people keep a new form as they age, the language changes. Adolescents are often its leaders.
  • For Individual Variation, describe how a speaker's choices construct identity; don't judge them.
Language diversity · sociolect, age and ethnicityScreen 2

Study: Eckert, jocks and burnouts (fieldwork 1980–82)

MethodTwo years of ethnography in a Detroit suburban high school ('Belten High'), plus shorter visits to four other schools
GroupsJocks: school-oriented, linked to middle-class culture. Burnouts: locally and urban-oriented, linked to working-class culture
FindingsBurnouts used more advanced forms of a vowel change spreading in the region's cities; what students did and who they did it with mattered more than family background
Key ideaCommunity of practice: a group that meets around a shared activity and shares ways of speaking (Eckert and McConnell-Ginet, 1992)
  • Evaluate: one US school, 40 years ago; but it shows speakers actively use variation to build identity.
Language diversity · sociolect, age and ethnicityScreen 3

Study: the Linguistic Innovators project (2004–07)

WhoKerswill, Cheshire, Fox and Torgersen; funded by the ESRC
MethodRecorded 16–19-year-olds and over-70s in Hackney (inner London) and Havering (outer London); a follow-up project (2007–10) added speakers aged 4 to 50
FindingsA new variety, Multicultural London English (MLE), was strongest among inner-London teenagers; it was not tied to one ethnicity, and speakers used more MLE features the more friends they had from different language backgrounds
ExplanationA feature pool: children learn English from each other, drawing on Cockney, Caribbean Creoles, African and South Asian Englishes, learners' English and Standard English
  • MLE is a multiethnolect: a variety shaped by many languages and used across ethnic groups.

Language investigation · question, data, methodScreen 1

From topic to question

  • Start with an area of interest, then narrow it to a research question your own data can answer.
  • A good question is specific, answerable from the data and often comparative (two groups, two settings).
  • You may add a hypothesis, a prediction to test, but your conclusion can reject it.

Language investigation · question, data, methodScreen 3

Collecting data

  • Choose the type: spoken, written, multimodal, word lists, or attitudes (surveys, interviews).
  • Collect enough to see patterns, and keep the conditions comparable.
  • Observer's paradox (Labov, 1972): we want to study how people speak when not being observed, but we can only find out by observing them. Reduce it by recording for longer and using later sections.
  • Transcribe spoken data with a key (pauses, overlaps); give speakers labels.
Language investigation · question, data, methodScreen 4

Choosing methods

  • Quantitative: count features and compare (e.g. number of questions per speaker per hour), shown in tables or charts.
  • Qualitative: close analysis of examples in context, using the language levels and frameworks (e.g. politeness, turn-taking).
  • Strong investigations combine both: counts show the pattern, examples explain it.
  • Evaluate your method: sample size, what you couldn't control, what you'd do next.
Language investigation · question, data, methodScreen 5

Ethics

  • Informed consent: tell people what you're studying and get their permission, spoken or written.
  • Under 16: you also need a parent's or carer's permission.
  • Right to withdraw: anyone can pull out, even after agreeing.
  • Anonymity: no real names or identifying details; use labels (Speaker A, Parent 1). Keep recordings secure.
  • No secret recording: BAAL guidance calls deception unacceptable. If knowing the aim would change how people speak, give the general purpose, or explain straight after and delete the data if anyone refuses.

?You've recorded your family at dinner. What question could it answer?

A focused, comparative question the recording can actually answer, such as how two speakers' questions differ in form and function. Choose data that fits the question, combine counting with close analysis, reduce the observer's paradox, and get informed consent before you record.

Recap

Big ideas

  • English spread by settlement, colonial rule and globalisation; Crystal (2003) links a language's global spread to its speakers' power.
  • Kachru (1985): Inner (norm-providing), Outer (norm-developing) and Expanding (norm-dependent) circles; criticised for sorting nations, not speakers.
  • Pidgins have no native speakers; creoles do (Tok Pisin). New Englishes create their own forms.
  • Evaluate attitudes: ELF (Jenkins), linguistic imperialism (Phillipson), prescriptive campaigns.
  • For Component 3, research the pre-released subtopic's origins, features, varieties and attitudes.
  • A sociolect is a group's variety; age, class, ethnicity, gender and friendship networks all shape it, and teenagers often lead change.
  • Eckert's jocks and burnouts show speakers using variation to build identity within communities of practice.
  • MLE (Linguistic Innovators, 2004–07) is a multiethnolect formed from a feature pool in multiethnic friendship groups; labels like 'Jafaican' misdescribe it.
  • Narrow a topic into a specific, answerable, often comparative research question; a hypothesis is optional and can be rejected.
  • Match data and method to the question: combine quantitative counts with qualitative analysis, and evaluate your method's limits. Reduce the observer's paradox.
  • Ethics: informed consent (parents too for under-16s), right to withdraw, anonymity, no secret recording.
