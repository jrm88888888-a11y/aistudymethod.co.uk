Mission 1

The nucleotide

  • DNA and RNA are polymers. Their monomers are nucleotides.
  • Each nucleotide has three parts:
  • a pentose (5-carbon sugar) in the middle
  • a phosphate group, bonded to the sugar
  • a nitrogen-containing base, also bonded to the sugar
  • DNA nucleotide: deoxyribose + phosphate + one base: Adenine, Cytosine, Guanine or Thymine.
  • RNA nucleotide: ribose + phosphate + one base: A, C, G or Uracil.
  • Say: "a nucleotide contains a base" — not: "a nucleotide is a base".

Mission 1

Nucleotides join into a strand

  • Condensation: the phosphate of one nucleotide bonds to the sugar of the next. Water is released.
  • The bond is a phosphodiester bond.
  • Repeated, this builds a sugar–phosphate backbone: one continuous chain of covalent bonds. Strong.
  • The bases stick out from the backbone. Each is bonded only to its own sugar.
  • n nucleotides in a chain: n − 1 bonds, n − 1 waters released.
  • Hydrolysis breaks phosphodiester bonds, using water, releasing nucleotides.
  • Say: "the backbone is sugar–phosphate–sugar" — not: "the bases are joined into a chain".
Mission 1

Two strands: the double helix

  • DNA has two polynucleotide strands.
  • Bases on opposite strands pair by hydrogen bonds.
  • Pairing is complementary: A with T, G with C. Never A–G or C–T.
  • The strands are antiparallel: they run in opposite directions.
  • The paired strands twist into a double helix.
  • One hydrogen bond is weak. Thousands along a molecule hold the strands firmly together.
  • Yet each can break: the strands can be separated without breaking the backbone.
  • Say: "complementary bases" — not: "matching" or "identical bases".
Mission 1

Purines and pyrimidines

  • Purines: A and G. Two rings (a double ring).
  • Pyrimidines: C, T and U. One ring.
  • Every base pair is one purine + one pyrimidine: A–T, G–C.
  • Say: "a purine pairs with a pyrimidine" — not: "purines pair with purines".

Mission 2

The message is in the sequence

  • The sugar–phosphate backbone is the same all along. Only the bases vary.
  • The order of bases, the base sequence, is the information.
  • Any base can follow any other: nothing fixes the order.
  • Same four bases, different order, different message. Like letters spelling different words.
  • Say: "the information is in the base sequence" — not: "in the shape of the helix".
Mission 2

Pairing copies the message and sends it out

  • Copy: the strands separate; each is a template. Free nucleotides pair with complementary bases. Two identical molecules result.
  • Express: a gene's sequence is copied into messenger RNA (mRNA), again by complementary pairing (A with U).
  • One rule, two jobs. Details: DNA replication, transcription.
Mission 2Practical

Getting DNA out of cells

  • PAG9: purification of DNA by precipitation.
  • Mash plant tissue (e.g. onion or kiwi fruit) in detergent + salt solution. Filter.
  • Detergent: breaks up the phospholipid membranes (cell surface and nuclear envelope).
  • Salt: Na⁺ ions neutralise DNA's negative phosphate groups, so the molecules can clump.
  • Protease (optional): digests proteins bound to the DNA.
  • Gently pour ice-cold ethanol on top. DNA is insoluble in it: white strands precipitate at the boundary. Spool them on a glass rod.
  • Key conditions: ethanol ice-cold, layered on top without mixing.
  • No calculation: the result is qualitative. The strands are millions of DNA molecules, with some RNA and protein: not one molecule.
Quick check
?In a DNA extraction, sodium chloride is added to the detergent solution. Why does the salt help the DNA to precipitate when ice-cold ethanol is added?
Mission check

?Two DNA molecules from two hypothetical species of bacteria each contain the same number of nucleotides, and in both, 30% of the bases are adenine, 30% thymine, 20% guanine and 20% cytosine. The two molecules carry different genetic information. Which statement best explains how this is possible?

Four bases are plenty: the message is in their order, and the order can be anything, at any length.

Mission 3

Meet ATP

  • ATP: adenosine triphosphate.
  • Adenine (a base) + ribose (a pentose) + three phosphate groups.
  • Adenine + ribose = adenosine.
  • ATP and ADP are phosphorylated nucleotides.
  • ADP: adenosine diphosphate. Two phosphate groups.
  • Same base and sugar as an RNA nucleotide carrying adenine.
Quick check
?Which statement about ATP (adenosine triphosphate) is correct?
Mission 3

Hydrolysis releases energy

  • ATP + H2O → ADP + Pi (inorganic phosphate).
  • Energy is released: enough for many single tasks in the cell.
  • Breaking any bond takes energy. Energy is released overall because ADP and Pi are more stable than ATP.
  • Say: "energy is released when ATP is hydrolysed" — not: "energy is stored in a high-energy bond".
Mission 3

Coupling: putting the energy to work

  • ATP hydrolysis is coupled to an energy-requiring process: one enzyme links them, so they happen together.
  • What ATP powers:
  • active transport of ions and molecules across membranes
  • synthesis of macromolecules (anabolism), e.g. proteins, DNA
  • movement: muscle contraction, cilia, chromosomes in cell division
  • Say: "active transport uses ATP" — not: "diffusion and osmosis use ATP".
Quick check
?Which statement correctly describes a life process that ATP supplies with energy?
Mission 3

Remaking ATP: the ATP–ADP cycle

  • ADP + Pi → ATP + H2O: a condensation reaction.
  • Energy comes from respiration (every cell) and photosynthesis (in chloroplasts).
  • ATP is not stockpiled. A cell keeps a small pool and recycles it, second by second.
  • A resting adult turns over roughly their own body mass of ATP each day.
  • So plants, animals and microorganisms must all respire: to keep remaking ATP for active transport, synthesis and movement.
Quick check
?During a sprint, the small store of ATP in muscle fibres is hydrolysed and then re-formed several times over. Which statement about the energy transfers is correct?
Mission 3

Why not spend glucose directly?

  • Glucose releases its energy in many enzyme-controlled steps. ATP: one step, one enzyme.
  • One glucose holds far more energy than one reaction needs. ATP comes in small packets.
  • About 30 kJ per mole of ATP hydrolysed; about 2870 kJ per mole of glucose fully oxidised.
  • Released immediately, right where it is needed.
  • Soluble: moves freely within the cell.
  • Quickly remade from ADP + Pi.
Quick check
?A cell needs an energy currency that can supply many different reactions in the cytoplasm. Which property makes ATP suitable for this role?
Mission check

?A muscle fibre holds only enough ATP for a few seconds of hard contraction. Yet a marathon runner's leg muscles keep contracting for hours. Which statement explains this?

A small, fast-recycled pool of ATP, refilled by respiration, beats spending glucose directly.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Before a cell divides, it copies all of its DNA. How does the copy come out almost perfect?

Make a guess: Before a cell divides, it copies all of its DNA. How does the copy come out almost perfect?

Mission 4

Why copy DNA?

  • Every cell made by mitosis needs a complete copy of the DNA.
  • So DNA is copied before every mitosis: for growth, repair and asexual reproduction.
  • It is also copied once before meiosis, the division that makes gametes.
  • Copying happens in interphase, before the cell divides.
  • The copies are exact: identical base sequences.
Quick check
?A cut in the skin heals as new skin cells are produced by cell division. Why must DNA replication take place before each of these divisions?
Mission 4

Replication, step by step

  • Helicase unwinds the double helix and breaks the hydrogen bonds between complementary bases. The strands separate.
  • Each separated strand is a template.
  • Free DNA nucleotides are attracted to the exposed bases and pair by complementary base pairing: A with T, C with G. Hydrogen bonds hold each pair.
  • DNA polymerase joins adjacent nucleotides of the new strand in condensation reactions, building its sugar–phosphate backbone.
  • Result: two molecules, each with one original strand and one new strand. This is semi-conservative replication.
  • Say: "helicase breaks the hydrogen bonds between bases" — not: "helicase cuts the DNA".
  • Say: "DNA polymerase joins nucleotides to each other" — not: "DNA polymerase pairs the bases". Base pairs form on their own.
Quick check
?A drug inhibits helicase but has no effect on DNA polymerase. What will happen to DNA replication in a cell treated with the drug?
Mission 4

Worked example: complementary, not identical

One template strand reads G A T T C A.

  • Pair each base: G→C, A→T, T→A, T→A, C→G, A→T.
  • New strand: C T A A G T.
  • The new strand is complementary to its template.
  • It is identical to the strand that was paired with this template before.
  • The other template is copied the same way. Both daughter molecules match the parent.
  • Pairing is set by hydrogen bonding: each base can pair stably only with its complement.
Quick check
?During replication, a section of a template strand with the base sequence TACGGA is copied. Which statement describes the new strand built on this section?
Mission 4

Accurate, but not perfect

  • Every new strand is built against an original template, so the sequence is conserved.
  • Only complementary bases pair stably, so nearly every nucleotide added is correct.
  • Very rarely, a wrong base is added. If not corrected, it is copied in every later replication.
  • These errors are random and spontaneous: they can happen anywhere in the DNA, with no outside cause.
  • Say: "a random, spontaneous mutation" — not: "a mutation that happens because the cell needs it".
Mission check

?A short section of a DNA molecule has one strand reading GATTC, paired base by base with a second strand reading CTAAG. The molecule is replicated once. Which statement describes the two DNA molecules produced?

Each old strand is the pattern for its new partner, and base pairing makes that pattern hard to misread. That is why the copy is almost perfect.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Nucleotide = pentose + phosphate + base. Condensation joins nucleotides into a strand with a covalent sugar–phosphate backbone.
  • DNA: two strands held by hydrogen bonds between complementary bases (A–T, G–C), twisted into a double helix. RNA: ribose, uracil, one strand.
  • The two strands are antiparallel.

Mission 2

  • The base sequence is the information; the backbone is the same throughout. Complementary pairing lets the sequence be copied and passed to RNA.

Mission 3

  • ATP = adenine + ribose + three phosphates. Hydrolysis to ADP + Pi releases energy in a small, immediate packet; respiration supplies the energy to remake it.

Mission 4

  • Helicase unwinds DNA and breaks hydrogen bonds; free nucleotides pair with each exposed template; DNA polymerase joins them by condensation.
  • Each new molecule has one original and one new strand: semi-conservative replication, giving identical copies.
