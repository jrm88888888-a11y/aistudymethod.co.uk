Paper 1: Shakespeare & Post-1914 Literature

This mini-lesson teaches the skills and structure of Edexcel GCSE (9-1) English Literature Paper 1 (1ET0/01): how to analyse a printed Shakespeare extract and link it to the whole play, how to write about your post-1914 text from memory, and how to hit the Assessment Objectives AO1, AO2 and AO3 (context). It drills terminology, quotations and technique — not the finished essay.

Work through each screen, answer the questions as you go (some ask you to pick the strongest point, some to name a method) and collect ⭐ stars. Press Start when you're ready.

The shape of the paper

Two sections, one with an extract, one from memory

Paper 1 is a 1 hour 45 minute exam worth 80 marks — 50% of your whole GCSE. It is closed book: you get no copy of your texts, apart from one printed Shakespeare extract. You answer two questions:

  • Section A — Shakespeare (40 marks): two parts on your set play. Part (a): analyse a printed extract closely — this part rewards AO2 (method & effect). Part (b): write about the whole play, from memory, on the same theme — this part rewards AO1 and AO3 (context).
  • Section B — Post-1914 British play or novel (40 marks): one essay (choice of two) on your set text, written entirely from memory — there is no extract. Rewards AO1, AO2 and AO3 (context).

Set texts differ by school: your Shakespeare might be Macbeth, The Tempest, Romeo and Juliet, Much Ado About Nothing, Twelfth Night or The Merchant of Venice; your post-1914 text might be An Inspector Calls, Blood Brothers, Journey's End, Animal Farm, Lord of the Flies, Anita and Me or The Woman in Black. The skills here work for any of them.

The heart of AO2

Method → effect, every time

Naming a technique earns almost nothing. The marks come from linking the method to the effect it creates on the reader or audience:

method + evidence → effect on reader/audiencee.g. "the imperative 'Look' (method) forces the audience to share the character's shock (effect)"

Useful methods to spot in prose & drama: metaphor, simile, personification, imagery, symbolism, pathetic fallacy, semantic field, dramatic irony, foreshadowing, soliloquy, aside, stage directions, narrative voice, structure and form.

Golden rule: never stop at "this is a metaphor." Always finish the sentence: "…which suggests / creates / emphasises / makes the audience feel …".

Worked model · annotation

Annotating a Shakespeare extract (Section A part a)

Here is a short, made-up dramatic line (not a set text) annotated the way you'd mark up a real printed extract in Section A:

Out, dark whisper! Will these hands
ne'er be clean? The blood still speaks,
though all the seas could not wash me.
  • "Out, dark" — imperative + oath: a mind in frantic, guilty panic.
  • "blood" — symbol/motif: guilt made physical and impossible to escape.
  • "the seas could not wash me" — hyperbole: the guilt is vast and permanent.
  • "still speaks" — personification: the crime haunts and accuses the speaker.

Notice the pattern: each note is quote → method (with terminology) → effect. That is exactly the sentence shape your part (a) analysis needs.

Context skill · AO3

Context: the world behind the text

AO3 asks you to show you understand the context a text was written in. On Edexcel Paper 1 it is credited in the Shakespeare whole-play part (b) (worth 5 of its 20 marks) and across Section B (your post-1914 text). Useful contexts include:

  • Macbeth (Shakespeare, part b): Jacobean beliefs about kingship and the divine right, the Gunpowder Plot, witchcraft and James I.
  • An Inspector Calls (Priestley): Edwardian class and inequality, the 1912 setting, Priestley's socialism, responsibility.
  • Journey's End / War writing: the reality of the First World War, the trenches, duty and fear.
  • Animal Farm / Lord of the Flies: revolution and power, the Second World War era, human nature.

Misconception: context is not a history essay pasted in. Whether in the Shakespeare whole-play part (b) or in Section B, one relevant clause that explains why a writer's choice lands the way it does beats a page of dates.

Argument skill

Answer with a thesis, not a plot summary

Open with a thesis — a clear line of argument that answers the exact question about the writer's method and purpose. Everything after it is proof.

Model opening (generic)

Question: "How does Shakespeare present ambition in this extract and in the play as a whole?"

Thesis: "Shakespeare presents ambition as a corrupting force that destroys those who let it override conscience, using the supernatural and violent imagery to dramatise its cost."

Why it works: it takes a position (ambition corrupts), names methods (supernatural, imagery) and points to a purpose/effect (its cost). Now every paragraph proves one part of it.

Evidence done well

Embed quotes — keep them short

Because Paper 1 is closed book, learn a bank of short, flexible quotations — you'll need them from memory for the whole play (part b) and the whole of Section B. Weave them into your own sentence rather than dropping in a whole line:

Clumsy vs embedded

❌ "The Inspector is powerful. The quote is: 'We are members of one body. We are responsible for each other.'"

✔ "Priestley's declarative 'one body' fuses the characters into a single moral unit, hammering home his message of collective responsibility."

Misconception: long quotations don't earn more marks — they waste time and bury your analysis. Quote the few words you'll actually analyse.

Context & AO3 (Shakespeare part b & Section B)

Context serves the analysis

In the Shakespeare whole-play part (b) and in Section B, context can enrich your points — but only if it explains a choice in the text. It must be woven in, not bolted on as a history lecture:

Bolted-on vs woven-in

❌ "Britain in 1912 was very unequal. There were rich and poor. Now back to the play."

✔ "Setting the play in 1912, Priestley makes the Birlings' complacency dramatically ironic — his post-war audience knew the disasters their smugness ignored, sharpening his social critique."

Misconception: context is not a paragraph you paste in. One relevant clause that illuminates a method or theme beats a page of dates.

Exam technique

Planning under the clock

Split your 1h 45m evenly — roughly 50 minutes on each section, since both are worth 40 marks. Spend the first 5 minutes of each planning:

  • Underline the key words in the question (the focus + the command word "how").
  • Section A: read the printed extract twice, annotating 3–4 methods and effects for part (a); then plan whole-play points for part (b).
  • Section B: plan from memory — 3 points, each with a short quote, a method + effect, and woven-in context (AO3).
  • Write a one-line thesis, then write. In Section A part (b), remember to weave in relevant context (AO3) as well as your whole-play response.

Closed book: only the Shakespeare extract is printed — everything else is from memory. Learn a bank of short, flexible quotations in advance.

Worked model · Section A

A paragraph in action

Watch how one Section A paragraph moves from the printed extract to the whole play, with method and effect:

Model paragraph (generic)

"In the extract, the imperative 'Out, damned spot' shows guilt has overwhelmed the character's mind, the harsh plosive 'd' sounds mirroring her frantic self-disgust. This physical, unwashable guilt develops across the play: what began as ambition curdles into a conscience she cannot silence, until her sleepwalking dramatises a mind that will not rest — deepening Shakespeare's presentation of ambition's ruinous cost."

Spot the moves: extract analysed (method + effect, AO2), then linked to the whole play for part (b) — a sustained personal response (AO1). In part (b) you also earn credit for weaving in relevant context (AO3).

Avoid these traps

The five marks-killers

  • Feature-spotting: naming techniques without explaining their effect.
  • Extract-only: in Section A part (b), never ranging out to the whole play.
  • Context bolted on: dumping a block of history instead of weaving it into a point (context earns AO3 in Shakespeare part (b) and Section B).
  • Fat quotes: long copied lines instead of short, embedded ones.
  • Forgetting context in part (b): skipping the 5 AO3 marks for context in the Shakespeare whole-play answer.

Fix them with one habit: every sentence should make a point, prove it with a short quote, or explain a method's effect — and weave context into a point wherever it counts (Shakespeare part (b) and Section B).

Recap

The skills to take into the exam

Paper 1: A Shakespeare (40) · B Post-1914 text (40) — 80 marks, 50%, 1h 45m, closed book

Section A: part (a) printed extract (AO2), part (b) whole play from memory + context (AO1 + AO3)

Section B: one essay on your post-1914 text, from memory — context (AO3) credited here

AO1: argue a thesis, support with short quotes · AO2: method + terminology → effect

AO3: weave in context — Shakespeare part (b) and Section B

Timing: ~50 min each section; plan 5 min; weave context into part (b)
