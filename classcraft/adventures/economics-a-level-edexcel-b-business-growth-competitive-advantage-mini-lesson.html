Mission 1

Mission 1: Why can a giant firm make things more cheaply than a small one, and when does size start to hurt?

Make a guess: Why can a giant firm make things more cheaply than a small one, and when does size start to hurt?

Mission 1

Cost per unit as a firm grows

  • Economies of scale: long-run average cost falls as the scale of output rises.
  • Diseconomies of scale: long-run average cost rises as the scale of output rises.
  • They are long run: the firm grows by changing all its inputs, including plant size.
  • A firm's own growth moves it along its LRAC curve.
  • Say: "economies of scale lower average cost" — not: "lower costs". Total cost still rises with output.
Mission 1

Internal economies of scale

  • Internal economies come from the firm's own growth.
TypeWhy average cost falls
TechnicalBigger, specialised machines; mass production; larger containers cost less per unit of capacity
ManagerialSpecialist managers (finance, marketing, engineering) each run a bigger output
FinancialLenders see big firms as less risky: lower interest rates, more ways to raise money
PurchasingBulk buying earns discounts on inputs
MarketingOne advert or brand spread over many more sales
Risk-bearingMany products and markets: a fall in one is offset by others
  • Many are about indivisibilities: some inputs (a machine, an advert, a specialist) cannot be bought in small slices.
Mission 1

Worked example: why bigger ships carry more cheaply

  • Think of a cargo hold as a cube (simplified model).
  • Side 1 → 2: surface area (steel to build) 6 → 24, ×4. Volume (cargo it holds) 1 → 8, ×8.
  • Steel per unit of cargo: 6 ÷ 1 = 6 → 24 ÷ 8 = 3. It halves.
  • This is the law of increased dimensions, a technical economy of scale.
  • A bigger ship also needs only a slightly larger crew and engine per container carried.
  • Real data: MSC Irina, delivered in 2023, carries 24,346 twenty-foot containers.
Quick check
?Which is an example of a financial economy of scale?
Mission 1

Diseconomies of scale

  • As a firm grows very large, average cost can rise. Main causes:
  • Communication: more layers of managers; messages are slow and distorted.
  • Coordination and control: hard to keep many sites and departments working together; harder to check that staff do their jobs well.
  • Motivation: workers feel like a small part of a huge machine; absence and staff turnover rise.
  • Skills: growth can outrun the supply of skilled managers and workers.
  • Corporate culture: the informal ways of working that suited a small firm may not fit a large one; merged firms can have clashing cultures.
  • These are internal diseconomies: they come from the firm's own size.
Quick check
?A firm has grown to 40,000 employees in 30 countries. Decisions take months to pass through layers of managers, and staff surveys show falling motivation. Its cost per unit is now rising as output grows. What is the best explanation?
Mission 1

Economies of scale as a barrier to entry

  • Illustrative data. An established firm makes 500,000 units a year: average cost £4.
  • A new firm can at first sell only 50,000 units: average cost £7.
  • At a market price of £5: the established firm earns £5 − £4 = £1 per unit; the new firm loses £5 − £7 = −£2 per unit.
  • So the new firm cannot match the established firm's price until it grows, and it may not survive long enough to grow.
  • Economies of scale act as a barrier to entry: they protect established firms and help keep markets concentrated.
Mission 1Evaluate

Is bigger better for consumers?

It depends on …

  • Whether cost savings are passed on: with strong competition, lower average cost means lower prices; a dominant firm may keep the savings as profit.
  • Diseconomies: past a point, more size raises average cost.
  • Barriers to entry: economies of scale protect established firms, which can weaken the pressure to keep costs and prices down.
  • Problems of growth: fast growth can strain communication, skills and corporate culture.
  • Judge: size can lower costs, but whether consumers gain depends on competition and how the firm is run.
Mission check

?A food firm grows over several years (illustrative data). At 10,000 meals a week its total cost is 200,000. At 50,000 meals it is 600,000. At 120,000 meals it is 1,800,000. What do these figures show?

Growing can lower cost per unit, but only up to a point; judge it by average cost, not total cost.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why do some firms grow by buying up other firms, while most firms stay small?

Make a guess: Most businesses never grow beyond a handful of people. What is the main reason?

Mission 2

Why grow?

  • Economies of scale: a bigger firm can cut its average cost (bulk buying, specialist machines, cheaper finance).
  • Market power: a larger market share gives more control over price and less threat from rivals.
  • Spreading risk: selling more products or in more countries means one bad market hurts less.
  • Survival: a large firm is harder to take over and better able to ride out a downturn.
  • Managers' goals: pay, status and power often rise with size (the principal–agent problem, another lesson).
  • Firms set growth as an objective for these reasons, and to raise profit over time.
Mission 2

Two routes: organic or external

  • Organic (internal) growth: the firm expands by itself: new outlets, new products, more capacity, often paid for from retained profit.
  • Slower and lower risk; the firm keeps its culture and control.
  • Inorganic growth is Edexcel B's term for external growth.
  • External growth joins firms together:
  • Merger: two firms agree to combine into one.
  • Takeover (acquisition): one firm buys a controlling share of another, with or without its agreement.
  • Faster: it buys market share, brands and skills at once. But it is costly, risky, and the two cultures may clash.
Mission 2

Three directions of integration

  • Think of a chain of production: raw materials → manufacturing → wholesale → retail.
  • Horizontal integration: firms at the same stage of the same industry join (two car makers).
  • Vertical integration: firms at different stages of the same chain join.
  • Backward: towards the supplier (a car maker buys a steel works).
  • Forward: towards the customer (a car maker buys a chain of car showrooms).
  • Conglomerate integration: firms in unrelated industries join (a car maker buys a hotel chain).
  • Say: "backward vertical integration" for buying a supplier — not: "forward", which means moving towards the final customer.
Quick check
?A coffee-shop chain buys a company that roasts coffee beans and already supplies the chain. What type of integration is this?
Mission 2

Motives for each type

TypeMain motivesMain risks
HorizontalEconomies of scale; bigger market share; removes a rivalCompetition authorities may block it; may raise prices for consumers
Vertical (backward)Secures supplies; controls quality and cost; captures the supplier's profit marginLocked into one supplier even if cheaper ones appear
Vertical (forward)Controls how the product reaches customers; captures the retailer's margin; may shut rivals out of outletsFirm may lack retail skills
ConglomerateSpreads risk across unrelated markets; uses spare cash or management skillFew economies of scale; managers may not understand the new industry
  • The motive should match the type: a firm worried about supplies looks backward; a firm worried about one market falling looks to conglomerate growth.
Mission 2

Real cases

  • Horizontal, blocked: in April 2019 the UK's competition authority (the CMA) blocked a merger of Sainsbury's and Asda, two of the country's largest supermarkets. It expected higher prices, less choice or lower quality for shoppers.
  • Vertical: in March 2018 Tesco, a supermarket chain, completed its takeover of Booker, a food wholesaler that supplies shops and restaurants.
  • Demerger (break-up): in July 2022 the drugs company GSK split off its consumer health products (toothpaste, painkillers) as a separate company, Haleon.
  • The same patterns appear in every economy.
Mission 2

What holds growth back

  • Problems of growth (costs once the firm is big):
  • Diseconomies of scale: average cost rises as the firm becomes too big to manage well.
  • Communication: more layers of management slow decisions and distort messages.
  • Skills: the firm may lack managers or workers with the skills a bigger operation needs.
  • Corporate culture: after a merger, two ways of working may clash, lowering motivation and productivity.
Quick check
?Two engineering firms merge. Within two years, average cost per unit has risen. Staff complain that decisions now pass through three extra layers of managers, and that the two halves of the firm still work in very different ways. Which explanation fits best?
Mission 2Evaluate

Does growth pay?

  • Whether growth benefits a firm and its customers depends on:
  • Type: horizontal and vertical growth can give real cost savings; conglomerate growth gives few economies of scale.
  • Route: organic growth is slower but less risky; a takeover is fast but the buyer may overpay.
  • Culture and management: savings promised before a merger may be lost to clashing ways of working.
  • Size reached: past a point, diseconomies of scale outweigh economies.
  • Who you ask: shareholders may gain from market power while consumers pay higher prices; competition authorities weigh both.
  • Workers may gain promotion and job security, or lose jobs where the merged firm cuts duplicate roles.
  • So growth is not right for every firm. Most firms stay small: their market is small, finance for expansion is hard to get, or the owner prefers to keep control.
Mission check

?A chocolate maker's profits swing wildly because the price and supply of cocoa beans are unreliable. Its managers want growth that most directly reduces this problem. Which route fits best?

Match the direction of growth to the problem: supply worries point backward, selling worries point forward.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why do some new products launch at a high price while others start out cheap?

Make a guess: Why do some new products launch at a high price while others start out cheap?

Mission 3

Cost-plus pricing

  • Cost-plus: price = unit cost + a mark-up (a percentage of unit cost).
  • Illustrative data: a candle costs £6 to make (average cost). Mark-up 50%.
  • Mark-up = £6 × 50% = £3. Price = £6 + £3 = £9. (Or £6 × 1.5.)
  • Mark-up is profit as a % of cost: 3 ÷ 6 = 50%. Margin is profit as a % of price: 3 ÷ 9 = 33.3%.
  • Pros: simple; every sale covers its average cost if the forecast output is reached.
  • Cons: ignores demand, PED and rivals' prices. Unit cost depends on output, which depends on price.
  • Say: "a 50% mark-up on cost" — not: "a 50% profit margin".
Quick check
?Illustrative data. A bike shop's unit cost for a helmet is £24. It uses cost-plus pricing with a 25% mark-up. What price does it charge?
Mission 3

Skimming and penetration

  • Price skimming: a high launch price, lowered over time.
  • Fits when the product is new or has strong USPs, demand from keen first buyers is price inelastic, and rivals cannot copy it quickly (patents, technology).
  • Gains: recovers R&D costs fast; a premium image. Risk: high profits attract rivals.
  • Penetration pricing: a low launch price to win market share fast, raised later.
  • Fits when demand is price elastic, rivals already sell similar products, and high sales bring economies of scale.
  • Gains: builds a customer base and habit. Risk: low early profit; buyers may resist later rises.
Mission 3

Competitive, predatory and psychological

  • Competitive pricing: setting price at or near rivals' prices. Common when products are similar and many firms compete.
  • Predatory pricing: setting price below cost to drive a rival out, then raising it.
  • In the UK and EU, predatory pricing by a dominant firm can break competition law.
  • Psychological pricing: prices that feel lower: £9.99 not £10; £399 not £400.
  • Say: "predatory pricing: below cost, to remove a rival" — not: any low price.
Quick check
?A games company launches a new console at a high price. Eighteen months later, once the keenest buyers have bought and rival consoles have appeared, it cuts the price. Which pricing strategy is this?

  • The factors often pull in different directions. Firms weigh them.
Mission 3Graph lab

Will a price cut raise revenue?

Illustrative data: a product sells 1,000 units at £20. The firm cuts the price by 10% to £18. At PED = −1 the readout shows £19,800, not exactly £20,000: the simple percentage method is only approximate for a 10% change.

  • Takeaway: a price cut (penetration, competitive pricing) raises revenue only when demand is price elastic.
Mission 3

Online sales and price comparison

  • Price comparison sites let buyers see every seller's price in seconds.
  • For similar products, demand for one seller becomes more price elastic: a small price gap loses many sales.
  • So firms move towards competitive pricing and watch rivals closely.
  • Online-only sellers have no shops to run, so lower costs can allow lower prices.
  • Online prices can be changed many times a day, as demand and rivals' prices change.
Mission 3

Non-price competition and the demand curve

  • Non-price competition: winning customers by anything except price.
  • Product differentiation: design, quality, features, a USP.
  • Advertising and promotion: adverts, sponsorship, social media, loyalty cards, free samples.
  • Distribution: where and how it is sold (online, supermarkets, own shops, delivery).
  • Successful marketing does two things to demand:
  • (1) Shifts D to the right: more bought at every price.
  • (2) Makes D less price elastic: loyal buyers are less likely to switch when price rises.
  • Say: "advertising increased demand (a shift)" — not: "an extension of demand", which is a movement after a price fall.
Quick check
?A coffee chain runs a successful advertising campaign and launches a loyalty card, but keeps its prices unchanged. Which change on its demand diagram (vertical axis Price, horizontal axis Quantity) shows the most likely effect?
Mission 3

Devising a marketing approach

  • Start with the target market: who buys, what they value, how price-sensitive they are.
  • Then make every choice point the same way:
Luxury watchBudget supermarket own-brand
ProductCraft, brand, USPBasic, reliable
PriceHigh; skimming or premiumLow; competitive or penetration
PromotionGlossy adverts, sponsorshipPrice-led adverts, offers
DistributionFew exclusive shops, own websiteEvery store, online
  • A mismatch confuses buyers: a premium brand on a discount shelf, or a cheap price on a "luxury" product.
Mission 3

The product life cycle and pricing

  • Sales over time: introduction → growth → maturity → decline.
  • Introduction: skimming or penetration. Promotion builds awareness.
  • Growth: rivals arrive; skimmed prices fall.
  • Maturity: sales level off; competitive pricing and heavy promotion.
  • Decline: price cuts, or withdraw the product.
  • Extension strategies delay decline: new features, new markets or users, new packaging or rebranding, new uses.
Mission 3

Evaluate: which pricing strategy is best?

It depends on…

  • PED. Cuts raise revenue only if demand is elastic; rises raise revenue only if it is inelastic.
  • Rivals' reactions. A price cut can start a price war that leaves every firm with lower profit.
  • The stage in the life cycle and the brand. A skim works once, for something new. A strong brand can hold a premium for years.
  • Costs and the law. A price below average cost cannot last, and below-cost pricing aimed at a rival can be illegal.
  • Judgement: price, product, promotion and distribution have to fit together.
Mission check

?A small firm is launching a reusable water bottle. Several well-known brands already sell very similar bottles in the same shops and online, and buyers often check price comparison sites. Which launch pricing strategy fits best?

Launch price follows from how easily buyers can switch. Close rivals and price comparison make demand elastic, so a low price wins share.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Economies of scale: long-run average cost falls as output grows; diseconomies: it rises.
  • Internal economies (technical, managerial, financial, purchasing, marketing, risk-bearing) come from the firm's own growth.

Mission 2

  • Firms grow for economies of scale, market power, lower risk and survival; organically (by themselves) or externally (merger or takeover).
  • Horizontal joins the same stage, vertical joins different stages (backward to suppliers, forward to customers), conglomerate joins unrelated industries.
  • Growth can bring diseconomies of scale: poor communication, skills gaps and culture clashes.
  • Most firms stay small because of small markets, limited finance or the owner's own goals.

Mission 3

  • Cost-plus: price = unit cost × (1 + mark-up). Skimming starts high; penetration starts low; competitive matches rivals; predatory goes below cost to remove a rival; psychological looks cheaper.
  • The right price depends on PED, USPs, competition, brand, life-cycle stage and costs.
  • Marketing (differentiation, promotion, distribution) aims to shift demand right and make it less price elastic.
