Comparative Study: The Gothic

Component 02 is the Comparative and Contextual Study. You study a prose topic area — here, the Gothic — and face two tasks: an unseen prose passage to analyse, and a comparative essay on two Gothic texts. This lesson teaches the genre’s conventions and criticism, so it works for any pairing.

  • Two tasks: close analysis of an unseen Gothic passage, and a comparison of two studied Gothic texts.
  • Genre is the lens: the sublime, the uncanny, transgression, the villain and the double.
  • AO weighting: the unseen rewards AO2 close reading; the essay rewards AO3 context, AO4 connection and AO5 interpretation.

Named works are illustrative only; no quotations are invented. Press Start.

Convention · setting and atmosphere

The Gothic world

The Gothic builds meaning through place:

  • The ruined castle or decaying house — architecture as the past, or a mind, made physical; secret rooms, crypts and thresholds.
  • The sublime landscape — mountains, storms and vast obscurity that dwarf the human.
  • Darkness and the liminal — night, ruins, borders between life and death, waking and dreaming.

Edmund Burke (A Philosophical Enquiry, 1757) theorised the sublime: terror and awe before the vast, obscure and powerful. Ann Radcliffe distinguished expansive terror (which awakens the imagination) from crude physical horror (which shuts it down).

Convention · transgression and the villain

Crossing the line

The Gothic is a literature of transgression:

  • The Gothic villain — the tyrant, the seducer, the overreacher who violates limits (moral, sexual, natural, divine).
  • The Byronic hero-villain — brooding, charismatic, guilty and alluring at once.
  • Forbidden desire — incest, obsession, the crossing of taboo, often coded rather than stated.
  • Excess — emotional and stylistic extremity that Fred Botting takes as the genre’s signature (Gothic).

Comparative angle: ask what each text treats as the ultimate taboo, and whether it punishes or secretly indulges the transgressor.

Convention · the psyche and the double

The uncanny and the split self

The Gothic externalises the mind:

  • The double / doppelganger — a second self carrying the repressed or forbidden.
  • The uncanny — Sigmund Freud’s Das Unheimliche (1919): the dread when the familiar returns as strange; the home that is un-home.
  • The return of the repressed — buried guilt, crime or desire resurfacing as haunting or monstrosity.
  • The abject — Julia Kristeva’s term for what disturbs identity and order, the borderline between self and not-self (corpse, decay, the improper).

Female Gothic: Ellen Moers coined the term for a tradition in which the heroine’s terror dramatises real fears of confinement, marriage and the body.

Task 1 · the unseen Gothic passage

Reading the extract

The unseen passage asks you to analyse a Gothic extract you have not studied:

  • Identify the Gothic markers — setting, atmosphere, transgression, the uncanny — and how the prose creates them.
  • Read the narrative voice — first person, omniscient, reliable or not.
  • Analyse prose method — syntax, pace, diction, imagery — and convert each to effect.
  • Relate the passage to the genre — how it typifies or complicates the Gothic.

Avoid feature-spotting: every device must become an effect and a meaning; naming “pathetic fallacy” earns nothing on its own.

AO4 · comparing two Gothic texts

The comparative essay

The essay compares two studied Gothic texts, so AO4 governs structure:

  1. Lead with a genre proposition — e.g. both texts locate horror not in the monster but in the family.
  2. Interleave in convention-paragraphs (the sublime; transgression; the double) across both texts.
  3. Prize the productive difference — where one text punishes transgression and the other mourns it.
  4. Let period matter — an early Gothic and a modern one fear different things.

Stem: “Where the earlier text expels its monster to restore order, the later text suggests the monster was the order all along.”

AO3 · context and AO5 · criticism

Why the Gothic, and read by whom

The Gothic does cultural work, and criticism debates it:

  • Context — the genre rises with Enlightenment reason (as its shadow), revolution, secularisation and anxieties about class, empire and the body.
  • Criticism — David Punter (The Literature of Terror) reads the Gothic as the return of what a rational culture represses; Eve Sedgwick analysed its recurring conventions; Kristeva’s abjection and Moers’s Female Gothic open psychoanalytic and feminist readings.

Discipline: not “the Gothic is about repression”, but “the text buries its crime in a locked room and then makes the house itself confess — repression given architecture.”
