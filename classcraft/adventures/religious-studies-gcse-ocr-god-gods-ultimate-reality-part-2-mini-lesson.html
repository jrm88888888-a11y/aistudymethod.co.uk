Mission 1

Mission 1: Why do we feel some things are wrong even when nobody would ever find out?

Make a guess: Why do we feel some things are wrong even when nobody would ever find out?

Mission 1

The moral argument

  • Most people believe some things are really wrong, not just disliked: torturing a child for fun, for example.
  • If right and wrong are real and binding on everyone (objective morality), where does that law come from?
  • The moral argument: a moral law needs a lawgiver, a source of goodness beyond human opinion. Believers say this is God.
  • A second strand: we feel answerable to our conscience, as if to a person.
  • Both strands treat human morality as a pointer to God, not a proof by itself.
Mission 1

Conscience

  • Conscience: the inner sense of right and wrong that urges us to do good and judges what we have done.
  • John Henry Newman (1801–1890), Catholic cardinal, A Grammar of Assent (1870): when we feel shame or fear at disobeying conscience, this implies Someone we are answerable to (paraphrase).
  • Paul: people without the Jewish Law still "show the work of the law written in their hearts, their conscience testifying with them" (Romans 2:15, WEBBE).
  • Muslims: every person is born with fitrah, a natural sense of God and of good. Allah inspired the soul with "(conscience of) what is wrong for it and (what is) right for it" (Surah 91:8, Pickthall).
  • Jews: every person has a yetzer tov (inclination to good) as well as a yetzer ra (inclination to evil); conscience is the struggle between them.
Mission 1Read the source

Kant: the moral law within

Immanuel Kant (1724–1804), Critique of Practical Reason (1788), Conclusion (Abbott translation, 1889):

> "Two things fill the mind with ever new and increasing admiration and awe, the oftener and the more steadily we reflect on them: the starry heavens above and the moral law within."

  • Kant's moral argument (paraphrase): morality commands us to aim at the highest good, where goodness and happiness go together. In this life they often do not. We must therefore postulate (assume) God, who can bring them together.
  • Kant did not think God could be proved; he thought morality makes belief in God reasonable.

Q1 Which two things fill Kant with awe?

Show answer

The starry sky and the moral law inside him.

Q2 Why does Kant think a moral person must assume God exists?

Show answer

Only God could make goodness and happiness meet, as morality demands.

Mission 1

Objections

  • Conscience from upbringing. Sigmund Freud (1856–1939), The Ego and the Id (1923): conscience is the superego, the rules of parents and society taken inside us.
  • Morality from evolution. Charles Darwin, The Descent of Man (1871): the moral sense grew out of social instincts that helped groups survive.
  • Morals differ. Cultures and ages disagree about right and wrong, so morality may be relative, not one law from God.
  • The Euthyphro dilemma (Plato, Euthyphro, 4th century BCE): is something good because God commands it (then goodness seems arbitrary), or does God command it because it is good (then goodness does not depend on God)?
  • Humanists: people can be good without God. Morality comes from empathy, reason and care for human wellbeing.
Mission 1

Religious replies

  • Goodness is God's nature. Aquinas and many Jewish thinkers: God commands what is good because God is good. Goodness is neither arbitrary nor outside God.
  • Muslim theologians differ: the Ash'ari school says good is what Allah commands; the Mu'tazila said reason can know good and evil.
  • Shared core. Cultures differ in detail, but almost all value life, honesty, fairness and care for the weak: what Natural Law predicts.
  • Explaining how is not explaining away. Evolution or upbringing may explain how conscience develops; believers say this does not show it is not also God's voice.
  • Conscience can defy society. People have followed conscience against their upbringing and their government, as some Christians did under the Nazis. That is hard to explain if conscience is only absorbed rules.
  • Believers accept that non-religious people can be very good: the argument asks where goodness comes from, not who has it.
Quick check
?"If something is good only because God commands it, God could have commanded cruelty. If God commands it because it is good, goodness exists without God." What is this challenge called?
Mission 1

Awe and wonder

  • Standing under a night sky, at the sea, or holding a newborn baby, many people feel awe (amazement mixed with reverence) and wonder.
  • Believers see these moments as pointing to the Creator: "The heavens declare the glory of God" (Psalm 19:1, WEBBE).
  • The psalmist, looking at the moon and stars, asks: "what is man, that you think of him?" (Psalm 8:4, WEBBE).
  • Muslims: in creation "are tokens (of His Sovereignty) for men of understanding" (Surah 3:190, Pickthall).
  • Objections: awe is an emotion, not evidence. Humanists and scientists also feel deep awe at nature, without believing in God. A feeling of greatness does not show a person behind it.
  • Reply: believers say awe fits a world made by God and leads many to worship; it supports faith rather than proving it.

Mission 1Evaluate

"Conscience is the voice of God."

  • Reasons for: we feel answerable to conscience as to a person (Newman); a law "written in their hearts" (Romans 2:15); shared moral basics across cultures (Natural Law); conscience can stand against society.
  • Reasons against: conscience is the superego (Freud); evolution explains the moral sense (Darwin); consciences disagree; Humanists are good without God.
  • Another view: conscience can be God-given and still need forming by teaching and reason, so it can err (Catholic teaching).
  • Judge it by: Does explaining how conscience develops settle where it comes from? Why do consciences disagree? Does objective morality need a source beyond humans?

The same experience of conscience can be read as God's voice, as absorbed rules, or as evolved instinct. The moral argument asks which reading explains it best.

Mission 2

What revelation means

  • Revelation: God (or ultimate reality) making known something humans could not find out on their own.
  • Believers say God is transcendent (beyond the world), so humans need God to take the first step.
  • Two kinds:
  • Special revelation: God revealed to particular people at particular times.
  • General revelation: God revealed to everyone, everywhere, through ordinary things.
Mission 2

Special revelation

  • Direct, to particular people or groups, often life-changing.
  • Prophets receiving God's word (Moses at Sinai; the Prophet Muhammad in the cave of Hira).
  • Visions and dreams: seeing or hearing something sacred.
  • Miracles: events showing God acting in the world (see Mission 3).
  • In a person: Christians believe God is revealed most fully in Jesus.
Mission 2

General revelation

  • Open to anyone, at any time: no special event is needed.
  • Nature: the beauty, order and size of the universe point to its Creator.
  • Conscience and reason: an inner sense of right and wrong; the ability to think about God (see Mission 1).
  • History and inspirational people: seeing God at work in events and in holy lives.
  • Most Christians say nature shows that God exists and is powerful, but God's love and purposes are known through special revelation.
  • Your spec's ways include meeting inspirational people, figures from history, God acting in the world, and conscience.

God has spoken by his Son

"God, having in the past spoken to the fathers through the prophets at many times and in various ways, has at the end of these days spoken to us by his Son" (Hebrews 1:1–2, WEBBE)

  • The Son is "the radiance of his glory, the very image of his substance" (Hebrews 1:3, WEBBE), greater than the angels (Hebrews 1:4).
  • "The Word became flesh and lived amongst us. We saw his glory" (John 1:14, WEBBE).

Q1 How did God speak before Jesus, according to Hebrews?

Show answer

Through the prophets, at many times and in various ways.

Q2 What does Hebrews 1:3 say Jesus shows of God?

Show answer

God's glory and very being: "the very image of his substance".

Mission 2

Revelation in the Bible

  • Covenant: a binding agreement in which God reveals himself and makes promises.
  • Noah: never again a flood; the rainbow is "a sign of a covenant between me and the earth" (Genesis 9:13, WEBBE).
  • Abraham: God calls him to a new land and promises descendants (Genesis 12:1–3; 15).
  • Jesus: the "new covenant" (Luke 22:20).
  • What it shows: God is personal, faithful, keeps promises, acts in history.
Mission 2

The Qur'an as revelation

  • Muslims believe the Qur'an is Allah's own word, revealed to the Prophet Muhammad through the angel Jibril over about 23 years, from 610 CE.
  • The first words: "Read: In the name of thy Lord Who createth" (Surah 96:1, Pickthall).
  • Allah speaks to humans only "by revelation or from behind a veil, or (that) He sendeth a messenger" (Surah 42:51, Pickthall).
  • The same revelation came to earlier prophets: Noah, Abraham, Moses, Jesus (Surah 4:163–164).
Mission 2

The Tenakh and Gurbani

  • Jews: God revealed the Torah to Moses at Sinai; the Tenakh (Torah, Prophets, Writings) records God's revelation.
Mission 2

Hindu and Buddhist views

  • Hindus: shruti ("what is heard"): the Vedas and Upanishads, revealed to ancient sages; smriti ("what is remembered"): epics such as the Ramayana and the Bhagavad Gita.
  • The divine is revealed through avatars of Vishnu, such as Rama and Krishna; some Hindus also count Jesus and the Buddha as avatars.
  • Buddhists do not believe in a God who reveals. The Dhamma was discovered by the Buddha and is revealed through scripture, meeting inspirational people, Bodhisattvas and arhats, and meditation.
Mission 2Diversity

Nature as general revelation: contrasting beliefs

  • Many Christians: "The heavens declare the glory of God" (Psalm 19:1, WEBBE). Nature shows God's power (Romans 1:20). Catholics teach God can be known through creation by reason (CCC 36).
  • Some Protestants: Karl Barth (1886–1968) taught that nature on its own gives no true knowledge of God; only God's revelation in Christ does.
  • Atheists and Humanists: nature shows natural processes, explained by science (evolution, physics).
  • Nature also contains cruelty and disaster; to many non-religious people it does not look like the work of a loving God.
  • Humanists still feel awe at nature, without seeing it as a message from God.
Mission 2

Value and problems

  • Value: revelation can give knowledge of God that reason cannot; it guides life; it can lead people to faith and change their lives.
  • Conflicting revelations: religions report different ideas of the divine (personal or impersonal; one God or many forms). They cannot all be literally true.
  • Alternative explanations: illness, drugs, tiredness, wishful thinking or upbringing.
  • Lying or mistaken: a person might invent a revelation, or honestly misunderstand an experience.
  • Responses: believers test claims against scripture, tradition and the person's life ("by their fruits"). Some, like John Hick (1922–2012), suggest different revelations show different sides of one ultimate reality.
Mission 2Evaluate

"Revelation is a reliable way of knowing about God."

  • Reasons for: if God exists and is personal, God could make himself known; many lives have been transformed; scripture and tradition test claims; general revelation is open to everyone.
  • Reasons against: revelations conflict; they cannot be tested by others; natural explanations are possible; people can lie or be mistaken.
  • Another view: revelation may be reliable for the person who receives it, but not proof for anyone else.
  • Judge it by: Who can check a revelation? Do differences between religions undermine all revelation, or only some? Do changed lives count as evidence?

Mission 3

What is a miracle?

  • A miracle: an extraordinary event that seems to break the laws of nature, believed to be caused by God.
  • David Hume (1711–1776): "A miracle is a violation of the laws of nature" (An Enquiry concerning Human Understanding, 1748, Section X).
  • Believers add that a miracle has meaning: it shows something about God or answers prayer.
  • Some believers also call a striking, well-timed coincidence a miracle, if they see God at work in it.
  • Miracles are a form of special revelation (see Mission 2): God acting in the world.
Mission 3Read the source

The official's son

John 4:43–54: at Cana, a royal official begs Jesus to come and heal his dying son in Capernaum.

  • "Jesus said to him, 'Go your way. Your son lives.' The man believed the word that Jesus spoke to him, and he went his way." (John 4:50, WEBBE)
  • On the way his servants meet him: the fever left the boy at the very hour Jesus spoke.
  • "He believed, as did his whole house." (John 4:53, WEBBE)
  • John calls this "the second sign" (John 4:54): miracles are signs pointing to who Jesus is.

Mission 3

Lourdes

  • In 1858, Bernadette Soubirous (1844–1879), aged 14, reported 18 appearances of the Virgin Mary at a grotto in Lourdes, France. A spring appeared there.
  • Millions of pilgrims now visit each year; some report healings.
  • The Lourdes Medical Bureau (from 1883) examines claimed cures. A cure must be of a serious illness, sudden, complete and lasting, with no medical explanation.
  • Only then can the local bishop declare it a miracle. Very few cures pass.
  • One example: Sister Bernadette Moriau, a French nun disabled for decades, recovered suddenly in 2008 after a pilgrimage; her bishop declared it a miracle in 2018.
Mission 3

The Qur'an: the greatest miracle

  • Muslims believe the Qur'an itself is the greatest miracle: the Prophet could not read or write, yet brought words no one could match.
  • "Say: Verily, though mankind and the jinn should assemble to produce the like of this Qur'an, they could not produce the like thereof" (Surah 17:88, Pickthall).
  • Other miracles: the Night Journey (Surah 17:1); the splitting of the moon (Surah 54:1); miracles of earlier prophets, such as Isa healing the sick by Allah's leave.

Mission 3

Miracles in Judaism

  • Hebrew nes: a miracle, also meaning a sign or banner: something lifted up to show God's presence.
  • In the desert God sends manna: "It is the bread which the LORD hath given you to eat." (Exodus 16:15, JPS 1917)
  • The manna was also a test: whether Israel "will walk in My law, or not" (Exodus 16:4, JPS 1917).
  • Hanukkah celebrates the miracle of the oil: "a great miracle happened there".
  • Daily prayer thanks God for miracles "which are with us every day": hidden miracles in ordinary life.
  • Divergent views: many Orthodox Jews accept the biblical miracles literally; the Mishnah says some, like the manna, were built into creation from the start (Pirkei Avot 5:6). Many Reform Jews read them as stories of faith.
Mission 3

Scientific and Humanist objections

  • Hume (Enquiry, 1748, Section X): our experience that the laws of nature hold is very strong; a report of a miracle is weaker. "A wise man, therefore, proportions his belief to the evidence."
  • Hume adds: few reliable witnesses; people love wonder stories; and miracles from rival religions cancel each other out.
  • Science: cures can come from spontaneous remission, the placebo effect, wrong diagnosis or coincidence.
  • Unexplained is not the same as explained by God: science may explain it later.
  • Unfairness: why would God heal one person and not millions of others? (Maurice Wiles, 1923–2005, God's Action in the World, 1986.)
  • Humanists: wonder at nature, but trust evidence; no miracle has passed a controlled test.
Mission 3

Religious responses

  • Careful testing: Lourdes doctors (believers and non-believers) reject most claims. The Church does not call every recovery a miracle.
  • Laws describe, God acts: laws of nature describe what normally happens; the God who made them can act in new ways.
  • Signs, not shows: miracles are rare by purpose, pointing to God, not solving every problem.
  • Jews: hidden miracles in daily life matter more than spectacular ones.

Mission 4

What is a religious experience?

  • A religious experience: an experience in which a person believes they have encountered God or ultimate reality.
  • It may be sudden or gradual, alone or with others, ordinary or overwhelming.
  • William James (1842–1910), The Varieties of Religious Experience (1902), said mystical states are hard to put into words (ineffable), feel like knowledge (noetic), do not last long (transient), and happen to the person (passive).
  • For many believers it is the most direct evidence of God they have.
Mission 4

Kinds of religious experience

  • Conversion: a change of heart, belief or religion.
  • Mystical: a sense of union with God or ultimate reality, beyond words.
  • Numinous: awe and fear before a holy power. Rudolf Otto (1869–1937), The Idea of the Holy (1917): the holy is a mystery that makes us tremble yet draws us in.
  • Charismatic: gifts of the Holy Spirit, such as glossolalia (speaking in tongues: Acts 2:4), prophecy and healing.
  • Worship and sacraments: Catholics believe Christ is truly present in the Mass; many feel God's presence in prayer and liturgy.
  • Visions and answered prayer are also forms.

Mission 4

Conversion

  • Saul, a persecutor of Christians, travels to Damascus: "suddenly a light from the sky shone around him." (Acts 9:3, WEBBE)
  • He hears: "Saul, Saul, why do you persecute me?" … "I am Jesus, whom you are persecuting." (Acts 9:4–5, WEBBE)
  • Blinded for three days, he is baptised and becomes Paul, the great missionary.
  • Some conversions are sudden like this; many are gradual.
  • Muslims: every baby is born with fitrah (natural sense of Allah), so many converts call themselves reverts: returning to their original nature.
  • Buddhists: becoming a Buddhist is marked by taking the Three Refuges: in the Buddha, the Dhamma and the Sangha.

Mission 4

Christian attitudes: welcome and caution

  • So the Church does not approve every experience: it tests whether one agrees with scripture and teaching, and looks at the person and the fruits.
  • Many Protestants test experiences by scripture: "test the spirits, whether they are of God" (1 John 4:1, WEBBE).
  • Pentecostal and charismatic Christians (including Catholic charismatics) expect experiences of the Spirit, such as glossolalia, today.
  • Quakers wait in silence for the inner light. Experience as revelation: God still makes himself known personally (Exodus 3 as the model).
Mission 4

Muslim attitudes

  • "Those who have no knowledge say: Why doth not Allah speak unto us, or some sign come unto us? … We have made clear the revelations for people who are sure." (Surah 2:118, Pickthall)
  • Muslims take from this: Allah's signs are already clear in the Qur'an and creation. Demanding a private experience is not needed for faith.
  • Yet believers find closeness to Allah in prayer: "Verily in the remembrance of Allah do hearts find rest!" (Surah 13:28, Pickthall)
  • Sunni: value prayer, Qur'an recitation and true dreams, but are cautious about claimed experiences.
  • Shi'a: deep experience in mourning for Imam Husayn (Muharram) and visiting shrines of the Imams.
  • Sufi: seek direct experience of Allah through dhikr (remembrance) and fana (passing away of the self); some Sunni scholars warn against claims that go beyond the Qur'an.
Mission 4Diversity

Jewish, Hindu, Buddhist and Sikh attitudes

  • Jews: Exodus 3 and Sinai are the great experiences of God. Many Jews meet God mainly through community, prayer and keeping the mitzvot; Kabbalah and Hasidism value mystical experience.
  • Hindus: Bhagavad Gita 12 praises bhakti: loving devotion to the personal Lord, who quickly lifts devotees out of rebirth (paraphrase). Gita 13.23–27: some see the Self within through meditation, some through knowledge, some through selfless action (paraphrase). Murti puja and darshan (seeing the deity) bring experience of the divine.
  • Buddhists: in the Kevatta Sutta, a monk asks the gods where the elements cease; even Great Brahma cannot answer and sends him to the Buddha (paraphrase). Experiences of gods are not final knowledge. The jhanas (meditative absorptions) and Zen kensho/satori (flashes of insight) matter only as steps to awakening (see Mission 6).
  • Sikhs: vismad, wonder before God's creation, is a numinous experience. Guru Granth Sahib 448 describes love for God piercing mind and body (paraphrase); Guru Granth Sahib 1402 praises God as Waheguru ("Wonderful Lord") (paraphrase). Enlightenment (mukti) comes by God's grace through the Guru's word.
Mission 4

Challenges from atheists and Humanists

  • Hallucination: illness, drugs, tiredness or fasting can produce powerful experiences.
  • Wish-fulfilment: Sigmund Freud (1856–1939), The Future of an Illusion (1927): belief in God answers our longing for a protective father.
  • Lack of evidence: private experiences cannot be checked by others.
  • Culture shapes experience: Christians meet Jesus, Hindus meet Krishna, Buddhists find no God. If the source were one God, why so different?
  • Lying or mistaken: some claims are invented; honest people can misread a feeling.
  • Humanists: the experiences are real, but have natural, psychological causes.
Mission 4

Religious responses

  • Fruits: "By their fruits you will know them." (Matthew 7:16, WEBBE) A lasting change for good (Paul) is hard to explain as illusion.
  • Shared experiences: at Pentecost a whole group was filled with the Spirit (Acts 2).
  • Testing: traditions check claims against scripture and teaching; most are not approved.
  • God may use natural means: brain activity during an experience does not show God is absent.
  • Different traditions, one reality? Some believers see differences as different responses to the same divine reality; others think their own tradition's account is the true one.

Mission 5

Personal God or ultimate reality?

  • Personal God: a being who creates, knows, loves, judges and answers prayer (Judaism, Christianity, Islam, Sikhism, many Hindus).
  • Ultimate reality: whatever is most real and final, the ground of everything. It need not be a person.
  • Non-theistic: a religion without a creator God at its centre, e.g. Buddhism.
  • Impersonal: ultimate reality is beyond personal qualities, e.g. nirguna Brahman in Hinduism.
  • Non-theistic does not mean non-spiritual: these traditions still have worship, ethics, and a final goal.
Mission 5

The Buddha and the creator question

  • The Buddha did not teach a creator God. He taught a path to end suffering (dukkha).
  • Asking who made the world does not help that goal.
  • The Poisoned Arrow (Cula-Malunkyovada Sutta): a man shot by a poisoned arrow refuses treatment until he knows who shot it, the bow, the feathers… He would die first. Pull out the arrow.
  • Some questions the Buddha left unanswered: is the world eternal? Is it infinite? What happens to an enlightened one after death? His Noble Silence: these questions do not lead to awakening.
  • The first teaching (Dhammacakkappavattana Sutta): the Four Noble Truths about suffering, its cause, its end and the path: nothing about a creator.
Mission 5Read the source

The Devadaha Sutta and Dhammapada 188

  • "Men, driven by fear, go to many a refuge, to mountains and forests, to groves and sacred trees." (Dhammapada 188, Müller 1881). The next verse: that is "not a safe refuge"; the safe refuge is the Buddha, the Dhamma and the Sangha (189–190).

Q2 What is the safe refuge instead?

Show answer

Mission 5

Gods, but not God: the devas

  • Devas: gods living in heavenly realms. Long-lived, powerful, happy.
  • But they are not creators, not all-powerful, and not eternal: they are still in samsara and will die and be reborn.
  • They cannot give liberation; they too need the Dhamma. The Buddha is called "teacher of gods and humans".
  • Personal God (Abrahamic) vs devas: eternal creator vs mortal beings within the cycle.
  • Rebirth among the gods is one of the six realms (gods, demi-gods, humans, animals, hungry ghosts, hell beings).

Mission 5

What is ultimate for Buddhists?

  • Dhamma: the truth about how things are, taught by the Buddha.
  • Dependent origination: everything arises from causes and conditions; nothing exists by itself.
  • Nibbana: the unconditioned: the end of greed, hatred and ignorance; not a place and not a god.
  • Mahayana: sunyata (emptiness) and Buddha-nature in all beings.
  • Pure Land: Amida (Amitabha) Buddha's vow creates a Pure Land; devotees hope for rebirth there. Many Pure Land Buddhists treat Amida's light and life as limitless; Amida is still not a creator God.
Mission 5

Brahman: with and without qualities

  • Brahman: Hindu ultimate reality: the one source, sustainer and goal of all.
  • Nirguna Brahman: "without qualities": beyond every description; impersonal. Upanishads describe it only as "not this, not this" (neti neti).
  • Saguna Brahman: "with qualities": Brahman known in personal forms, such as Vishnu, Shiva or Devi, who can be loved and worshipped.
  • Atman: the true self in each being. Many Hindus teach that atman and Brahman are one: "Tat twam asi" (That thou art), Chandogya Upanishad 6.8.7.
  • Trimurti: Brahma (creator), Vishnu (preserver), Shiva (destroyer). Tridevi: Saraswati, Lakshmi, Parvati.
  • So many Hindus hold both: one impersonal reality, known through personal deities.
Mission 5

Shankara: beyond a personal God

  • Adi Shankara (traditionally 788–820 CE), teacher of Advaita Vedanta ("non-dual").
  • Only Brahman is ultimately real. The world of many separate things, and a personal Lord ruling it, belong to maya: how reality appears to the unenlightened.
  • Implications: knowledge (jnana yoga) and meditation; worship of deities is valued as a step, since they are saguna forms of Brahman.
  • Divergent: Ramanuja and Madhva, and many Vaishnavas today (e.g. ISKCON), teach that ultimate reality is personal: Vishnu or Krishna; loving devotion (bhakti) is the path.
Mission 5

Buddhism: cause, cycle and consequence

  • Designed or caused? The Buddha did not argue for a designer or a first cause. He said the beginning of samsara cannot be found; asking about it does not end suffering.
  • Cyclical world: samsara has no known start; dependent origination explains how suffering arises and ends.
  • Human realm: a balance of pleasure and pain, so the best place to practise. Human rebirth is rare: like a blind turtle surfacing once in a century and putting its head through a ring floating on the ocean.
  • Moral consequence: kamma: intentional actions bring results, without a judge.
  • Value of arguments about cause: they may be interesting, but they do not help on the path.
Mission 5

Hinduism: cause, cycle and consequence

  • Caused: the universe comes from Brahman: as the cosmic egg (Brahmanda) and the primal sound Aum.
  • Purusha Sukta (Rig Veda 10.90): the world arises from the sacrifice of a cosmic being, Purusha.
  • The Rig Veda's creation hymn (10.129) ends with a question: perhaps even the highest one does not know how it began.
  • Cyclical: worlds are created, sustained and dissolved again and again: the Trimurti.
  • Purpose: the world is the field of samsara where souls work out karma towards moksha.
  • Value of arguments: some Hindu philosophers (the Nyaya school) argued from design and causation to God; others, like Shankara, said reasoning cannot reach Brahman; only knowledge from scripture and experience can.

Mission 5

Non-religious views of ultimate reality

  • Naturalism: the physical universe and its laws are all there is; science is the best guide to it.
  • Many atheists and Humanists: no ultimate reality beyond nature; meaning is made by humans.
  • Agnostics: we cannot know whether there is an ultimate reality beyond nature.
  • Some people describe themselves as "spiritual but not religious": they sense a deeper reality without a God or a religion.
  • Buddhists reply that Dhamma is open to testing in experience; Hindus, that Brahman is known through scripture and inner realisation.
Mission 5Evaluate

"Religion does not need a personal God."

  • Reasons for: Buddhism offers ethics, worship and a goal without a creator; some Hindus see personal deities as steps to an impersonal reality; avoids the problem of evil (no all-good creator to blame).
  • Reasons against: most religious people worship a personal God; love and prayer need someone to relate to; many Christians, Muslims and Jews: without God, morality and meaning have no final ground.
  • Also against: Hindu bhaktas following Ramanuja or Madhva turn to a personal, saving God.
  • Also against: Pure Land Buddhists turn to Amida Buddha as a saving figure.
  • Another view: "personal" and "impersonal" may describe two sides of one reality.
  • Judge it by: What makes something a religion? Does practice depend on belief in a creator? Which view better explains religious experience?
Mission check

A religion can centre on liberation, Dhamma or Brahman rather than a personal creator.

Mission 6

Revelation and realisation

  • Revelation: God makes something known to people (scripture, prophets, visions).
  • Enlightenment: a person sees for themselves the truth about reality, through practice and insight.
  • "Buddha" means "awakened one": someone who has woken up to things as they really are.
  • Enlightenment ends ignorance; it is called knowing, not just believing.
  • In Hinduism and Sikhism the goal is liberation (moksha, mukti): freedom from rebirth, often with direct knowledge of the divine.
Mission 6

The Buddha's night of awakening

  • Siddhattha Gotama spent six years in extreme austerities: fasting until he was close to death. They brought no insight.
  • He remembered sitting as a child under a rose-apple tree, calm and joyful in the first jhana (meditative absorption). Could this be the way?
  • He ate food again (the Middle Way), sat under the Bodhi tree and passed through the four jhanas.
  • In the three watches of the night he knew: (1) his own past lives; (2) how beings die and are reborn according to their kamma; (3) the Four Noble Truths and the ending of the taints (craving, ignorance).
  • This account is in the Maha-Saccaka Sutta (Majjhima Nikaya 36).
Mission 6Read the source

Knowledge arose

The Buddha's words, in the Maha-Saccaka Sutta (MN 36, paraphrase): when my mind was concentrated, pure and steady, I directed it to knowing the ending of the taints. I knew as it really is: "This is suffering; this is its origin; this is its ending; this is the way." Ignorance was destroyed; knowledge arose; darkness was destroyed; light arose.

> "Without knowledge there is no meditation, without meditation there is no knowledge: he who has knowledge and meditation is near unto Nirvana." (Dhammapada 372, Müller)

Show answer

Q2 Why do Buddhists call this knowledge rather than faith?

Show answer

It is seen directly, "as it really is", not accepted from someone else.

Mission 6

The jhanas

  • Jhanas: deep states of meditative concentration.
  • Four form jhanas: (1) joy and happiness, with thought still present; (2) joy and happiness, thought falls away; (3) happiness with equanimity; (4) pure equanimity, beyond pleasure and pain.
  • Four formless jhanas: infinite space; infinite consciousness; nothingness; neither perception nor non-perception.
  • In the Ariyapariyesana Sutta (MN 26) the future Buddha learns the two highest formless states from two teachers, but leaves: they do not lead to nibbana (paraphrase).
  • MN 26:34–42 lists the jhanas and formless states, then the cessation of perception and feeling, where the monk, with taints destroyed by wisdom, has "blindfolded Mara" (paraphrase).
  • So the jhanas are states approaching an enlightened mind; insight (wisdom) completes the path.

Mission 6

Theravada and Mahayana

  • Theravada: the goal is to become an arhat ("worthy one"), who has ended greed, hatred and delusion and will not be reborn.
  • The path: ethics, meditation and wisdom: seeing the three marks of existence: anicca (impermanence), dukkha (unsatisfactoriness), anatta (no fixed self).
  • Mahayana: the ideal is the bodhisattva, who seeks enlightenment in order to free all beings.
  • Many Mahayana Buddhists teach Buddha-nature: every being already has the potential to be a Buddha; enlightenment uncovers it.
  • Mahayana stresses sunyata (emptiness): nothing exists independently.
  • Both agree: enlightenment is nibbana (nirvana), the end of craving and suffering, not union with a creator God.
Mission 6

Zen: kensho and satori

  • Zen: a Mahayana school of China and Japan.
  • Kensho: "seeing one's true nature": a flash of insight into Buddha-nature.
  • Satori: awakening; often used for a deeper, more lasting enlightenment.
  • Rinzai Zen uses koans (puzzles, e.g. "What is the sound of one hand clapping?") to break ordinary thinking. Soto Zen stresses zazen ("just sitting").
  • Kensho differs from the jhanas: not a deep stillness, but a sudden seeing into one's true nature.
  • Many Zen teachers say kensho must be deepened by years of practice.

Mission 6

Hindus: liberation and knowing the divine

  • Moksha: liberation from samsara (rebirth). Many Hindus say it brings direct knowledge of the divine.
  • Advaita Vedanta (Shankara, 8th century): the true self (atman) is Brahman. Liberation is realising this through knowledge (jnana) and meditation.
  • Bhakti traditions: liberation through loving devotion to a personal God (Vishnu, Krishna, Shiva); the soul knows and loves God, but is not identical with God.
  • Brahman in the Gita: "He is within all beings--and without--" (Bhagavad Gita 13.15, Arnold).
  • Revelation also comes through the avatars of Vishnu, such as Rama (the Ramayana) and Krishna (the Bhagavad Gita); some Hindus see Jesus and the Buddha as avatars too.
  • Detachment: acting without clinging to results frees the mind for liberation (Gita 2.47, paraphrase).
Mission 6

Non-religious explanations

  • Brain states: brain scans show meditation changes attention and emotion areas. Intense experiences may be brain events, nothing more.
  • Altered states: fasting, lack of sleep and long isolation can produce vivid experiences.
  • Illogical: atheists may say "knowing" by meditation cannot be tested; experiences of different faiths contradict each other.
  • Lying or mistaken: people may exaggerate, or misread a strong feeling as insight.
  • Humanists: value calm and mindfulness for their real benefits, without a supernatural or "ultimate" claim.
Mission 6

Religious responses

  • Buddhists: the Buddha told people to test teachings for themselves, not accept them on faith (Kalama Sutta, paraphrase). So enlightenment is a claim anyone can check by practice.
  • The Buddha himself rejected the extreme fasting that produces altered states (MN 36).
  • Many believers reply that brain activity during insight does not show the insight is false: seeing a tree also involves the brain.
  • Many traditions test claims by fruits: compassion, wisdom, freedom from greed.
Mission 6Evaluate

"Enlightenment gives real knowledge of ultimate reality."

  • Reasons for: direct seeing, not hearsay; practice can be repeated and taught; enlightened people show lasting compassion and calm; the Buddha invited testing.
  • Reasons against: cannot be checked from outside; brain-state explanations; experiences may be mistaken or exaggerated.
  • Problem of different ideas: Buddhists find no creator; Advaita Hindus find the self is Brahman. Can both be knowledge?
  • Another view: traditions may describe one reality in different ways; or each tradition's own account may be the true one.
  • Judge it by: Is inner seeing a kind of knowledge? Do changed lives count as evidence? Do the differences weaken every claim?
Mission check

?Mei, a Zen Buddhist, says that during zazen she suddenly saw her true nature. Arjun, an Advaita Hindu, says that in deep meditation he realised his self is Brahman. Sam, an atheist, says both were having unusual brain states. Which statement is accurate?

Enlightenment is claimed as knowledge seen from the inside. Whether it shows ultimate reality, and which reality, is where traditions and critics disagree.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • The moral argument: objective moral law and the experience of conscience point to God as lawgiver (Newman; Romans 2:15; Kant's postulate).
  • Objections: Freud (superego), Darwin (evolution), relativism, Euthyphro, Humanist ethics.
  • Awe and wonder supports faith for many, but non-believers feel it too.

Mission 2

  • Revelation is God making himself known: special (prophets, visions, miracles, Jesus, scripture received) and general (nature, conscience, reason, scripture read by all).
  • Christians see Jesus as the culmination of revelation (Hebrews 1:1–4); Muslims see the Qur'an as Allah's word revealed through Jibril.
  • Problems: conflicting revelations, alternative explanations, lying or mistake; believers reply with tests of scripture, tradition and fruits.

Mission 3

  • A miracle is an extraordinary event, beyond the laws of nature, believed to be caused by God and to carry meaning: John 4:43–54; Lourdes (Bernadette Moriau).
  • Muslims see the Qur'an as the greatest miracle (Surah 17:88).
  • The argument from miracles faces Hume's challenge, scientific explanations and the "why some?" problem; believers reply with careful testing and the meaning of signs.

Mission 4

  • Religious experience: conversion, mystical, numinous, charismatic, worship and sacraments.
  • Exodus 3 (the burning bush) and Acts 9 (Saul on the Damascus road) are key examples.
  • Challenges: hallucination, wish-fulfilment, lack of evidence, cultural differences; responses: fruits, shared experiences, testing.

Mission 5

  • Buddhism: the Buddha taught no creator God; the Poisoned Arrow: such questions do not help end suffering.
  • Devas are mortal, not creators; the Noble Silence.
  • The Dhamma, dependent origination and nibbana are what is ultimate.
  • Hinduism: Brahman is nirguna (beyond qualities) and saguna (personal forms).
  • Shankara's Advaita vs Ramanuja's and Madhva's personal God.
  • Non-religious views: nature is all there is (naturalism), or we cannot know (agnosticism).

Mission 6

  • Enlightenment is seeing reality for oneself; the Buddha's awakening (Maha-Saccaka Sutta) came through the jhanas and insight into the Four Noble Truths. Theravada stresses the arhat; Mahayana the bodhisattva and Buddha-nature. Critics give brain-state and mistake explanations.
  • Jhanas are stages, not the goal; Zen speaks of kensho and satori.
  • Hindus seek moksha (Advaita: atman is Brahman; bhakti: loving union).
