Mission 1

Mission 1: Why are the markings on a spring weighing scale evenly spaced?

Make a guess: Why are the markings on a spring weighing scale evenly spaced?

Mission 1

Hooke's law

  • Stretch a spring: the force it exerts is proportional to its extension.
  • This holds only up to a limit.
  • Extension = stretched length − original (natural) length. Not the whole length.
  • F = kx. k is the spring constant: force per unit extension.
  • Unit of k: N m⁻¹. Larger k: stiffer spring.
  • Works for compression too: the extension is then the decrease in length.
Quick check
?In a lab, a student models a spring as a light spring that obeys Hooke's law. Which statement describes how the spring's force behaves in this model?
Mission 1

Worked example

  • A spring hangs with natural length 12.0 cm. A 4.0 N weight stretches it to 17.0 cm.
  • Extension = 17.0 − 12.0 = 5.0 cm = 0.050 m.
  • k = force ÷ extension = 4.0 ÷ 0.050 = 80 N m⁻¹.
  • Hang 6.0 N instead: extension = 6.0 ÷ 80 = 0.075 m = 7.5 cm.
  • New length = 12.0 + 7.5 = 19.5 cm. Always add the extension back to the natural length.
Mission 1

The force–extension graph

  • Plot force against extension. Hooke's law: a straight line through the origin.
  • Gradient = k.
  • Beyond the limit of proportionality the line curves.
  • Below the elastic limit: remove the load and it returns to its original length.
  • Beyond the elastic limit: it is permanently stretched.
Quick check
?The graph shows the magnitude F of the force exerted by a light spring that obeys Hooke's law, as a function of the spring's length L, from its natural length to a length of 20 cm. What is the spring constant of the spring?
Mission check

?A spring that obeys Hooke's law is 15.0 cm long when it supports a 3.0 N load and 18.0 cm long when it supports a 6.0 N load. What is its natural (unloaded) length?

Extension ∝ force, so each extra newton adds the same extension: evenly spaced markings.

As long as the spring stays within its limit of proportionality.

Mission 2

Mission 2: A thin steel wire and a thick steel cable carry the same load. Why does the wire stretch more?

Make a guess: A thin steel wire and a thick steel cable carry the same load. Why does the wire stretch more?

Mission 2

Stress and strain

  • Tensile stress = force ÷ cross-sectional area. Unit: Pa (N m⁻²).
  • Tensile strain = extension ÷ original length. No unit (a ratio).
  • Same load on a thinner wire → larger stress.
Mission 2

The Young modulus

  • Young modulus = tensile stress ÷ tensile strain. Unit: Pa.
  • Young modulus = stress/strain, which gives E = Fl/(A × extension).
  • Valid only where Hooke's law holds (up to the limit of proportionality).
  • It is the gradient of the straight part of a stress–strain graph.
  • It belongs to the material. The spring constant (Young modulus × area ÷ length) belongs to the object.
  • Steel: about 2 × 10¹¹ Pa. Rubber: about 10⁶ Pa.
Mission 2

Worked example

  • Steel wire: length 2.50 m, diameter 0.40 mm, load 20 N, E = 2.0 × 10¹¹ Pa. Extension?
  • A = π(d/2)² = π(0.20 × 10⁻³)² = 1.26 × 10⁻⁷ m². (Radius, not diameter.)
  • Stress = F/A = 20 ÷ 1.26 × 10⁻⁷ = 1.6 × 10⁸ Pa.
  • Strain = stress/E = 1.6 × 10⁸ ÷ 2.0 × 10¹¹ = 8.0 × 10⁻⁴.
  • Extension = strain × original length = 8.0 × 10⁻⁴ × 2.50 = 2.0 × 10⁻³ m (2.0 mm).
Quick check
?A metal wire of length 1.80 m and diameter 0.30 mm is loaded, and a graph of force F against extension is plotted. The straight part of the graph has a gradient of 7.9 × 10³ N m⁻¹. What is the Young modulus of the metal?
Mission 2

Measuring the Young modulus

  • A long, thin wire: a large extension from a modest load, so its percentage uncertainty is small.
  • Clamp one end. Run it over a pulley, or hang it beside an identical reference wire (Searle's apparatus).
  • Diameter: micrometer, several places and directions; average. Length: metre rule to the marker.
  • Add masses in steps; read the extension from a marker against a scale (or a vernier). Unload to check it returns: still elastic.
  • Plot force against extension. Young modulus = gradient × original length ÷ area. (Or plot stress against strain: gradient = Young modulus.)
  • The reference wire cancels temperature changes and sag of the support. Wear eye protection: the wire may snap.
Quick check
?A student repeats the Young modulus experiment with the same loads on a wire of the same metal and diameter, but only one tenth as long. The extension is read with the same scale. What is the main effect?
Mission 2

Choosing a material

  • Strength (breaking stress): sets the smallest safe area. A = F ÷ working stress, with a safety factor.
  • Stiffness (Young modulus): sets how much it stretches or bends under the working load.
  • Density: sets how heavy the part is. Cost: price per kg × mass needed.
  • Example: a 5.0 kN load on a steel cable, working stress 200 MPa: A = 5.0 × 10³ ÷ 2.0 × 10⁸ = 2.5 × 10⁻⁵ m² (25 mm²).
  • A stiffer or stronger material may allow a thinner, lighter part, which can cost less overall.
Quick check
?A cable must carry 12 kN. Steel: working stress 200 MPa, density 7850 kg m⁻³, £1.00 per kg. Aluminium alloy: working stress 100 MPa, density 2700 kg m⁻³, £2.50 per kg. Each cable is made with the smallest safe area. Which statement is correct?
Mission check

?Two cables are made of the same aluminium alloy and carry the same load. Cable Q is 3 times as long as cable P and has 3 times its diameter. Both obey Hooke's law. What is the extension of Q divided by the extension of P?

Same steel means the same Young modulus. The thin wire has a smaller area, so the same load gives a larger stress, so a larger strain and a larger extension.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Bend a paperclip a little and it springs back. Bend it further and it stays bent. Why?

Make a guess: Bend a paperclip a little and it springs back. Bend it further and it stays bent. Why?

Mission 3

Force–extension graph of a metal wire

  • Limit of proportionality (P): up to here, extension ∝ force (Hooke's law). Straight line.
  • Elastic limit (EL): the largest force after which the wire still returns to its original length.
  • Beyond the elastic limit: plastic region. Then the wire breaks.
Mission 3

Elastic and plastic

  • Elastic deformation: removed load → returns to its original length.
  • Plastic deformation: removed load → permanent extension.
  • The limit of proportionality and the elastic limit are different points.
  • A metal loaded into the plastic region unloads along a line parallel to the original straight line. It ends up permanently longer.
Quick check
?A wire is loaded until its force–extension graph has just started to curve. The load is then removed, and the wire returns exactly to its original length. What can you conclude about the load?
Mission check

?A copper wire is loaded beyond its elastic limit. At the maximum load of 30 N its extension is 6.0 mm. The straight first part of its force–extension graph has a gradient of 20 N per mm. The load is then removed completely. What is the permanent extension of the wire?

A small bend stays below the elastic limit: elastic, so the clip springs back. A bigger bend passes the elastic limit: the deformation becomes plastic, and a permanent bend remains.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Stretch a spring twice as far. Does it store twice the energy?

Make a guess: Stretch a spring twice as far. Does it store twice the energy?

Mission 4

Work done stretching = area

  • Stretching a spring or wire takes work: force × distance moved.
  • The force is not constant: it rises from 0 to its final value as the extension grows from 0.
  • Work done = area under the force–extension graph.
  • In the elastic region this work is stored as elastic strain energy (also called elastic potential energy). Unload, and you get it back.
Mission 4

Energy stored = ½ × force × extension

  • Hooke's law region: the graph is a straight line, so the area is a triangle.
  • E = ½Fx (F = final force, x = extension).
  • Use F = kx: E = ½(kx)x = ½kx².
  • Double the extension → four times the energy.
Mission 4

Worked example

  • Spring: k = 80 N m⁻¹, stretched by 0.050 m.
  • Force: F = kx = 80 × 0.050 = 4.0 N.
  • Energy: E = ½Fx = ½ × 4.0 × 0.050 = 0.10 J.
  • Check: ½kx² = ½ × 80 × 0.050² = 0.10 J.
  • Stretched to 0.10 m: ½ × 80 × 0.10² = 0.40 J. Twice the extension, four times the energy.
  • Not ½ × 4.0 × 0.10: the force is now 8.0 N, not 4.0 N.
Quick check
?A steel wire obeys Hooke's law. A tension of 60 N gives an extension of 1.2 mm. How much elastic strain energy does the wire store?
Mission 4

Curved graphs: use the area

  • Beyond the limit of proportionality the graph curves. ½ × force × extension no longer works.
  • Find the area: count squares, or split it into triangles and trapezia.
  • Energy of one square = (force per square) × (extension per square).
  • Plastic region (the sample stays permanently stretched when unloaded): the work done is not all stored. Most becomes internal energy (the sample warms); the wire stays longer.
Quick check
?The graph shows an idealised force–extension graph for a metal wire. The force rises linearly from 0 to 30 N over the first 4.0 mm, then stays at 30 N as the extension increases to 10.0 mm. How much work is done stretching the wire to 10.0 mm?
Mission 4

Spring energy becomes kinetic and potential energy

  • A launcher spring, k = 200 N m⁻¹, compressed by 0.050 m, fires a 0.020 kg ball.
  • Stored: E = ½kx² = ½ × 200 × 0.050² = 0.25 J.
  • Fired horizontally, all to kinetic energy: ½mv² = 0.25 → v = √(2 × 0.25 ÷ 0.020) = 5.0 m s⁻¹.
  • Fired straight up, all to gravitational potential energy at the top: Δh = 0.25 ÷ (0.020 × 9.81) = 1.3 m above the release point.
  • Real launchers lose some energy to friction and air resistance: the ball rises less.
Quick check
?The spring in a toy dart launcher has a spring constant of 400 N m⁻¹. It is compressed by 0.030 m and then released, firing a dart of mass 0.012 kg horizontally. All the elastic potential energy is transferred to the dart's kinetic energy. What is the speed of the dart?
Mission check

?A spring obeys Hooke's law. When it is compressed by 3.0 cm it stores 0.45 J. How much energy does it store when it is compressed by 4.5 cm?

Energy = ½k × (extension)², so twice the stretch stores four times the energy: twice the extension, at twice the final force.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Keep cooling a gas in a sealed box. At what temperature would its pressure reach zero?

Make a guess: Keep cooling a gas in a sealed box. At what temperature would its pressure reach zero?

Mission 5

Pressure and the mole

  • p = F/A, F the force perpendicular to the surface. 1 Pa = 1 N m⁻².
  • A gas pushes perpendicular to every surface it touches.
  • 1 mole: NA = 6.02 × 10²³ particles (atoms or molecules).
  • Amount of substance n = N/NA.
  • Molar mass M: the mass of one mole. n = total mass ÷ M.
Quick check
?A cylinder contains 0.64 kg of oxygen gas, which consists of O2 molecules. The molar mass of oxygen atoms is 16 g mol⁻¹, and NA = 6.02 × 10²³ mol⁻¹. How many oxygen molecules are in the cylinder?
Mission 5

Boyle's law and its experiment

  • Fixed mass of gas at constant temperature: pV = constant. Halve the volume, double the pressure.
  • Apparatus: air trapped above oil in a sealed tube; a pump pushes the oil up; Bourdon gauge reads p; scale reads V. (Or: syringe sealed to a pressure sensor.)
  • Change V slowly, then wait: compressing warms the gas, and T must return to room temperature.
  • Plot pressure against 1/V: a straight line through the origin.
  • Errors to name: leaks (mass not fixed); temperature not constant; volume of tubing not counted.
Mission 5

Charles's law and the pressure law

  • Charles's law: constant pressure, V/T = constant.
  • Apparatus: air trapped in a capillary tube by a bead of liquid, tube in a water bath. Length of air column ∝ V.
  • Pressure law: constant volume, p/T = constant.
  • Apparatus: flask of air in a water bath, joined by short tubing to a pressure gauge.
  • T in kelvin, always.
  • Combine the three: pV/T = constant for a fixed mass of gas.
Quick check
?Predict first. Students measure the pressure of three samples of gas, each sealed in a rigid container, at several temperatures between 0 °C and 100 °C. The samples are of different gases and different amounts, and each behaves as an ideal gas. For each sample they plot pressure against temperature in °C and get a straight line. They then extend each best-fit line to zero pressure. What do they find?
Mission 5

Absolute zero by extrapolation

  • Fixed volume of gas: measure pressure at several temperatures, 0–100 °C.
  • Plot pressure against θ in °C: a straight line.
  • Extend the line to zero pressure: it meets the axis at about −273 °C.
  • Every gas, any amount: the same intercept.
  • This is absolute zero: 0 K = −273.15 °C.
  • T(K) = θ(°C) + 273.15 (often + 273).
Mission 5

The ideal gas equation

  • pV = nRT (n moles) and pV = NkT (N molecules)
  • R = 8.31 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ (molar gas constant).
  • k = R/NA = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J K⁻¹ (Boltzmann constant).
  • SI units only: pressure in Pa, V in m³, T in K.
Mission 5

Worked example: a gas cylinder

  • Rigid cylinder of helium: V = 0.020 m³, pressure 2.0 × 10⁵ Pa, θ = 20 °C → T = 293 K.
  • Moles: n = pV/RT = (2.0 × 10⁵ × 0.020)/(8.31 × 293) = 1.6 mol.
  • Molecules: N = pV/kT = (2.0 × 10⁵ × 0.020)/(1.38 × 10⁻²³ × 293) = 9.9 × 10²³.
  • Warmed to 60 °C (333 K), V fixed: pressure ∝ T, so the pressure = 2.0 × 10⁵ × 333/293 = 2.3 × 10⁵ Pa.
Quick check
?A bubble of gas of volume 2.0 cm³ is released at the bottom of a lake, where the pressure is 3.0 × 10⁵ Pa and the temperature is 7 °C. It rises to the surface, where the pressure is 1.0 × 10⁵ Pa and the temperature is 17 °C. Treat the gas as ideal, with no gas gained or lost. What is its volume at the surface?
Mission 5

Reading gas graphs

  • p against V, constant T: a curve (pV = constant). Hotter gas: curve further from the origin. Each curve is an isotherm.
  • pV against p, constant T: horizontal line.
  • V against T (constant p) and p against T (constant V): straight lines through 0 K.
  • On p–V axes, the product pV at a point is proportional to T (fixed amount of gas).
Quick check
?The pressure–volume diagram shows three states, A, B and C, of a fixed amount of ideal gas. Which ranks the temperatures of the gas in the three states, from highest to lowest?
Mission check

?A sealed, rigid container holds an ideal gas at 7 °C. The gas is heated to 287 °C. How does its new pressure compare with its starting pressure?

Pressure ∝ T in kelvin. Every gas line points to the same zero: 0 K = −273 °C, absolute zero.

Mission 6

Mission 6: In a cold room, metal feels colder than wood. Is it really at a lower temperature?

Make a guess: In a cold room, metal feels colder than wood. Is it really at a lower temperature?

Quick check
?A 0.20 kg copper block at 80 °C is lowered into a large tank containing 200 kg of water at 20 °C. The water has far more internal energy than the block. Which statement describes the resultant thermal energy transfer?
Mission 6

Which way does energy flow?

  • Temperature decides the direction: net energy transfer is always from higher to lower temperature.
  • Not "more energy to less": a hot cup can lose energy to a huge cold bath.
  • Heat is energy in transit because of a temperature difference. A body does not "contain heat".
  • Thermal equilibrium: no net energy transfer between bodies in contact. They are at the same temperature.
  • If A and B are each in thermal equilibrium with C (a thermometer), A and B are at the same temperature. This is why thermometers work.
Quick check
?Sample P is warmed from 10 °C to 30 °C. Sample Q is warmed from 290 K to 310 K. Which statement about the two temperature changes is correct?
Mission 6

Internal energy

  • Internal energy = total random kinetic energy + total potential energy of the particles.
  • KE: random motion (vibration in solids; moving about in liquids and gases).
  • PE: from the forces between particles. It depends on their separation.
  • The energies are randomly distributed: particles have a range of energies.
  • Not included: the KE of the whole object moving, or its gravitational PE.
  • Heat it → internal energy rises, and (unless it is changing phase) its temperature rises.
  • Temperature ≠ internal energy. A bath at 40 °C has far more internal energy than a cup at 40 °C: many more particles.
Quick check
?You have 1.0 kg of ice at 0 °C and 1.0 kg of liquid water at 0 °C. Which statement about their internal energies is correct?
Mission check

?A thermometer at room temperature, 20 °C, is placed in a cup of water at 70 °C. Its reading rises for a while and then stays steady. Why does the steady reading give the temperature of the water?

Metal and wood left in the same room reach thermal equilibrium with it: same temperature.

Metal feels colder because it conducts energy away from your warmer hand much faster.

Mission 7

Mission 7: How fast are the air molecules around you moving right now?

Make a guess: How fast are the air molecules around you moving right now?

Mission 7

The ideal gas model

  • A very large number of particles (atoms or molecules) in rapid, random motion.
  • Particle volume is negligible compared with the volume of the container.
  • Collisions with each other and the walls are perfectly elastic: no kinetic energy lost.
  • Collision time is negligible compared with the time between collisions.
  • No forces between particles except during collisions.
Quick check
?Careful measurements show that at very high pressures a real gas no longer obeys the ideal gas equation closely. Which reasoning, based on the assumptions of the ideal-gas model, best supports this observation?
Mission 7

Pressure from collisions

  • A molecule of mass m hits a wall with velocity component u perpendicular to it and rebounds elastically.
  • Its momentum changes by 2mu. So the wall pushes on it, and it pushes back on the wall (Newton's third law).
  • Many hits every second → an average force on the wall. Pressure = total perpendicular force ÷ area.
  • Smaller volume (T fixed): same speeds, but hits on each square metre are more frequent → pressure rises.
Quick check
?A sealed, rigid container of ideal gas is heated. What happens to the pressure of the gas, and why?
Mission 7

Temperature measures mean kinetic energy

  • ½m⟨c²⟩ = (3/2)kT, where ⟨c²⟩ is the mean square speed
  • Mean translational kinetic energy per molecule ∝ absolute temperature.
  • Translational: moving from place to place. Molecules such as N2 also spin; that energy is extra.
  • Same temperature → same mean translational kinetic energy, whatever the gas.
  • In this model, as T → 0 K this mean kinetic energy → 0: absolute zero.
  • k = 1.38 × 10⁻²³ J K⁻¹, the Boltzmann constant.
Mission 7

Worked example: root-mean-square speed

  • rms speed: square every speed, take the mean, then the square root.
  • Speeds 300, 400, 500 m s⁻¹: mean = 400; rms = √((300² + 400² + 500²)/3) = 408 m s⁻¹. Not the same.
  • Nitrogen, N2, at 300 K.
  • m = 0.028 kg mol⁻¹ ÷ 6.02 × 10²³ = 4.65 × 10⁻²⁶ kg.
  • Mean translational kinetic energy = 1.5 × 1.38 × 10⁻²³ × 300 = 6.21 × 10⁻²¹ J.
  • rms speed = √(3kT/m) = √(3 × 1.38 × 10⁻²³ × 300 ÷ 4.65 × 10⁻²⁶) = 517 m s⁻¹.
Quick check
?A sealed container of gas is heated from 27 °C to 177 °C. By what factor does the mean kinetic energy of the gas molecules increase?
Mission 7

Internal energy of an ideal gas

  • Internal energy: total random kinetic energy of the particles + total potential energy from the forces between them.
  • Ideal gas: no forces between particles → no potential energy. U is all kinetic.
  • Monatomic: U = (3/2)NkT = (3/2)nRT
  • U depends only on T (for a fixed amount). Same T → same U, whatever the volume.
Mission check

?A container holds a mixture of hydrogen (H2) and oxygen (O2) at one temperature. An oxygen molecule has 16 times the mass of a hydrogen molecule. What is (rms speed of the hydrogen molecules)/(rms speed of the oxygen molecules)?

Temperature fixes the mean translational kinetic energy. For air (mostly N2) at room temperature that means rms speeds near 500 m s⁻¹, faster than a passenger jet.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Extension (not length) is proportional to force: gradient of the force–extension graph = k, in N m⁻¹.
  • This holds up to the limit of proportionality.
  • Beyond the elastic limit the stretch is permanent.

Mission 2

  • Stress = F/A (Pa); strain = extension/original length (no unit); Young modulus = stress/strain, the gradient of the linear part of a stress–strain graph.
  • Measure the Young modulus with a long, thin wire: Young modulus = gradient of force against extension × original length ÷ area.
  • Design: breaking stress sets the minimum area, E sets the stretch; density and cost decide between materials that pass both.

Mission 3

  • Limit of proportionality: end of the straight line. Elastic limit: beyond it, deformation is plastic and permanent.

Mission 4

  • Elastic strain energy = area under the force–extension graph = ½Fx = ½kx² while Hooke's law holds; for a curved graph, find the area.

Mission 5

  • Fixed mass: pV = constant (constant T); V/T and p/T constant; combined pV/T = constant. T in kelvin.
  • Extrapolating pressure against θ (°C) to zero pressure gives absolute zero, −273 °C.
  • pV = nRT = NkT; k = R/NA.

Mission 6

  • Net energy transfer goes from higher to lower temperature until thermal equilibrium (same temperature, no net transfer).
  • Internal energy = random KE + intermolecular PE of the particles; absolute temperature measures the average random KE. T (K) ≈ temperature in °C + 273; temperature changes are the same in K and °C.

Mission 7

  • Ideal gas: many particles in random motion, negligible volume, elastic collisions, no forces except in collisions.
  • Pressure comes from momentum changes as particles hit a surface.
  • Mean translational KE per particle ∝ absolute temperature: (3/2)kT. Same T, same mean translational KE; lighter particles move faster.
  • Ideal gas internal energy is all kinetic: U = (3/2)nRT for a monatomic gas.
