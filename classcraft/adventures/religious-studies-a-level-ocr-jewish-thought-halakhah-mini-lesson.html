Mission 1

Kosher means "fit"

  • Kasher (kosher) means fit or proper; the system of food laws is kashrut.
  • It covers three things: which animals may be eaten; how they are killed and prepared; keeping meat and milk apart.
  • The laws come from the Torah (mainly Leviticus 11, Deuteronomy 14) and are developed in the Oral Torah (Talmud, tractate Chullin) and later codes (Shulchan Arukh, Yoreh De'ah).
  • Food that is not kosher is often called treif.
  • Plant foods are all kosher in themselves; they must be checked for insects.
  • Your spec writes G-d, a form many Jews use out of respect.

Q1 Why is the pig not kosher?

Show answer

Q2 What reason does Leviticus 11:44 give for the food laws?

Show answer

  • Birds: the Torah lists forbidden species (mostly birds of prey); Jews eat only birds with a tradition of being kosher (e.g. chicken, turkey, duck).
Mission 1

Why keep kashrut?

  • Holiness: eating becomes a way of being set apart for God (Leviticus 11:44; 20:26).
  • Self-discipline: Maimonides (1138–1204), Guide for the Perplexed III.35 and III.48: the food laws train people to control desire; he also suggests some forbidden foods are unhealthy.
  • Obedience: many laws are chukkim, statutes with no reason given. The Sifra on Leviticus 20:26 (paraphrase): do not say "I dislike pork", but "I would like it, yet my Father in heaven has forbidden it".
  • Respect for life: "the blood is the life" (Deuteronomy 12:23, JPS 1917), so blood is never eaten; slaughter must cause as little suffering as possible.
  • Identity: kashrut marks Jewish homes and community.
  • Spec rationales: "holiness and self-discipline".

Mission 1

Meat, dairy, parve

  • The Torah says three times: "Thou shalt not seethe a kid in its mother's milk" (Exodus 23:19, JPS 1917; also Exodus 34:26; Deuteronomy 14:21).
  • The Oral Torah reads the three verses as three bans: no cooking, eating or benefiting from meat and milk together (Chullin 115b).
  • The rabbis extend it: all meat and poultry (a rabbinic extension) with all dairy.
  • Parve (neutral): neither meat nor dairy, e.g. fish, eggs, fruit, vegetables, grains.
  • Waiting after meat before dairy: customs of six hours (most), three (many German Jews) or one (Dutch custom); based on Chullin 105a.
Mission 1

The kosher kitchen

  • Two sets of crockery, cutlery, pans and cloths: meat (fleishig) and dairy (milchig), often colour-coded.
  • Two sinks or separate washing-up bowls; sometimes two dishwashers or ovens.
  • Packaged food carries a hechsher (supervision mark) from a rabbinic authority, e.g. the London Beth Din (KLBD).
  • Kashering: making a utensil kosher again (e.g. boiling or heating), when a rabbi allows it.
  • A third set for Pesach, when no leaven (chametz) is used.
  • Children learn the system at home: the kitchen becomes a place of holiness.
Mission 1

Shehitah

  • Shehitah: kosher slaughter of mammals and birds. Source: "thou shalt kill … as I have commanded thee" (Deuteronomy 12:21, JPS 1917); the method is in the Oral Torah (Chullin).
  • The shohet (slaughterer) is a trained, God-fearing Jew, licensed by a rabbinic authority.
  • Knife (chalaf): very sharp, perfectly smooth, checked before and after.
  • One swift cut across the throat, severing the trachea and oesophagus; blood drains quickly.
  • Five faults make it invalid, e.g. pausing, pressing, tearing.
  • Then: inspection (bedikah), removal of forbidden fat and the sciatic nerve (Genesis 32:32 [Heb. 32:33]), salting to draw out blood.
  • Development: most authorities forbid stunning before the cut, because the animal must be uninjured when slaughtered.
Mission 1

Trefah

  • Trefah means "torn": "ye shall not eat any flesh that is torn of beasts in the field" (Exodus 22:31 [Heb. 22:30], JPS 1917).
  • In halakhah, trefah = an animal with a fatal defect or injury, even if slaughtered perfectly (Mishnah Chullin 3).
  • Nevelah = an animal that died by itself or was slaughtered wrongly (Deuteronomy 14:21).
  • Inspection of the lungs is key; glatt ("smooth") means lungs with no adhesions, a stricter standard.
  • In everyday speech "treif" covers any non-kosher food, a widening of the word.

Mission 1Diversity

Orthodox and progressive

  • Orthodox: kashrut is God's law, binding in every detail; strict supervision; many eat only in kosher restaurants.
  • Masorti: binding, with some leniencies (e.g. some eat vegetarian food in non-kosher restaurants).
  • Reform (1885): the Pittsburgh Platform said laws on diet "originated in ages and under the influence of ideas entirely foreign to our present mental and spiritual state".
  • Progressive today: informed choice: many avoid pork and shellfish, or keep meat and milk apart at home; some are vegetarian; some keep "eco-kashrut" (ethical, sustainable food).
Mission 1Evaluate

Kashrut in the 21st century

  • Claim: Halakhah on food is still relevant today.
  • For: It shapes Jewish identity and family life; holiness and self-discipline do not date.
  • For: Shehitah aims at a fast cut with minimal suffering; rabbis argue it causes rapid loss of consciousness.
  • Against: Animal-welfare groups (e.g. the British Veterinary Association) argue that slaughter without stunning causes avoidable pain; Denmark (2014) and the two largest Belgian regions (2019) banned it.
  • Against: Many progressive Jews keep little or none; for them ethics may matter more than ritual.
  • Judgement criteria: Relevance to whom (Orthodox or progressive)? Is relevance measured by practice, by meaning or by ethics? Should religious freedom limit animal-welfare law?
  • Spec issues: "how halakhah has shaped the expression of Jewish religious identity"; "the relevance of halakhah for the twenty-first century".

Mission 2

Business is a religious matter

  • In Judaism, money is not outside religion: one of the four sections of the Shulchan Arukh, Choshen Mishpat, deals with money, property and trade.
  • Talmud, Shabbat 31a (paraphrase): the first question asked of a person at the final judgement is "Did you do business faithfully?"
  • Dishonest trade is chillul ha-Shem, profaning God's name; honest trade is kiddush ha-Shem, making it holy.
  • Two topics in your spec: loans and interest; deception.
  • Your spec writes G-d, a form many Jews use out of respect.
Mission 2Read the source

Lending without interest

> "If thou lend money to any of My people, even to the poor with thee, thou shalt not be to him as a creditor; neither shall ye lay upon him interest." (Exodus 22:25 [Heb. 22:24], JPS 1917)

> "Take thou no interest of him or increase; but fear thy God; that thy brother may live with thee." (Leviticus 25:36, JPS 1917)

Q1 Who is the loan in Exodus 22:25 meant to help?

Show answer

The poor among God's people.

Q2 What reason does Leviticus 25:36 give for the ban?

Show answer

Fear of God, and so that the brother "may live with thee": the loan must help, not trap him.

Mission 2

Ribbit: the ban on interest

  • Hebrew words: neshekh ("bite") and tarbit or marbit ("increase"); the rabbis call interest ribbit.
  • The ban is between Jew and Jew: "Unto a foreigner thou mayest lend upon interest; but unto thy brother thou shalt not" (Deuteronomy 23:20 [Heb. 23:21], JPS 1917).
  • Mishnah Bava Metzia 5:11: lender, borrower, guarantor and witnesses all break the law. The borrower's consent does not make it allowed.
  • The rabbis add a fence: avak ribbit, "dust of interest" (e.g. a gift or favour in return for a loan).
  • The Psalms praise the one who "putteth not out his money on interest" (Psalm 15:5, JPS 1917); Ezekiel 18:8 likewise.
Mission 2

Lending as a mitzvah

  • Lending to someone in need is a mitzvah: "… shalt surely lend him sufficient for his need" (Deuteronomy 15:8, JPS 1917).
  • Talmud (Shabbat 63a, paraphrase): lending is greater than giving charity, because it keeps the borrower's dignity.
  • Gemach (from gemilut chasadim, "acts of loving-kindness"): free-loan funds of money or goods, found in many Jewish communities.
  • Shemittah: every seventh year debts were released (Deuteronomy 15:1–2).
  • Development: people stopped lending before the seventh year (the Torah warns against this, Deuteronomy 15:9). Hillel created the prozbul, a court document that keeps the loan collectable, so the poor could still borrow (Mishnah Shevi'it 10:3).

Mission 2

From medieval moneylending to the heter iska

  • Medieval Europe: the Church forbade Christians to lend at interest; Jews were often barred from guilds and land, so moneylending became one of few trades open to them.
  • Rulers taxed Jewish lenders heavily, then often expelled them (England 1290). The usurer stereotype became an antisemitic myth.
  • Rabbis debated lending to non-Jews at interest (allowed by Deuteronomy 23:20), some justifying it by necessity.
  • Heter iska ("permission for a venture"), developed in Poland from the 17th century: the loan is rewritten as a business partnership; the investor earns a share of profit, not interest.
  • Today Israeli banks and Orthodox lenders use a heter iska so that business between Jews can follow halakhah.
Mission 2

Verbal deception

  • Ona'at devarim: wronging with words. "Ye shall not wrong one another; but thou shalt fear thy God" (Leviticus 25:17, JPS 1917).
  • Mishnah Bava Metzia 4:10: do not ask "How much is this?" if you have no intention of buying: it raises false hope.
  • Do not remind a person who has changed of their past wrongs.
  • Talmud (Bava Metzia 58b, paraphrase): shaming someone in public is like shedding blood; verbal wrong is worse than monetary wrong, because money can be returned but words cannot.
  • "Fear thy God" is attached because only God knows the speaker's intention.
Mission 2

Monetary deception

  • Ona'at mamon: wronging in money, e.g. overcharging. "Ye shall not wrong one another" (Leviticus 25:14).
  • Mishnah Bava Metzia 4:3: an overcharge of one sixth must be refunded; more than one sixth, the sale can be cancelled.
  • Geneivat da'at ("stealing the mind"): creating a false impression, even with no money lost; forbidden towards anyone, Jew or non-Jew (Chullin 94a).
  • Examples (Mishnah Bava Metzia 4:11–12): no painting old goods to look new; no mixing poor produce into good.
  • Lifnei iver ("before the blind", Leviticus 19:14): do not give advice that suits you but harms the person who trusts you.
Mission 2Read the source

Just weights

> "Ye shall do no unrighteousness in judgment, in meteyard, in weight, or in measure. Just balances, just weights, a just ephah, and a just hin, shall ye have …" (Leviticus 19:35–36, JPS 1917)

> "Thou shalt not have in thy bag diverse weights, a great and a small." (Deuteronomy 25:13, JPS 1917)

  • Mishnah Bava Batra 5:10: traders must clean their measures and weights regularly, so no residue changes the amount; the Talmud has communities appoint market inspectors (Bava Batra 89a).

Mission 2

Contemporary applications

  • Advertising: "sale" prices that were never the normal price; fake reviews; hidden fees = geneivat da'at.
  • Pricing: exploiting a shortage to overcharge = ona'at mamon (rabbis debate how far the one-sixth rule fits modern markets).
  • Advice: a financial adviser pushing a product for commission = lifnei iver.
  • Online words: public shaming and abusive reviews = ona'at devarim.
  • Law of the land: "dina de-malkhuta dina" ("the law of the kingdom is the law", Bava Kamma 113a): Jews must keep honest tax and consumer law.
  • Lending: many communities run gemachs; some campaign against exploitative high-interest lending.
Mission 2Diversity

Orthodox and progressive approaches

  • Orthodox: these laws are binding halakhah, studied in detail (Choshen Mishpat); rabbinic courts (Beth Din) can hear business disputes; heter iska is used for loans between Jews.
  • Progressive (Reform, Liberal): the ethical mitzvot are the heart of Judaism (Pittsburgh 1885 kept "moral laws" as binding); the detail of ribbit is often not applied to modern commercial interest, but its purpose (no exploitation of the poor) is.
  • Shared: honesty, fair weights, dignity in lending and care for the poor.
  • Difference: how the laws bind (divine law in detail, or ethical principle), not whether honesty matters.
Mission 2Evaluate

Is halakhah on business relevant today?

  • Claim: Halakhah on business ethics is relevant in the 21st century.
  • For: Deception, overcharging and exploitative lending are modern problems; the rules are precise and practical.
  • For: Geneivat da'at reaches further than most consumer law: it forbids false impressions even when no money is lost.
  • Against: Rules framed for an agricultural economy (interest-free loans, the one-sixth rule) fit modern finance badly; heter iska can look like a way round the law.
  • Against: Secular law and regulators now cover much of this ground.
  • Judgement criteria: Are the rules or the principles what matters? Does relevance mean being kept, or being right? Does religious motivation add anything to legal duties?
  • Spec issue: "the relevance of halakhah for the twenty-first century".

Mission 3

Sex as a mitzvah

  • In Judaism, sex within marriage is holy, not shameful; marriage is called kiddushin, "holiness".
  • Procreation: "Be fruitful, and multiply" (Genesis 1:28, JPS 1917) is counted as the first mitzvah. The school of Hillel (Mishnah Yevamot 6:6): at least a son and a daughter.
  • Companionship and pleasure: a husband owes his wife onah, her "conjugal rights" (Exodus 21:10, JPS 1917): a duty to her, not to himself.
  • So sex has value even when children are not possible (e.g. after menopause).
  • Holiness also brings boundaries: when, with whom and how.
  • Your spec writes G-d, a form many Jews use out of respect.

Mission 3

Niddah

  • Niddah: a woman during and after menstruation. Couples refrain from sex, and in Orthodox practice from all touch.
  • Origin: "she shall be in her impurity seven days" (Leviticus 15:19, JPS 1917); "thou shalt not approach unto a woman … as long as she is impure" (Leviticus 18:19, JPS 1917).
  • Development: the rabbis added seven "clean" days after bleeding ends (Niddah 66a), so the separation usually lasts about 12 days.
  • These laws are called taharat ha-mishpachah, "family purity".
  • Tumah (ritual impurity) is not dirt or sin: it is a ritual status.
  • Supporters: separation renews the marriage; respects the woman's body. Critics: impurity language can make women feel unclean.
Mission 3

Mikveh

  • Mikveh: a pool of natural water (rainwater or spring) of at least 40 se'ah, built to set rules.
  • At the end of niddah, the woman immerses fully, at night, with a blessing; the couple may then resume relations.
  • Other uses: conversion, new utensils; some men (especially Hasidim) immerse before Shabbat and Yom Kippur.
  • Orthodox: keep niddah and mikveh; communities build a mikveh even before a synagogue.
  • Progressive: most do not keep niddah; some reclaim mikveh for new meanings (healing, milestones, conversion).
  • Spec term: "niddah … including use of mikveh".
Mission 3

Prohibited relations

  • Leviticus 18 and 20 list the arayot ("nakedness"): forbidden relations.
  • They include incest, adultery, sex during niddah (a woman's monthly period and the days after), male–male intercourse ("Thou shalt not lie with mankind, as with womankind", Leviticus 18:22, JPS 1917) and bestiality.
  • Gilui arayot (forbidden relations) is one of three sins a Jew must die rather than commit (Sanhedrin 74a).
  • Sex before marriage: forbidden in Orthodox halakhah; many progressive Jews accept committed relationships before marriage.
  • Spec wording: "'prohibited' sexual acts or relations": the quotation marks signal that what is prohibited is now debated.

Mission 3Diversity

Same-sex relationships

  • Orthodox: Leviticus 18:22 forbids male–male intercourse; rabbis also forbid sex between women (a lesser, rabbinic ban). Orientation itself is not a sin; acts are what halakhah addresses. Same-sex marriage is not recognised.
  • Orthodox responses vary: a 2010 US "Statement of Principles" (many Modern Orthodox rabbis) urged welcome and dignity for gay Jews; the UK Chief Rabbi's guide for Orthodox schools (2018) addresses LGBT+ pupils' wellbeing.
  • Masorti / Conservative (US): since 2006 allows gay and lesbian rabbis; since 2012 same-sex ceremonies.
  • Reform and Liberal: full equality: same-sex marriages, LGBT+ rabbis; Leviticus 18:22 read as tied to its ancient context, or outweighed by the dignity of every person made in God's image (Genesis 1:27).
Mission 3

Transgender matters

  • Many Orthodox authorities: a person's halakhic sex is fixed by the body at birth; surgery raises bans (e.g. on castration, Leviticus 22:24; Deuteronomy 22:5 on dress). Pastoral responses differ.
  • A minority Orthodox view: Rabbi Eliezer Waldenberg (1915–2006) held that after surgery a person's halakhic sex can change in some respects.
  • Reform and Liberal: affirm transgender and non-binary Jews; e.g. the US Union for Reform Judaism resolution (2015); liturgy for transition.
  • Question for all: how ancient categories (male, female) apply to modern medicine.

Mission 3

Rachel Adler

  • Rachel Adler (b. 1943), Jewish feminist theologian; Engendering Judaism (1998).
  • 1972, as an Orthodox writer: defended niddah and mikveh as a cycle of death and rebirth; later (1993) rejected her own essay, saying purity laws harm women.
  • Argues halakhah must be rebuilt with women's voices as full partners.
  • Brit Ahuvim ("lovers' covenant"): a marriage ceremony based on a partnership contract, not on the traditional act of acquisition (kinyan) in which the man acquires the wife.
  • It is open to same-sex couples.
  • Critics (Orthodox): rejects the halakhic form of kiddushin.
Mission 3Evaluate

  • Claim: Halakhah on sex is relevant in the 21st century.
  • For: It values sex as holy, protects the wife's rights (onah) and builds respect and boundaries into marriage.
  • For: Many Orthodox couples say niddah renews intimacy each month.
  • Against: Laws on same-sex relations and gender clash with modern equality; LGBT+ Jews may feel excluded.

Mission 4

Four possible answers

  • "Who is a Jew?" has more than one Jewish answer. Four factors keep coming up:
  • Birth: being born into the Jewish people.
  • Faith: believing in God and the teachings of Judaism.
  • Works: keeping the mitzvot (commandments) and living a Jewish life.
  • Ritual: ceremonies that mark belonging, such as brit milah and bar/bat mitzvah.
  • Jews are a people as well as a faith: someone can be Jewish by birth and not religious at all.
  • Groups weigh these factors differently; that is where the disagreements lie.
Mission 4

Born of a Jewish mother

  • Halakhah (Jewish law) defines a Jew as someone born of a Jewish mother, or someone who has converted according to halakhah (see Mission 5).
  • The father's status does not decide it: the Mishnah rules that the child of a non-Jewish mother takes the mother's status (Kiddushin 3:12, paraphrase); the Talmud derives the converse, that the child of a Jewish mother is Jewish (next screen).
  • This matrilineal rule is held by Orthodox and Masorti (Conservative) Judaism, and is the basis of Jewish status in the Chief Rabbinate of Israel.
  • Reasons given: the Talmud derives it from the Torah (next screen); some modern writers add that a child's mother is always known, or that mothers were the main teachers of faith at home. Those are later explanations, not the legal source.
Mission 4Read the source

Deuteronomy 7:3–4

> "neither shalt thou make marriages with them: thy daughter thou shalt not give unto his son, nor his daughter shalt thou take unto thy son." (Deuteronomy 7:3, JPS 1917)

> "For he will turn away thy son from following Me, that they may serve other gods" (Deuteronomy 7:4, JPS 1917)

  • The Talmud (Kiddushin 68b, paraphrase) notes "he" in verse 4 is the non-Jewish father, who would turn away "thy son": that is, the grandson born to your daughter is still called "thy son". So the child of a Jewish mother is Jewish.
  • The child of a Jewish father and a non-Jewish mother is not called "thy son"; halakhically, that child is not Jewish without conversion.

Q1 In verse 3, which marriage leads to a child the Talmud still calls "thy son"?

Show answer

Your daughter marrying a non-Jewish man: her child stays Jewish.

Q2 Is this rule written plainly in the verse, or derived by interpretation?

Show answer

Derived: the Talmud reads it out of the wording, as part of the oral Torah.

Mission 4

Once a Jew, always a Jew?

  • In halakhah, Jewish status by birth cannot be lost. "An Israelite, even though he has sinned, remains an Israelite" (Talmud Sanhedrin 44a, paraphrase).
  • So a Jew who never practises, or who joins another religion, is still halakhically Jewish (though regarded as a sinner).
  • Israeli law is different. The Law of Return (1950; amended 1970) gives the right to settle to Jews, their children and grandchildren. In 1962 Israel's Supreme Court ruled that Brother Daniel (Oswald Rufeisen), born Jewish but a Catholic monk, did not count as a Jew under that law.

Mission 4

Faith and works: a people as well as a religion

  • Faith: Maimonides' Thirteen Principles set out beliefs; some Jews see belief as central to being a "good" Jew, but not to being a Jew.
  • Works: for many Orthodox Jews, Jewishness is lived through the mitzvot: Shabbat, kashrut, prayer, study.
  • Secular and cultural Jews: identity through family, history, language, food, Israel, memory of the Holocaust. Humanistic Judaism (founded 1963) celebrates Jewish culture without belief in God.
  • This is the background to patrilineal descent and "Jewishness" (see Mission 5).

Mission 5

A non-missionising religion

  • Judaism does not send missionaries or seek converts.
  • Reason: non-Jews do not need to become Jewish to be righteous. The rabbis taught that the righteous of all nations have a share in the world to come (Tosefta Sanhedrin 13:2, paraphrase), if they keep the seven Noahide laws.
  • So a convert is not "saved" by converting; they take on the covenant and its 613 mitzvot.
  • Yet sincere converts are welcomed: a convert (ger, or ger tzedek, "righteous convert") becomes fully Jewish.
  • Your spec writes G-d, a form many Jews use out of respect, and spells the code Shulkhan Arukh.
Mission 5Read the source

Ruth, the model convert

> "for whither thou goest, I will go; and where thou lodgest, I will lodge; thy people shall be my people, and thy God my God" (Ruth 1:16, JPS 1917)

  • Ruth, a Moabite widow, refuses to leave her Israelite mother-in-law Naomi.
  • Naomi urges her to go back several times (Ruth 1:8–15). The rabbis drew from this the custom of discouraging a candidate at first.
  • Ruth becomes the great-grandmother of King David (Ruth 4:17).

Q1 Which two things does Ruth join?

Show answer

A people, "thy people", and a faith, "thy God": both together.

Q2 Why might rabbis turn a candidate away at first?

Show answer

To test sincerity, as Naomi tested Ruth.

Mission 5

The Shulchan Arukh: questions to the convert

  • Shulchan Arukh ("set table"), the law code of Joseph Karo (1488–1575), printed 1565; conversion in Yoreh De'ah 268, drawing on the Talmud (Yevamot 47a–b).
  • The candidate is asked (paraphrase): Why do you want to convert? Do you not know that Jews in this time are oppressed and suffer?
  • If they answer, "I know, and I am not worthy", they are accepted.
  • They are taught the basics of faith (God's unity; the ban on idolatry) and some lighter and stricter mitzvot, with their rewards and punishments, but not burdened with too much at once.
  • The aim: an informed, free and sincere commitment.
Mission 5

Three requirements, before a beth din

  • Acceptance of the mitzvot (kabbalat ol mitzvot): commitment to live by halakhah. In Orthodox law, this is essential.
  • Circumcision (milah) for men. If already circumcised, a drop of blood is drawn as a sign of the covenant (hatafat dam brit).
  • Immersion in a mikveh (ritual pool of natural water) for men and women: the person emerges as a Jew.
  • All before a beth din (rabbinical court) of three judges.
  • In Temple times an offering was also brought; since the Temple's destruction this is not possible.
  • The convert takes a Hebrew name as a child of Abraham and Sarah.
Mission 5

Male and female processes compared

  • Same for both: questions; teaching; acceptance of the mitzvot; a beth din of three; immersion in the mikveh; full Jewish status afterwards.
  • Men: circumcision first; after healing, immersion before the beth din.
  • Women: immersion only. For modesty, women attend her in the water, while the three judges remain outside the room and witness it without seeing her (Shulchan Arukh, Yoreh De'ah 268:2, paraphrase; arrangements today vary).
  • So the woman's process is shorter, but not lesser: her status afterwards is the same.
  • Women converts in Orthodox law then keep the mitzvot that bind Jewish women (e.g. niddah).

Mission 5

After conversion: a full member

  • A convert is fully Jewish, as if newly born into the people (Talmud Yevamot 22a, paraphrase).
  • It is forbidden to remind a convert of their past to shame them (Talmud Bava Metzia 58b).
  • "thou shalt love him as thyself; for ye were strangers in the land of Egypt" (Leviticus 19:34, JPS 1917).
  • The community's role: the beth din decides; a rabbi and synagogue teach and support the candidate for months or years; afterwards the community must accept the convert in marriage, worship and daily life.
  • Identity: conversion is joining a people as well as a faith (Ruth 1:16); converts often describe learning Jewish history and customs as becoming part of a family story.
Mission 5Diversity

Orthodox, Masorti and Progressive conversion

  • Orthodox: full commitment to halakhah (Shabbat, kashrut, family purity), usually after 1–3 years of study and living near an Orthodox community; milah and mikveh required. Orthodox batei din accept only Orthodox conversions.
  • Masorti (Conservative): halakhic process with milah, mikveh and a beth din; commitment to Jewish practice, interpreted less strictly. Not accepted by Orthodox authorities.
  • Reform and Liberal: study (often about a year), commitment to Jewish life, and a beth din. Rules on milah and mikveh vary: British Reform generally asks for both; American Reform has not required them since the 1890s, though many rabbis encourage them.
  • Consequence: a convert may be Jewish in one movement and not in another. In Israel, Progressive conversions count for citizenship under the Law of Return (court rulings, latest 2021), but the Chief Rabbinate does not accept them for marriage.
Mission 5

Patrilineal descent

  • Patrilineal descent: the child of a Jewish father (and a non-Jewish mother) can be Jewish without conversion.
  • US Reform (Central Conference of American Rabbis, 1983): a child of either Jewish parent is presumed Jewish if raised as a Jew, shown by "timely" public acts of Jewish identity (e.g. naming, Jewish education, bar/bat mitzvah). Reconstructionist Judaism adopted a similar policy in 1968.
  • UK Liberal Judaism also accepts a child of either Jewish parent raised as a Jew.
  • Orthodox and Masorti reject it: status passes through the mother (Kiddushin 68b). Such a person must convert to be Jewish in their eyes.
  • Note: the Reform rule asks more of some children (a Jewish upbringing), not only less: a child of a Jewish mother raised with no Jewish identity may not be presumed Jewish.
  • "Jewishness": the debate is over whether identity is a legal status (halakhah) or a lived, chosen belonging.

Mission 5Evaluate

Should halakhah be the only test of who is a Jew?

  • Claim: Halakhah (the mother's line or halakhic conversion) should be the only test of who is a Jew.
  • For: One clear standard keeps the Jewish people united; every movement accepts Orthodox conversions, but Orthodox authorities accept no others.
  • For: It rests on the Torah as interpreted by the oral law (Deuteronomy 7:4 in Kiddushin 68b; Yevamot 47a–b).
  • Against: It can exclude people who live fully Jewish lives (a child of a Jewish father raised in a synagogue).
  • Against: Progressive Jews see halakhah as evolving; equal treatment of fathers and mothers reflects modern values and many mixed families.
  • Judgement criteria: Unity or inclusion? Status or lived identity? Who holds authority: the beth din, a movement, the State of Israel?

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Kashrut (fitness) comes from Leviticus 11 and Deuteronomy 14 and is developed in the Oral Torah; reasons include holiness, self-discipline and obedience.
  • Meat and milk are kept apart (from Exodus 23:19 as the rabbis read it); parve foods are neutral; a kosher kitchen keeps two sets of utensils.
  • Shehitah by a trained shohet; trefah means an animal with a fatal defect; Orthodox Jews keep every detail, progressive Jews choose how far.

Mission 2

  • Interest (ribbit) between Jews is forbidden for lender and borrower alike; lending to the needy is a mitzvah; Hillel's prozbul and the heter iska show the law developing.
  • Deception has three faces: verbal (ona'at devarim), monetary (ona'at mamon) and false impressions (geneivat da'at); weights and measures must be just.
  • Orthodox Jews keep these as detailed halakhah; progressive Jews stress the ethical principles behind them.

Mission 3

  • In Judaism, sex within marriage is a mitzvah: for procreation, and for closeness and pleasure (onah, the wife's right).
  • Niddah separates a couple during and after menstruation (seven clean days, then mikveh); Leviticus 18 lists prohibited relations.
  • Orthodox Jews forbid same-sex acts while many stress welcome for gay Jews; Reform and Liberal Jews affirm same-sex marriage.
  • Reform and Liberal Jews affirm transgender Jews; Rachel Adler rebuilds marriage as a partnership (Brit Ahuvim).

Mission 4

  • Halakhah: a Jew is born of a Jewish mother or converted; birth status cannot be lost.
  • Faith, works and ritual shape how Jews live their identity; secular Jews show identity can be cultural as well as religious.

Mission 5

  • Judaism welcomes sincere converts but does not seek them; Ruth is the model.
  • Shulchan Arukh (Yoreh De'ah 268): questions, teaching, acceptance of the mitzvot, circumcision (men) and mikveh (all), before a beth din of three.
  • Orthodox, Masorti and Progressive movements differ on conversion and on patrilineal descent, so "Jewishness" depends on who is asked.
