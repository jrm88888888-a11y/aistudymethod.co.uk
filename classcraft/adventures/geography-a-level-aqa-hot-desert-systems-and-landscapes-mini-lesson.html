Mission 1

Mission 1: Some desert dunes hold sand that has travelled thousands of kilometres. Where does desert sand come from?

Mission 1

The desert as an open system

  • Inputs: energy (sunlight, wind, rare rain) and sediment (from rock, rivers, the coast).
  • Processes (transfers): weathering, mass movement, erosion, transport, deposition.
  • Stores and outputs: dune fields, playas, fans; sediment exported by rivers or wind.
  • A landform is one feature (a dune, a fan); a landscape is the whole set of related landforms in an area.
Mission 1

Energy 1: insolation

  • "The clear, dry air and sparse plant cover allow sunlight to heat the desert surface".
  • Death Valley, 15 July 1972: ground 93.9 °C while the air was 53.3 °C (converted from 201 °F and 128 °F).
  • Summer days often top 49 °C in the shade; nights fall only to the mid-30s °C.
  • Heating and cooling stress rock; heat also dries the surface so that wind can lift grains.
Mission 1

Energy 2: wind

  • Wind erodes by deflation (removing loose fine grains) and abrasion (sand-blasting).
  • It transports fine particles under 0.2 mm in suspension; sand moves by saltation, usually no more than 1 cm above the ground, and by surface creep, up to 25% of grain movement.
  • Almost half of Earth's desert surfaces are stony deflation zones.
Mission 1

Energy 3: runoff

  • Rain is rare but intense: 44 mm once fell in 3 hours in the Sahara; big storms deliver up to 1 mm a minute.
  • Dry channels (wadis, arroyos) fill fast in flash floods.
  • Floods "transport considerable quantities of sediment for a day or two".
  • USGS: "water is much more powerful than wind".
Quick check
?USGS says water is "much more powerful than wind" in deserts, yet much of a desert's surface is shaped by wind. How can both be true?
Mission 1

Processes in the system

  • Weathering breaks rock: at Arches (Utah), water slowly dissolves the calcite cement, and ice in cracks expands.
  • Mass movement moves weathered debris downslope under gravity, to the foot of cliffs and slopes.
  • Erosion and transport: wind and floods.
  • Deposition: alluvial fans where floods spread out; dunes where wind slows.
Mission 1

Sediment sources

  • Local rock: in Death Valley, "eroded canyons and washes provide plenty of sand".
  • Rivers: the Nile, Colorado and Yellow are exotic rivers that cross deserts and carry sediment to the sea.
  • Wind-blown dust from deflation: most dust is silt; it builds loess, up to 335 m thick on China's Loess Plateau.
  • Coast: in the Namib Sand Sea, sand from the interior, transported "thousands of kilometres", arrives by "river, ocean current and wind".
Mission 1

Sediment cells

  • A sediment cell links sources, transfers and sinks (stores) in one area.
  • Death Valley: sand from washes is blown into dune fields, "trapped by geographic features such as mountains".
  • Dunes cover less than 1% of Death Valley: sinks are small and patchy.
  • Other sinks: playas (clay, silt or salt flats) and alluvial fans.
Mission 1

Sediment budgets

  • Budget = inputs − outputs for a store, over a period.
  • Positive: more arrives than leaves; the dune field or fan grows.
  • Negative: more leaves than arrives; the surface is stripped, leaving gravel (desert pavement).
  • Dynamic equilibrium: inputs and outputs balance, so the landform keeps its size while grains pass through.
Quick check
?In a model dune field, washes supply 40,000 m³ of sand a year and wind blows in another 10,000 m³. Wind removes 65,000 m³ a year. What is the sediment budget?
Mission 1

How related landforms combine

  • Alluvial fans spread from canyon mouths; where they merge they form a bajada.
  • Sediment then reaches the basin floor: playa (fine sediment, salt) and dunes (sand blown off fans and washes).
  • Up on the plateau, a mesa erodes into buttes, then spires.
  • Linked by the same flows of energy and sediment, these landforms build a landscape.
Quick check
?Which of these is a landscape, rather than a single landform?
Mission check

?In a model desert basin, a flash flood carries gravel, sand and silt out of a canyon. Over the following years, where does each mainly end up?

Desert sand comes from weathered rock, rivers, dust and even the sea. Energy from insolation, wind and runoff moves it through sediment cells, and the budget decides whether dunes, fans and playas grow or shrink.

Mission 2

Dryness, not heat, makes a desert

  • The USGS summarises Meigs's scheme: arid land gets under 250 mm of rain a year; semi-arid land 250–500 mm; extremely arid land has at least 12 consecutive months without rain.
  • "Arid and extremely arid land are deserts", while semi-arid grasslands are usually called steppes.
  • About one-third of Earth's land is desert. Deserts "may be hot, they may be cold … But deserts are always dry".
Mission 2

The aridity index

  • Aridity index (AI) = mean annual precipitation ÷ potential evapotranspiration (P ÷ PET). PET is the water that would evaporate and transpire if water were always available.
  • UNEP classes, as used by the IPCC: hyper-arid below 0.05; arid 0.05–0.20; semi-arid 0.20–0.50; dry sub-humid 0.50–0.65; humid above 0.65.
  • Drylands (AI 0.65 or below) cover about 46.2% of the land and are home to 3 billion people.
  • An index beats a rainfall total: 300 mm is dry in a hot place, where PET is high, but less dry where PET is low.
Quick check
?A place receives 300 mm of precipitation a year, and its potential evapotranspiration is 1,200 mm. Which UNEP class is it in?
Mission 2

Where the hot deserts are

  • Subtropical high pressure: around 30° N and S, air that rose at the Equator sinks; it is dry "because its moisture has already been released near the Equator". Most non-polar deserts lie in the trade-wind belts, including the Sahara.
  • Cold currents: coastal deserts such as the Atacama and Namib lie "on the western edges of continents near the Tropics" and are "affected by cold ocean currents that parallel the coast". Air chilled from below is stable: fog, not rain.
  • Continentality: midlatitude deserts lie "in interior drainage basins far from oceans".
  • Rain shadow: four mountain ranges lie between Death Valley and the ocean, "each one adding to an increasingly drier rainshadow effect".
Mission 2

Arid cores and semi-arid margins

  • Deserts grade outward into semi-arid margins: steppe and savanna grassland with 250–500 mm of rain.
  • Margins have a short rainy season and very variable totals from year to year.
  • Margins hold far more people than desert cores, so they are where land degradation matters most.
Mission 2

Hot desert climate: Coober Pedy

  • Coober Pedy Airport, South Australia (1994–2026): mean annual rainfall 144.1 mm; January mean maximum 36.9 °C.
  • Rain is unreliable: the wettest year had 308.2 mm (2016), the driest 24.4 mm (2019).
  • Clear skies give a large diurnal range: strong daytime heating, rapid night-time cooling.
  • Exam move: quote the mean, the range and an extreme, then say what the variability means for plants and people.
Mission 2

The water balance

  • Water balance: precipitation = evapotranspiration + runoff ± change in storage.
  • In hot deserts potential evapotranspiration far exceeds precipitation, so there is a water deficit for most or all of the year.
  • Most rain evaporates or soaks briefly into the soil; runoff is rare but fast, in ephemeral channels after storms.
  • Groundwater (sometimes fossil water from wetter times) and exotic rivers such as the Nile are the main reliable stores.
Mission 2

Soils, vegetation and their interaction

  • Aridisols: thin, sandy or stony, low in organic matter; evaporation draws salts up through the soil (capillary rise), leaving salt or calcium carbonate crusts near the surface.
  • Xerophytes: plants adapted to drought, with deep or wide shallow roots, small or waxy leaves, spines, or water storage (succulents).
  • Biological soil crusts of cyanobacteria, lichens and mosses glue the top few millimetres of soil together, stabilising it against erosion; they "may constitute up to 70% of the living cover in some plant communities".
  • Interaction: sparse plants add little organic matter, so soils stay poor and hold little water, which keeps vegetation sparse.
Quick check
?Why are most of the world's large hot deserts found around 30° N and S?
Mission check

?Coober Pedy, in the Australian outback, gets about 144 mm of rain a year. Utqiaġvik, on Alaska's frozen Arctic coast, gets about 137 mm of precipitation. Why do both count as deserts?

Deserts are defined by dryness. Antarctica's ice has built up over long periods despite low precipitation, about 150 mm of water a year on average and under 50 mm on the plateau: it is a polar desert, like Utqiaġvik, while Coober Pedy is a hot desert.

Mission 3

Mission 3: The Atacama lies right beside the Pacific Ocean, yet it is the driest desert on Earth. How?

Make a guess: The Atacama lies right beside the Pacific Ocean, yet it is the driest desert on Earth. How?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

  • 3.1.2.1 causes of aridity: atmospheric pressure, winds, continentality, relief (rain shadow) and cold ocean currents
Mission 3

No rising air, no rain

  • Rain needs air to rise: a rising parcel "expands due to the lower pressure higher in the atmosphere" and cools.
  • Cooling makes vapour condense into cloud; rain follows if cloud droplets grow.
  • So a place is arid if (1) air sinks rather than rises, or (2) the air reaching it has already lost its moisture.
  • Five causes: pressure, winds, continentality, relief and cold ocean currents.
Mission 3

Pressure: the subtropical highs

  • Around 30° N and S, air from the equator "begins to descend toward the surface in subtropical high-pressure belts": the sinking limb of the Hadley cell.
  • That air "is relatively dry because its moisture has already been released near the Equator".
  • Sinking air is compressed and warms, so clouds evaporate rather than form.
  • The result is clear skies and high pressure "over the subtropical oceans and deserts like the Sahara".
Mission 3

Winds: the trade winds

  • Air flowing from the subtropical highs towards the equator is deflected west: the north-east and south-east trade winds.
  • "Most of the major deserts of the world lie in areas crossed by the trade winds".
  • "These dry winds dissipate cloud cover, allowing more sunlight to heat the land".
  • Where trade winds blow from land to sea, they carry little moisture inland.
Quick check
?Why does air sinking in the subtropical high-pressure belt bring so little rain?
Mission 3

Continentality

  • Continentality: distance from the sea. Moist air loses water as it travels inland, and the interior has little water to evaporate.
  • Midlatitude deserts lie between 30° and 50°, "in interior drainage basins far from oceans".
  • They have "a wide range of annual temperatures": land heats and cools faster than sea.
  • Examples: the Sonoran Desert of North America; the Tengger Desert in China.
Mission 3

Relief: the rain shadow

  • Air forced over mountains "expands and cools", and moisture falls as rain or snow on the windward slopes.
  • On the leeward side the air descends and "becomes warmer and drier … after it is compressed".
  • "Dry foehn conditions are responsible for the occurrence of rain shadows in the lee of mountains".
  • Death Valley: four mountain ranges lie between it and the ocean, "each one adding to an increasingly drier rainshadow"; average rainfall is under 5 cm a year.
Quick check
?Moist westerly winds cross a north–south mountain range. Where is a rain-shadow desert most likely?
Mission 3

Cold ocean currents

  • Coastal deserts lie "on the western edges of continents near the Tropics" and are "affected by cold ocean currents that parallel the coast".
  • Along-shore winds drive upwelling: "deep, cold water rises toward the surface".
  • Air over cold water is cooled from below. It forms fog, "common at sea when moist tropical air moves over cooler waters", but cold, dense air near the surface does not rise, so rain is rare.
  • "Winter fogs, produced by upwelling cold currents, frequently blanket coastal deserts".
  • Atacama: measurable rain (1 mm or more) "may occur as infrequently as once every 5-20 years".
Mission 3

Causes combine

  • Atacama, which USGS calls "the Earth's driest desert": subtropical high + cold current offshore + the Andes to the east, so neither ocean supplies rain.
  • Death Valley: subtropical latitude + rain shadow of four ranges + deep basin, 86 m below sea level.
  • Sahara: subtropical high + dry trade winds + vast size, so much of it is far from the sea.
  • Exam move (AQA 9-mark "Explain"): name each cause, give the process (sinking, drying, cooling from below), and say why it matters in this desert.
Quick check
?A desert lies 2,000 km from the nearest sea, in a basin between 40° and 45° N, with hot summers and very cold winters. Which cause is most likely to dominate?
Mission check

?The Namib Desert runs along the west coast of southern Africa, near the Tropic of Capricorn. It is often foggy, yet almost rainless. Which explanation fits best?

Coastal deserts such as the Namib and Atacama combine subtropical high pressure with a cold current. Air cooled from below forms fog but does not rise, so rain is rare even beside the ocean.

Mission 4

Energy in a hot desert

  • Insolation: clear skies let strong sunshine reach bare rock by day; heat escapes fast at night.
  • On 15 July 1972 the ground at Furnace Creek, Death Valley, reached about 94 °C (201 °F); the air, about 53 °C (128 °F).
  • Wind moves loose sand and dust. Runoff is rare but powerful.
Mission 4

Five processes, one sediment system

  • Weathering breaks rock down; mass movement brings it downslope.
  • Erosion, transport and deposition by wind and water move it on.
  • Result: sand covers only about 20% of deserts; almost half are stony plains where wind has removed the fine particles.
Mission 4

Thermal fracture

  • Rock is a poor conductor: the surface heats and cools faster than the inside, setting up stress.
  • Thermal fracture: repeated stress cracks the rock.
  • Field evidence: many desert boulders show fine, near-vertical cracks. At eight sites in the south-western USA, one type was "preferentially aligned north-south".
  • The authors linked these cracks to stresses that follow the Sun's daily path.
Mission 4

Exfoliation

  • Exfoliation: curved sheets of rock peel off parallel to the surface, like layers of an onion.
  • Daily heating and cooling may stress the outer layer most; dew or rare rain helps loosen it.
  • Rounded boulders result. Large rock domes peel in sheets mainly by pressure release, not daily heating.
Mission 4

Block and granular disintegration

  • Block disintegration: jointed rock breaks apart along its joints into blocks.
  • Granular disintegration: in coarse-grained rocks such as granite, dark and light minerals expand by different amounts when heated. Grains loosen and fall away, leaving sandy debris.
  • Salt weathering: as water evaporates, salt crystals grow in pores and force grains apart.
Mission 4

Chemical weathering

  • Water is scarce, so chemical weathering is slow. Dew and rare rain still drive it.
  • Solution: at Arches National Park, Utah, rain "can slowly dissolve the calcite bonding the sand together".
  • Oxidation: iron minerals react with oxygen and turn red and orange.
  • Desert varnish: a dark coat of "Manganese, iron oxides, hydroxides, and clay minerals" on long-exposed rock.
Quick check
?A granite boulder in a desert is surrounded by loose, coarse grains of the same minerals, and its surface crumbles under a finger. Which process is most likely?
Mission 4

Mass movement

  • Weathered blocks fall from cliffs (rockfall) and build steep slopes of debris below.
  • With little soil or vegetation to bind it, debris also slides and creeps downslope.
  • After rare storms, saturated debris can surge downslope as a flow.
Mission 4

Wind erosion

  • Deflation: wind removes loose, fine particles.
  • Abrasion: windborne grains grind and sand-blast surfaces.
  • Saltating sand rarely rises more than 1 cm above the ground, so abrasion works hardest close to the surface.
Mission 4

Wind transport

  • Suspension: typical winds hold particles under 0.2 mm aloft (USGS).
  • Saltation: sand moves in hops "no more than one centimeter above the ground", at one-half to one-third of the wind's speed.
  • Surface creep: grains too big to hop are pushed along by impacts; up to 25% of grain movement.
Quick check
?A 10 m/s wind is moving sand by saltation. Roughly how fast do the hopping grains travel?
Mission 4

Deposition

  • Wind drops its load when it slows: in the lee of obstacles, in hollows, and where it loses energy.
  • Sand sheets (flat, sandy surfaces) make up about 40% of wind-built surfaces; the Selima Sand Sheet in Egypt and Sudan covers 60,000 km².
  • Dust in suspension can travel far beyond the desert before it settles.
  • Dunes come in the next mission.
Mission 4

Water: rare but powerful

  • The USGS: "water is much more powerful than wind".
  • Desert floods carry heavy loads of sediment, often "for a day or two".
  • So weathering supplies debris, and both wind and water shape the surface.
Mission check

?A broad desert plain is covered by a tight layer of angular stones, cracked and darkly varnished, with almost no loose sand. Which processes best explain it?

Almost half of desert surfaces are stony deflation zones. Heat, salt and water weather the rock; wind deflates the fines; rare floods, "much more powerful than wind", move the rest.

Mission 5

Wind as a sculptor

  • Deflation removes loose, fine particles; abrasion grinds and sand-blasts surfaces with windborne grains.
  • Saltating sand is normally lifted no more than 1 cm above the ground, so abrasion is strongest low down.
  • When the wind slows, it drops sand: dunes and sand sheets.
Mission 5

Deflation hollows

  • Deflation hollows (blowouts) are "hollows formed by the removal of particles by wind".
  • They are usually small, but can be several kilometres across.
  • Deflation deepens a hollow until it reaches material the wind cannot lift: coarse stones, hard rock, or damp ground near the water table.
Mission 5

Desert pavements

  • Desert pavement: "a sheetlike surface of rock fragments that remains after wind and water have removed the fine particles".
  • The stones then protect the material beneath from further deflation.
  • Another explanation: stones crack in the Sun's heat, and wind-blown dust builds up beneath them, creating most pavements over millennia.
Quick check
?Why does deflation slow almost to a stop once a desert pavement has formed?
Mission 5

Ventifacts

  • Ventifacts: rocks "cut, and sometimes polished, by the abrasive action of wind".
  • Faces cut by sand from the prevailing wind are flat and smooth, meeting in sharp edges.
  • If the wind changes, or the stone is turned, new faces are cut.
Mission 5

Yardangs

  • Yardangs: streamlined ridges "up to tens of meters high and kilometers long", shaped by desert winds.
  • Abrasion and deflation cut down the softer rock between ridges, parallel to the wind.
  • The blunt, steep end faces upwind; the ridge tapers downwind.
  • The yardangs of Iran's Lut Desert are among the largest on Earth, with almost 100 m of relief.
  • The USGS notes that the Great Sphinx at Giza "may be a modified yardang".
Mission 5

Zeugen

  • Zeugen: tabular ridges where a hard, flat-lying rock layer caps softer rock.
  • Weathering opens joints in the cap; abrasion, strongest near the ground, undercuts the soft layer beneath.
  • Yardangs are streamlined along the wind; zeugen are controlled by the rock layers.
Quick check
?Flat-topped ridges in a desert each have a hard, horizontal cap layer over softer, undercut rock beneath. What are they?
Mission 5

Barchans

  • Barchans: crescent dunes that form where wind blows mainly from one direction.
  • Sand climbs the gentle windward slope and avalanches down the steep slipface. The horns, with less sand, move faster and point downwind.
  • A group in China's Ningxia Province moved more than 100 m a year between 1954 and 1959.
Mission 5

Seif (linear) dunes

  • Seif (linear) dunes are long, sharp-crested ridges. Many form where winds blow from two directions.
  • They may be more than 160 km long.
  • Sand moves along the ridge, so seifs grow in length rather than migrating sideways.
Quick check
?A barchan's horns point south-west. Which way does the wind mainly blow?
Mission 5

Process, time and landscape

  • Seconds: a gust lifts sand into saltation. Years: some barchans in Ningxia moved more than 100 m a year.
  • Millennia: pavements form; yardangs are cut.
  • Glacial cycles: about 18,000 years ago, sand seas covered almost 50% of the land between 30° N and 30° S; now about 10%.
  • Desert landscapes differ: sand seas (dunes), stony plains (pavements, deflation hollows) and rocky uplands (ventifacts, yardangs, zeugen).
Mission check

?In a desert basin, dozens of parallel ridges are cut in soft old lake beds. Each has a blunt, steep end facing north and tapers southwards. What shaped them?

Yardangs are streamlined ridges shaped by desert winds; the USGS notes that "The famous sphinx at Giza in Egypt may be a modified yardang".

Mission 6

Three sources of desert water

  • Exogenous (exotic) rivers rise outside the desert and cross it: the Nile, the Colorado and the Yellow.
  • Endoreic drainage (also spelt endorheic): water flows inland to a basin with no outlet to the sea. Most deserts lie in such basins.
  • Ephemeral streams flow only for a short time after rain or snowmelt.
  • Exogenous rivers lose water on the way, by seepage and evaporation.
Mission 6

An endoreic basin: Death Valley, California

  • Badwater Basin is an enclosed basin that "doesn't drain to the sea".
  • Floods come to rest at the lowest point and form temporary lakes; as the water evaporates, "only the salts remain".
  • The salt flats cover about 520 km² (200 square miles).
Quick check
?A desert river rises in mountains 500 km away, flows all year, and loses water as it crosses the desert. What type of source is it?
Mission 6

Water arrives in episodes

  • Rain is rare but can be intense: a record 44 mm fell in 3 hours in the Sahara; large Saharan storms can deliver up to 1 mm a minute.
  • Death Valley, 5 August 2022: about 43 mm (1.70 inches) fell at Furnace Creek, three-quarters of its 2.20-inch average annual rainfall, in a few hours.
  • Ephemeral streams then carry "considerable quantities of sediment for a day or two".
  • This is the episodic role of water: long quiet spells, then short events that do most of the work.
Mission 6

Why desert rain runs off

  • Little vegetation: little interception, and few roots to open the soil.
  • Bare, crusted or rocky surfaces have low infiltration capacity.
  • Intense rain exceeds that capacity: infiltration-excess (Hortonian) overland flow.
  • So a small amount of rain can make a large, fast flood.
Mission 6

Sheet flooding and channel flash flooding

  • Sheet flooding: "extensive, shallow" unchannelled flow; thin sheets of water "can cover large areas".
  • Channel flash flooding: a short-lived flood with a short time to peak, confined to a channel.
  • On fans, sheetfloods spread over the gentle lower slopes; higher up, flow is confined to steep channels, where velocities are high.
  • There is "no sharp line" between the two.
Quick check
?A storm drops 40 mm in two hours on a bare desert slope. Where would you expect sheet flooding rather than channel flash flooding?
Mission 6

Wadis, alluvial fans and bahadas

  • Wadis (arroyos, washes): dry stream beds that fill during storms; flash floods are common after thunderstorms.
  • Alluvial fan: a fan-shaped pile of sediment where a fast mountain stream enters a flat valley and slows down.
  • Bahada (bajada): neighbouring fans merge into an apron of debris that spreads towards the valley floor.
  • Flow paths on fans can switch suddenly (avulsion), so new channels form in each flood.
Mission 6

Pediments and inselbergs

  • Pediment: a gently sloping, near-bedrock surface at the foot of a receding mountain front, left as erosion removes the mountain's mass.
  • Inselberg: an isolated, steep-sided hill rising abruptly from a pediment; a remnant of resistant rock.
  • Uluru, central Australia, is an inselberg of arkose sandstone; later work found it formed as the surrounding plains were lowered.
  • How pediments are cut (sheet flow, streams, slope retreat) is still debated: say which explanation you use.
Mission 6

Playas: landforms of a wetter past

  • Playa: the flat floor of a basin with no outlet. After storms it holds a shallow, short-lived lake that quickly evaporates; among the flattest landforms on Earth.
  • Most playas are relics of large lakes from the last Ice Age, about 12,000 years ago.
  • Death Valley, 2023–24: remnants of Hurricane Hilary brought 2.2 inches on 20 August; with later storms, 4.9 inches fell in six months, and a lake about one foot deep returned to Badwater Basin.
Quick check
?Which landform is erosional, made of bedrock, and found at the foot of a mountain front?
Mission 6

Process, time and landscape

  • Process: weathering prepares debris; episodic floods move it; wind sorts the fines (USGS: "water is much more powerful than wind").
  • Time: one storm can reshape a wadi in hours; pediments and inselbergs form over far longer spans as whole mountain fronts retreat and plains are lowered; many playas record the last Ice Age.
  • Landscape: from mountain to basin, a typical sequence is mountain front → pediment (with inselbergs) → bahada → playa.
  • Some landforms are relict: made when the climate was wetter, now only modified.
  • Exam move (AQA "Assess"): link each landform to a process and a timescale, then weigh active against relict.
Mission check

?A desert basin has no outlet. Around its edges, merged fans spread from the mountains; below them is a salt flat. It rains about 50 mm a year. What has done most to shape this landscape?

Most of a desert is not sand. Episodic water, rare but powerful, cuts wadis and pediments and builds fans, bahadas and playas, while some forms are relict from wetter times.

Mission 7

Mission 7: Between 11,000 and 5,000 years ago, the Sahara had permanent lakes, plants and people. Today it is desert. Did people cause the change?

Make a guess: Between 11,000 and 5,000 years ago, the Sahara had permanent lakes, plants and people. Today it is desert. Did people cause the change?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

  • 3.1.2.4 changing extent and distribution of hot deserts over the last 10,000 years
  • 3.1.2.4 desertification: causes (climate change, human impact), areas at risk, impacts on ecosystems, landscapes and populations
  • 3.1.2.4 predicted climate change and impacts on drylands; alternative possible futures for local populations
Mission 7

What desertification is

  • IPCC: "Desertification is land degradation in arid, semi-arid, and dry sub-humid areas, collectively known as drylands, resulting from many factors, including human activities and climatic variations".
  • It means drylands losing productivity (soil, water, vegetation), not simply sand dunes advancing.
  • Its "range and intensity … have increased in some dryland areas over the past several decades (high confidence)".
Mission 7

The Green Sahara and its end

  • From 11,000 to 5,000 years ago, the Sahara had high rainfall, varied vegetation, permanent lakes and people; wet conditions reached 31° N. This is the African Humid Period.
  • It was paced by slow changes in Earth's orbit (precession), which strengthened the summer monsoon and pushed its rains north.
  • As that forcing faded, the monsoon retreated. The high rainfall is "best explained by strong vegetation and dust feedbacks"; the IPCC adds that a cooling of the north Atlantic played a role.
  • Feedback: fewer plants → brighter, dustier land → less rain → fewer plants.
Mission 7

Deserts over the last 10,000 years

  • Deserts have expanded and contracted naturally. During the last 12,500 years, "parts of the deserts were more arid than they are today".
  • The Sahara reached its smallest extent in the early to mid-Holocene, then expanded as the Green Sahara ended about 5,000 years ago.
  • Exam move (AQA): describe change in extent (how much) and distribution (where), with dates, and give the natural driver.
Mission 7

Case study: the Sahel

  • Where: the semi-arid belt along the southern edge of the Sahara, from Senegal to Sudan.
  • Ecosystem: grassland and dry savanna, with scattered trees; used for pastoralism and rain-fed crops (millet, sorghum).
  • Climate: a short summer rainy season brought by the West African monsoon. Summer rainfall was high in the 1950s and 1960s, fell sharply in the 1970s and 1980s (the driest year in the index was 1984) and has partly recovered since.
  • Hydrology: Lake Chad shrank to less than 2,000 km² in the 1980s, a fall of more than 90%. Since 2001 its southern pool has slightly increased, and the authors conclude that "for now Lake Chad is not disappearing".
Mission 7

Human causes

  • Croplands in the West African Sahel "have doubled since 1975, with settlement area increasing by about 150%".
  • Overgrazing reduced the seed bank of annual plants, leaving them more vulnerable to climate change.
  • IPCC: the main human drivers interacting with climate change are "expansion of croplands, unsustainable land management practices and increased pressure on land from population and income growth".
  • Bare soil loses more water to runoff and more soil to wind, so plants struggle to return: positive feedback.
Mission 7

Is the Sahel still desertifying?

  • IPCC: "wetting and greening conditions have been observed in this region over the last three decades".
  • One analysis found climate the dominant cause of greening in 1982–2003, with pockets of desertification in Nigeria and Sudan.
  • Studies disagree because they use different indicators, places and periods. The relative roles of climate and people "are location-specific and evolve over time (high confidence)".
Quick check
?Which conclusion best fits the IPCC's assessment of desertification in the Sahel?
Mission 7

Impacts on ecosystems, landscapes and people

  • Ecosystems: desertification has "contributed to the loss of biodiversity in some dryland regions (medium confidence)", and invasive plants have reduced ecosystem services.
  • Landscapes: soil erosion, more dust storms, and sand and dune movement that damages roads and infrastructure.
  • Food and people: the 1970s–80s Sahel droughts contributed to "hunger, out-migration and suffering for millions of pastoralists". Desertification "has already reduced agricultural productivity and incomes (high confidence)".
  • Food risk: the IPCC projects that desertification and changing climate will reduce crop and livestock productivity (high confidence).
Quick check
?Why does desertification raise food risk for households in the Sahel?
Mission 7

Areas at risk

  • Desertification hotspots (falling vegetation productivity, 1980s–2000s) covered about 9.2% of drylands and affected about 500 million people in 2015.
  • Most people affected are in South and East Asia, the circum-Sahara region including North Africa, and the Middle East including the Arabian Peninsula (low confidence).
  • Risk is highest where dryland populations are poor: poverty limits both adaptation and investment in sustainable land management.
Mission 7

Predicted change and alternative futures

  • Risks from desertification "are projected to increase due to climate change (high confidence)".
  • Under a middle-of-the-road pathway (SSP2), the dryland population exposed to water stress, drought and habitat degradation is projected to reach 951 million at 1.5 °C, 1,152 million at 2 °C and 1,285 million at 3 °C.
  • At 2 °C, a sustainability pathway (SSP1) gives 974 million exposed; a fragmented-world pathway (SSP3) gives 1,267 million.
  • Rainfall projections for the Sahel are uncertain: one study cited by the IPCC finds increases of up to 300% in Central Sahel rainfall by 2100, through a stronger monsoon.
Quick check
?Using the figures just taught: at 2 °C of warming, how many more dryland people are projected to be exposed under SSP3 than under SSP1?
Mission 7

Futures for local people

  • Adapt in place: rainwater harvesting such as zaï pits and stone bunds raises dryland yields.
  • Keep mobility: resilience is higher "with flexible tenure for allowing mobility for pastoralist communities".
  • Restore: the African Union's Great Green Wall aims to restore 100 million ha by 2030; about 4 million ha were restored in its intervention areas in 2011–2017.
  • Move: where pressures combine, out-migration continues; the IPCC notes that strong climate effects on dryland migration are disputed.
  • The SSP numbers show that choices about development change how many people are exposed, not only how much the climate warms.
Mission check

?About 18,000 years ago, sand seas covered almost 50% of the land between 30° N and 30° S; today they cover about 10%. Did people cause that change?

Deserts grow and shrink with natural climate change: the ice-age sand seas shrank, and the Green Sahara ended about 5,000 years ago through orbital change in the monsoon, amplified by feedbacks. Today, in the Sahel, climate and people both drive change, in proportions that vary by place.

Mission 8

Mission 8: In the Namib, a river that flows only every few years stops a sea of giant dunes. How can that be?

Make a guess: In the Namib, a river that flows only every few years stops a sea of giant dunes. How can that be?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

  • 3.1.2.3 relationship between process, time, landforms and landscapes in mid/low-latitude deserts; characteristic desert landscapes
  • 3.1.2.6 case study of a hot desert setting illustrating processes and landforms and engaging with field data (aeolian field data may come from coastal dunes)
Mission 8

The Namib: a low-latitude coastal desert

  • A cool coastal desert running about 1,900 km along the Atlantic coast of Africa.
  • Coastal deserts lie "on the western edges of continents near the Tropics of Cancer and Capricorn".
  • Namib Sand Sea: a World Heritage Site of 3,077,700 hectares.
  • Study area: the central Namib around the Kuiseb River and Gobabeb research station.
Mission 8

Physical factor 1: a cold-current climate

  • "The cold Benguela Current flows northward along the coast, chilling the air above it and thus producing fog".
  • Cool air under hot, dry air: an inversion, so rain clouds rarely form.
  • "Fog is the primary source of water" in the sand sea.
  • Rainfall: about 13 mm a year at the coast, 51 mm at the foot of the escarpment; "in some years … no rain falls at all".
Mission 8

Physical factor 2: sand supply and wind

  • Sediment comes "from the hinterland", carried "by river, ocean current and wind" over "thousands of kilometres".
  • The main sand source is the Orange River at the desert's southern edge.
  • Dunes are shaped by "pronounced daily and seasonal changes in dominant wind directions".
  • Geology and time: the area "has been arid for at least 55 million years".
Mission 8

Aeolian landforms: the sand sea

  • The sand sea "includes most known types of dunes".
  • Dunes reach 60–240 m and "run in lines from north-northwest to south-southeast": linear (seif) dunes.
  • Near the southern coast: "rapidly moving crescent-shaped barchans".
  • Two generations: "an ancient semi-consolidated one overlain by a younger active one".
Mission 8

Fluvial landforms: ephemeral rivers

  • Ephemeral rivers flow only after heavy rain inland; "they normally flow for no more than a few days in several years".
  • The Kuiseb has water below its dry bed: its underflow supplies Walvis Bay and Swakopmund.
  • South of the Kuiseb, "every stream terminates in a vlei" (a clay pan, the Namib's playa).
  • North: gravel plains and rock platforms, with isolated mountains rising steeply above them (inselbergs).
Quick check
?Why does the Namib get fog on many days but very little rain?
Mission 8

Landforms interact: the Kuiseb edge

  • Around Gobabeb: "the Sand Sea to the south, the Gravel Plains to the north", with the Kuiseb's riparian woodland between.
  • WWF: the Kuiseb "and the vegetation within it acts as a windbreak to the southerly winds".
  • Rare floods run down the channel for days.
  • Inference from these facts: wind moves sand north; trees slow it; occasional floods carry away sand that reaches the channel. The edge is a balance of wind and water.
Quick check
?Which pair of processes best explains why the sand sea stops at the Kuiseb?
Mission 8

Field data: a coast-to-inland transect

  • Fog reaches "as far as 50 km" inland.
  • Pattern: fog falls and rain rises inland. Gobabeb lies on "the ecotone between fog and rain precipitation".
Mission 8

Process, time, landform

  • Fast, local: barchans move rapidly near the coast.
  • Rare, powerful: floods for a few days in several years cut and clear channels.
  • Slow, regional: sand stays in the sand sea for at least a million years.
  • The landscape is "continuously refreshed and maintained by wholly natural processes".
Quick check
?On the coast-to-inland transect, rainfall at the escarpment foot is 51 mm and at the coast 13 mm. How many times wetter is the escarpment foot?
Mission 8

Exam move: one landscape system

  • Inputs: sand (Orange River), fog and rare rain, wind energy.
  • Stores: the sand sea; water under the Kuiseb's bed.
  • Outputs and edges: rivers ending in vleis; the Kuiseb edge.
  • Link each landform to its process and its time scale: that is AQA's "process, time, landform".
Mission check

?South of the Kuiseb, rivers end in clay pans among the dunes and do not reach the sea. What does this show?

At the Kuiseb, rare floods and a band of trees hold the edge against the wind. Further south, the dunes win: rivers die in vleis. One landscape, one contest between wind and water, two outcomes.

Mission 9

Mission 9: Farmers turned bare, crusted ground back into fields and forest. Will it hold in a hotter future?

Make a guess: Farmers turned bare, crusted ground back into fields and forest. Will it hold in a hotter future?

Specification: what this mission covers

AQA A-level Geography (7037)

  • 3.1.2.6 case study of a local-scale desertified landscape; causes, impacts, sustainable development; evaluate resilience, mitigation and adaptation
  • 3.1.2.4 predicted climate change and impacts on drylands; alternative possible futures for local populations
Mission 9

The place: a farm-scale case

  • Northern Central Plateau, Burkina Faso: rocky plateaus, unproductive soils, unreliable rainfall.
  • About 50% of Burkina Faso's rural population lives in this region.
  • Our local case: the fields of farmer Ali Ouédraogo, west of the town of Gourcy, and the forest of Yacouba Sawadogo, near Ouahigouya.
  • Desertification = "land degradation in arid, semi-arid, and dry sub-humid areas... resulting from many factors, including human activities and climatic variations" (IPCC).
Mission 9

Cause 1: drought

  • Sahel summer rainfall index, decade means: 1950s +1239, 1960s +741, 1970s −18, 1980s −229 (index units).
  • Driest year in the record: 1984.
  • The atlas: "The major droughts of the 1970s and 1980s exacerbated the difficult living conditions of rural people".
  • Climatic driver: less rain → less plant cover → bare soil exposed to sun, wind and runoff.
Mission 9

Cause 2: people and land pressure

  • Burkina Faso's population: just over 6.1 million (1975) → 17 million (2013).
  • Rain-fed cropland: 15% of the country (1975) → 39% (2013), "an overall increase of 160 percent".
  • Natural land cover: 82.5% (1975) → 57.4% (2013).
  • IPCC: main human drivers are "expansion of croplands, unsustainable land management practices and increased pressure on land from population and income growth".
  • On the plateau itself, poor soils and rainfall kept cropland growth small, yet population density roughly doubled in the 30 years after the last great drought.
Mission 9

Impacts on landscape and people

  • Landscape: in 1984, the land west of Gourcy was "barren, laterite" surface; Yacouba Sawadogo's land was "a barren plateau" in 1979.
  • Ecosystem: few trees; rain ran off instead of soaking in.
  • People: the droughts compelled "many farm families to leave their villages and to settle in regions of higher rainfall to the south".
  • IPCC: desertification "has already reduced agricultural productivity and incomes (high confidence)".
Quick check
?Which statement best fits the evidence for the northern Central Plateau?
Mission 9

Responses: stones, pits and trees

  • Stone bunds (rock lines along contours): Ali Ouédraogo learned to build them through the Oxfam-funded Agroforestry Project, starting in 1983.
  • Zaï planting pits: "the revival of an old practice"; seeds placed in thousands of small pits.
  • The IPCC defines zaï as "small basins used to capture surface runoff".
  • Farmer-managed natural regeneration: trees grew along the bunds from seeds carried by runoff; he protected them, and from 1986 encouraged them.
  • Rock lines "trap soil and seeds, and enhance water infiltration".
Mission 9

Outcomes and sustainable development

  • By 2010, 26 years on, the barren land was "productive cropland accompanied by a diverse tree parkland".
  • Across the region, such practices have rehabilitated 2,000–3,000 km² and produce 80,000 tonnes of extra food a year (Reij, Tappan and Smale 2009, cited in the atlas).
  • Sustainable development: low-cost, local skills, using local materials; food now without exhausting the land for the future.
  • IPCC: rainwater harvesting raises dryland farm output.
Mission 9

Evaluate: resilience

  • Resilience = ability to absorb a shock (a dry year) and recover.
  • Strengths: more water stays in the soil; zaï helped give "good yields of millet, sorghum and cowpea"; protected trees add products and shade.
  • IPCC: integrated crop, soil and water measures "increase the resilience of agricultural production systems" (high confidence).
  • Limits: the IPCC finds poverty limits both the capacity to adapt and money "to invest in sustainable land management" (high confidence). Building bunds and pits is heavy labour.
Quick check
?The rock lines west of Gourcy are best classed as which kind of response?
Mission 9

Evaluate: mitigation and adaptation

  • Mitigation (two senses): reducing the causes of desertification (runoff, bare soil), and reducing greenhouse gases.
  • IPCC: "Agroforestry practices and shelterbelts help reduce soil erosion and sequester carbon".
  • Adaptation: changing practice to live with dryness. The IPCC says sustainable land management practices "contribute to climate change adaptation with mitigation co-benefits (high confidence)".
  • Other adaptation: migration. IPCC: households responded through migration "during droughts in Burkina Faso in the 2000s".
  • Judgement: strong locally; scale depends on many farmers adopting, which labour and poverty constrain.
  • Exam move (AQA "Evaluate", 20 marks): weigh each response type with this place's evidence, then reach a supported overall view.
Mission 9

Predicted climate change and drylands

  • IPCC: "Climate change will exacerbate several desertification processes (medium confidence)".
  • Dryland population exposed (vulnerable) to water stress, drought and habitat degradation (SSP2): 951 m (178 m) at 1.5 °C; 1,152 m (220 m) at 2 °C; 1,285 m (277 m) at 3 °C.
  • "Desertification and changing climate are projected to cause reductions in crop and livestock productivity (high confidence)".
  • Uncertain rain: one study cited by the IPCC projects up to 300% more Central Sahel rainfall by 2100. Projections disagree.
Mission 9

Alternative futures for local people

  • Future A — restore and adapt: bunds, zaï and protected trees spread; yields and tree cover hold even in dry years.
  • Future B — degrade and leave: hotter, more variable rain; labour and money run short; land is abandoned and young people migrate.
  • Future C — wetter but riskier: more total rain, but heavier storms increase runoff; bunds become more valuable.
  • Which future happens depends on climate and choices (tenure, investment, labour) — the IPCC rates the roles of climate and people as "location-specific" (high confidence).
Mission check

?A district elsewhere in the Sahel is planning for 2050. It has the same soils as the Central Plateau. Which plan is best supported by the Burkina Faso evidence and the IPCC?

Local restoration west of Gourcy shows adaptation with mitigation co-benefits can reverse degradation. Its future depends on a changing climate and on whether poor farmers can afford the labour and investment.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Hot deserts as open systems: energy inputs (insolation, wind, rare intense runoff) drive weathering, mass movement, erosion, transport and deposition.
  • Sediment cells link sources (rock, rivers, dust, coast) through transfers to sinks (dunes, playas, fans, loess); budget = inputs − outputs; landforms combine into landscapes.

Mission 2

  • A desert is defined by dryness: under 250 mm a year, or an aridity index (P ÷ PET) below 0.20 for arid land.
  • Hot deserts lie under subtropical high pressure, beside cold currents, in continental interiors and in rain shadows; their soils, plants and water deficit interact.

Mission 3

  • Aridity comes from sinking air (subtropical highs), dry winds (trades), distance from the sea (continentality), descent in the lee of mountains (rain shadow) and air cooled from below over cold currents.
  • Most deserts have more than one cause; the exam answer names the process for each.

Mission 4

  • Arid weathering: thermal fracture, exfoliation, block and granular disintegration, salt, and slow chemical weathering.
  • Wind erodes by deflation and abrasion and moves grains by suspension, saltation and creep.

Mission 5

  • Deflation: hollows and pavements. Abrasion: ventifacts, yardangs, zeugen.
  • Barchans form under one wind; seifs under two. Landforms change on time-scales from seconds to glacial cycles.

Mission 6

  • Water in deserts is exogenous, endoreic or ephemeral, and arrives in episodes: sheet floods on open slopes, flash floods in channels.
  • From mountain to basin: pediment and inselberg (erosional), bahada and playa (depositional); some are active, some relict.

Mission 7

  • Desertification is land degradation in drylands, caused by climatic variation and human activity together.
  • The Sahara greened and dried naturally; in today's Sahel, cropland expansion, overgrazing and population pressure interact with rainfall change.
  • Risk is projected to grow, but by how much depends on the development pathway; local futures include adapting, restoring, keeping mobility or moving.

Mission 8

  • The Namib is a low-latitude coastal desert: a cold current gives fog, not rain (about 13 mm of rain a year at the coast); sand from the Orange River has stayed for at least a million years.
  • Landforms interact: dunes, ephemeral rivers, vleis, gravel plains and inselbergs; at the Kuiseb water and trees hold back the sand, while further south dunes block the rivers.

Mission 9

  • Gourcy and Ouahigouya, Burkina Faso: drought (1970s–80s) and land pressure on poor soils left bare laterite; families moved south. Stone bunds, zaï and protected trees (from 1983) restored fields and tree parkland.
  • Climate change will worsen several desertification processes (medium confidence); local futures range from restoration to abandonment, depending on climate and on investment, tenure and labour.
