Pride and Prejudice · structure and ironyScreen 5

Kinds of irony

  • Verbal irony: words mean more than, or the opposite of, what they say. The grand "truth" is local gossip.
  • Narrative irony: a calm narrator quietly mocks: "The business of her life was to get her daughters married" (Vol I ch 1).
  • Dramatic irony: we know more than a character. After Darcy's letter, we know Wickham's past; Meryton doesn't.
  • Structural irony: "the last man in the world" Elizabeth would marry is the man she marries.

Mission 2

Elizabeth trusts her judgement of people. Should we trust it too?

Make a guess: Elizabeth trusts her judgement of people. Should we trust it too?

Pride and Prejudice · ElizabethScreen 1

A lively, independent heroine

  • Her father says "Lizzy has something more of quickness than her sisters" (Vol I ch 1).
  • Insulted by Darcy, she tells the story "with great spirit": she "delighted in anything ridiculous" (Vol I ch 3).
  • She walks three miles alone to nurse Jane and arrives with "dirty stockings" (Vol I ch 7): energy over decorum.
  • She refuses two proposals: Mr Collins (Vol I ch 19) and Darcy (Vol II ch 11).
  • Austen presents her as witty, independent and quick to judge.

Two ways to read Elizabeth

  • Many readers see her as independent: she refuses security (Collins) and status (Darcy) when they come without respect.
  • Others stress her limits: Darcy's letter, not her own insight, corrects her, and her happy ending is still marriage, the only future the novel offers her.

Mission checkScreen 9

?Elizabeth trusts her judgement of people. Should we trust it too?

Trust her, but not completely. Elizabeth is quick and often right, yet her pride in her own "discernment" makes her misjudge Darcy and Wickham. Because the narrator stays close to her, we share those mistakes, and so share her shock when she says "I never knew myself".

Mission 3

Darcy insults Elizabeth at their first ball. Who changes?

Make a guess: Darcy insults Elizabeth at their first ball. Who changes?

Pride and Prejudice · DarcyScreen 1

First impressions of Darcy

  • Darcy owns Pemberley in Derbyshire and has "ten thousand a year" (Vol I ch 3): about twice Bingley's income.
  • At the Meryton assembly he is admired "for about half the evening", until "he was discovered to be proud" (Vol I ch 3).
  • Within Elizabeth's hearing, he calls her "tolerable" and refuses to dance.
  • The irony: the room's verdict flips with his manners, while his fortune stays the same.

Pride and Prejudice · DarcyScreen 5

How Darcy is revealed

  • The letter (Vol II ch 12): he explains why he separated Bingley and Jane, and tells the truth about Wickham.
  • Pemberley (Vol III ch 1): his housekeeper says, "I have never had a cross word from him in my life".
  • Lydia (Vol III ch 10): Mrs Gardiner's letter reveals that Darcy paid Wickham's debts and arranged the marriage.
  • Austen reveals him through others: letters, servants, relatives. We learn him as Elizabeth does.

Mission 4

Charlotte marries a man she can't respect. Foolish or wise?

Make a guess: Charlotte marries a man she can't respect. Foolish or wise?

Pride and Prejudice · marriage and moneyScreen 1

Everyone has a price

CharacterMoney, as the novel states it
Darcy£10,000 a year (Vol I ch 3)
Bingley£4,000–5,000 a year (Vol I ch 1)
Mr Bennet£2,000 a year, from an entailed estate (Vol I ch 7)
Elizabeth£1,000 "in the 4 per cents", only after her mother's death (Vol I ch 19)
Georgiana Darcy£30,000 (Vol II ch 12)
  • A gentleman's income came from land or invested money, counted "a year". £1,000 at 4 per cent earns about £40 a year.
  • News of Darcy's income spreads "within five minutes" of his arrival (Vol I ch 3).
Pride and Prejudice · marriage and moneyScreen 2

The entail and women's position

  • Longbourn is entailed "in default of heirs male" (Vol I ch 7): it must pass to a male heir, their distant cousin Mr Collins. When Mr Bennet dies, his wife and daughters lose their home.
  • Gentlemen's daughters were not expected to earn; marriage was their main route to security.
  • Under coverture, a wife's property largely came under her husband's control. The Married Women's Property Acts came only in 1870 and 1882.
  • This helps explain Mrs Bennet's panic and Charlotte's choice.

Pride and Prejudice · marriage and moneyScreen 6

How to read Charlotte

  • Elizabeth is shocked that her friend has "sacrificed every better feeling to worldly advantage" (Vol I ch 22).
  • Charlotte's own view: "I am not romantic, you know. I never was. I ask only a comfortable home" (Vol I ch 22).
  • At Hunsford she "had chosen it with her eyes open", and her home and "her poultry" keep their charms (Vol II ch 15).
  • Some readers see a warning; others see Austen's sympathy for a woman of twenty-seven with no other prospects.

?Charlotte marries a man she can't respect. Foolish or wise?

The novel doesn't give a single verdict. Charlotte's reasoning makes sense for a woman with "small fortune" and an uncertain future, and Austen lets her explain it. But Elizabeth's shock, and her own refusals of Collins and Darcy, show what such a marriage gives up. Argue both, with evidence.

Pride and vanity

  • Mary Bennet: "Pride relates more to our opinion of ourselves; vanity to what we would have others think of us" (Vol I ch 5).
  • Darcy's pride: he thinks Meryton beneath him. Elizabeth's pride: "I could easily forgive his pride, if he had not mortified mine" (Vol I ch 5).

  • Austen puts a useful definition in the mouth of Mary, whom the narrator mocks: wisdom from a comic source.

Mission checkScreen 8

?Lady Catherine says Elizabeth isn't good enough. Does the novel agree?

No, but not because the novel abolishes class. Austen's irony exposes snobbery (the Bingley sisters' forgotten trade money, Lady Catherine's rudeness), and good character crosses ranks (the Gardiners). Elizabeth still wins her argument as "a gentleman's daughter": the novel criticises pride in rank more than rank itself.

Mission 6

Lydia runs off with Wickham. Who is to blame?

Make a guess: Lydia runs off with Wickham. Who is to blame?

Pride and Prejudice · the Bennets and LydiaScreen 1

Five daughters, two parents

  • Mr Bennet: "sarcastic humour, reserve, and caprice" (Vol I ch 1); he retreats to his library and laughs at his family.
  • Mrs Bennet: "When she was discontented, she fancied herself nervous" (Vol I ch 1). Her aim is her daughters' marriages.
  • The daughters: Jane (gentle), Elizabeth (quick), Mary (bookish, moralising), Kitty (follows Lydia), Lydia (fifteen in Vol I ch 9, "high animal spirits").
  • Darcy's letter blames their "total want of propriety" for his objections (Vol II ch 12).

Pride and Prejudice · the Bennets and LydiaScreen 5

The elopement and who pays

  • At Brighton Lydia runs off with Wickham: "What a good joke it will be!" (Vol III ch 5).
  • Her letter names Gretna Green: in Scotland, unlike England, under-21s could marry without parental consent (Marriage Act 1753). They go to London, unmarried.

  • Darcy secretly pays Wickham's debts (Vol III ch 10).

Mission checkScreen 8

?Lydia runs off with Wickham. Who is to blame?

Blame is shared, and Austen makes sure we see it. Lydia and Wickham make the choice, but Mrs Bennet's indulgence and Mr Bennet's ironic detachment let it happen: he ignores Elizabeth's warning, then admits, "let me once in my life feel how much I have been to blame". Darcy even blames his own silence.

Mission 7

Austen rarely writes 'she thought'. So whose voice are you reading?

Make a guess: Austen rarely writes 'she thought'. So whose voice are you reading?

Pride and Prejudice · context and free indirect styleScreen 1

Free indirect style

  • Free indirect style (or free indirect discourse): third-person narration that takes on a character's thoughts and words, without "she thought".
  • Clues: the character's own words, exclamations, questions, and judgements the narrator wouldn't make.
  • Effect: we feel close to Elizabeth, so we can share her mistakes before we see them.
  • Austen didn't invent it, but used it more consistently than earlier novelists.

Pride and Prejudice · context and free indirect styleScreen 6

Regency context that helps

  • Drafted 1796–97 as First Impressions; revised and published 28 January 1813, in the Regency (1811–20).
  • The militia: county home-defence regiments, quartered in towns during the French wars. That is why officers fill Meryton and camp at Brighton.
  • Manners: introductions, calls, dances and letters followed strict rules, so breaking them (a muddy walk, an elopement) is noticed.
  • Context is AO3: link it to a choice or event.

Mission checkScreen 8

?Austen rarely writes 'she thought'. So whose voice are you reading?

Often a blend. In free indirect style the narrator's voice takes on Elizabeth's thoughts without saying "she thought", so a sentence like "Whatever he said, was said well" can be her judgement, not a fact. Spotting the switch lets you write about how Austen makes us share, and then question, her view.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Three volumes: misjudgement (I), the proposal and letter that turn the plot (II), correction and resolution (III).
  • Austen's irony works at every level: the first sentence, the narrator's comments, what we know that characters don't, and a plot that reverses.
  • Your question starts from a printed extract, so know where it sits in the three-volume shape.
  • Austen presents Elizabeth as witty, independent and quick to judge: she refuses Collins and Darcy on her own terms.
  • Her misjudgements of Darcy and Wickham are corrected in Volume II Chapter 13 (ch 36): "Till this moment, I never knew myself."
  • Readers disagree on how independent she is: argue both ways, with evidence.
  • First impressions: wealth ("ten thousand a year") and pride at the Meryton assembly (Vol I ch 3).
  • The first proposal mixes love with "His sense of her inferiority" (Vol II ch 11); the letter, Pemberley and Lydia's rescue reveal more.
  • Darcy explains himself in Vol III ch 16: good principles, but "pride and conceit"; use this to argue how far he changes.
  • Money is named precisely: incomes "a year", the entail on Longbourn, Elizabeth's £1,000 portion.
  • Marriage was the main security for gentlewomen; coverture gave husbands control of a wife's property until the Acts of 1870 and 1882.
  • Use context to explain choices (Charlotte, Mrs Bennet), not as a free-standing history lesson.
  • The novel's ranks: titled family, landed gentry, new trade money, the professions and trade; Wickham's place is uncertain.
  • Pride (self-opinion) and vanity (wanting admiration) are Mary's distinction in Vol I ch 5; both Darcy and Elizabeth show pride.
  • Context: land gave status; trade money carried less. Link it to a character: the Bingley sisters, Sir William, the Gardiners.
  • Mr Bennet's wit hides neglect; Mrs Bennet's indulgence brings Lydia "into public" early.
  • Lydia's elopement threatens every sister's reputation; Darcy pays to make it a marriage.
  • Context: an unmarried elopement shamed a whole family; Gretna Green shows the law on parental consent. Link it to the plot.
  • Free indirect style: third-person narration that carries a character's thoughts; clues are her words, exclamations and judgements.
  • It makes us share Elizabeth's view of Wickham and of Darcy, so the novel's irony works on the reader too.
  • Context helps when it explains a choice or event: the militia in Meryton, the entail, shared family disgrace.

Key quotations to learn

  • "It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune must be in want of a wife." (Vol I ch 1 (ch 1)) The ironic opening: money and marriage.
  • "the rightful property of some one or other of their daughters" (Vol I ch 1 (ch 1)) The families, not the man, do the wanting.
  • "The business of her life was to get her daughters married" (Vol I ch 1 (ch 1)) Narrative irony aimed at Mrs Bennet.
  • "the last man in the world whom I could ever be prevailed on to marry" (Vol II ch 11 (ch 34)) Elizabeth's certainty, later reversed.
  • "Till this moment, I never knew myself." (Vol II ch 13 (ch 36)) The turning point of recognition.
  • "had been the means of uniting them" (Vol III ch 19 (ch 61)) The last sentence: the Gardiners, from trade, brought the couple together.
  • "Lizzy has something more of quickness than her sisters" (Vol I ch 1 (ch 1)) Her father's favourite; her wit.
  • "a lively, playful disposition, which delighted in anything ridiculous" (Vol I ch 3 (ch 3)) She laughs off Darcy's insult.
  • "a rational creature speaking the truth from her heart" (Vol I ch 19 (ch 19)) She demands to be taken seriously.
  • "blind, partial, prejudiced, absurd" (Vol II ch 13 (ch 36)) Self-judgement: the title's fault is hers.
  • "But vanity, not love, has been my folly." (Vol II ch 13 (ch 36)) She names her own fault exactly.
  • "Till this moment, I never knew myself." (Vol II ch 13 (ch 36)) The turning point of recognition.
  • "She is tolerable: but not handsome enough to tempt me" (Vol I ch 3 (ch 3)) The first impression.
  • "he was discovered to be proud" (Vol I ch 3 (ch 3)) The room's verdict, reported with irony.
  • "In vain have I struggled. It will not do." (Vol II ch 11 (ch 34)) Love as a battle he has lost.
  • "His sense of her inferiority" (Vol II ch 11 (ch 34)) Pride inside the proposal.
  • "I have been a selfish being all my life, in practice, though not in principle." (Vol III ch 16 (ch 58)) His own account of his fault.
  • "By you, I was properly humbled." (Vol III ch 16 (ch 58)) Elizabeth's effect on him.
  • "entailed, in default of heirs male, on a distant relation" (Vol I ch 7 (ch 7)) The legal fact behind the plot.
  • "the only honourable provision for well-educated young women of small fortune" (Vol I ch 22 (ch 22)) Why marriage matters so much.
  • "pleasantest preservative from want" (Vol I ch 22 (ch 22)) Marriage as protection from poverty.
  • "I am not romantic, you know. I never was." (Vol I ch 22 (ch 22)) Charlotte's own view.
  • "between the mercenary and the prudent motive" (Vol II ch 4 (ch 27)) Elizabeth asks where prudence ends and greed begins.
  • "captivated by youth and beauty" (Vol II ch 19 (ch 42)) A marriage for attraction, and its result.
  • "entitled to think well of themselves and meanly of others" (Vol I ch 4 (ch 4)) The narrator's irony at the sisters' expense.
  • "Pride relates more to our opinion of ourselves; vanity to what we would have others think of us." (Vol I ch 5 (ch 5)) The novel's key distinction.
  • "I could easily forgive his pride, if he had not mortified mine" (Vol I ch 5 (ch 5)) Elizabeth's own pride.
  • "the upstart pretensions of a young woman without family, connections, or fortune" (Vol III ch 14 (ch 56)) Lady Catherine's view of rank.
  • "He is a gentleman; I am a gentleman’s daughter; so far we are equal." (Vol III ch 14 (ch 56)) Elizabeth's reply: equality within rank.
  • "Are the shades of Pemberley to be thus polluted?" (Vol III ch 14 (ch 56)) Snobbery as melodrama.
  • "captivated by youth and beauty" (Vol II ch 19 (ch 42)) Why the Bennet marriage failed.
  • "Lydia will never be easy till she has exposed herself in some public place or other" (Vol II ch 18 (ch 41)) Mr Bennet jokes instead of acting.
  • "What a good joke it will be!" (Vol III ch 5 (ch 47)) Lydia's letter: thoughtless glee.
  • "The death of your daughter would have been a blessing in comparison of this." (Vol III ch 6 (ch 48)) Collins on family disgrace.
  • "let me once in my life feel how much I have been to blame" (Vol III ch 6 (ch 48)) Mr Bennet's admission.
  • "untamed, unabashed, wild, noisy, and fearless" (Vol III ch 9 (ch 51)) Lydia unchanged after her marriage.
  • "Whatever he said, was said well; and whatever he did, done gracefully." (Vol I ch 16 (ch 16)) Free indirect style: Elizabeth's view as narration.
  • "the recent arrival of a militia regiment in the neighbourhood" (Vol I ch 7 (ch 7)) Context: the officers arrive in Meryton.
  • "A woman must have a thorough knowledge of music, singing, drawing, dancing, and the modern languages" (Vol I ch 8 (ch 8)) Regency "accomplishments", as Miss Bingley lists them.
  • "She grew absolutely ashamed of herself." (Vol II ch 13 (ch 36)) Narration close to Elizabeth's feeling.
  • "at that moment she felt that to be mistress of Pemberley might be something!" (Vol III ch 1 (ch 43)) Her thought, with an exclamation, in the narrator's voice.
  • "But while he was mortal, there must be a triumph." (Vol III ch 8 (ch 50)) Comic free indirect style: her proud assumption.
