Christianity: Practices

This mini-lesson works through the OCR GCSE topic Christianity — Practices: how Christians worship and pray, the sacraments of baptism and the Eucharist, the major festivals of Christmas and Easter, pilgrimage, and the mission and work of the Church in the local community and worldwide.

The big question

What are "Practices"?

OCR calls this topic Christianity: Practices. It asks: how do Christians put their faith into action — in worship, in ritual, in the seasons of the year, and in service to others?

  • Worship & prayer — how Christians praise God, together and alone.
  • Sacraments — outward signs of God's grace, especially baptism and the Eucharist.
  • Festivals & pilgrimage — Christmas, Easter and special religious journeys.
  • The Church in action — mission, evangelism and charity, locally and worldwide.

Spec focus: the specification asks you to describe these practices and explain why they matter, showing you understand how and why practices differ between denominations (e.g. Catholic, Orthodox, Anglican and free churches).

Key vocabulary

Words you must be able to define

  • Liturgical worship — worship that follows a set structure or form (e.g. a Catholic Mass or Anglican service).
  • Non-liturgical worship — informal worship with no fixed set order (e.g. in many free churches).
  • Sacrament — an outward sign of inward grace; a holy ritual through which Christians receive God's grace.
  • Eucharist — the sharing of bread and wine in memory of the Last Supper (also called Holy Communion, Mass or the Lord's Supper).
  • Pilgrimage — a special religious journey to a holy place, e.g. Lourdes or Iona.

Common mix-up: liturgical = a set, structured form of worship; non-liturgical = informal and free. Both are ways of worshipping the same God — they are not "better" or "worse".

Sacraments

Baptism — welcome with water

In baptism, water is used as a sign of washing away sin and welcoming a person into the Church. Denominations practise it differently:

Scripture: "Go and make disciples of all nations, baptising them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit." (Matthew 28:19) — the command Christians follow in baptism.
Sacraments

The Eucharist — bread and wine

The Eucharist (also called Holy Communion, Mass or the Lord's Supper) is the sharing of bread and wine in memory of Jesus' Last Supper with his disciples before his death. Christians understand it in different ways:

  • Roman Catholics teach transubstantiation: the bread and wine truly become the body and blood of Christ.
  • Many Protestants (e.g. Baptists) see it mainly as a memorial that recalls Jesus' sacrifice.

Scripture: at the Last Supper Jesus said "This is my body… this is my blood" (based on Luke 22:19–20), telling his followers to "do this in remembrance of me." This is why the Eucharist is central to worship.

Festivals

Christmas — celebrating the incarnation

At Christmas, Christians celebrate the birth of Jesus — the belief that God became human in Jesus, known as the incarnation. It is marked with church services (including Midnight Mass), carols, nativity plays and giving to others.

Scripture: "The Word became flesh and made his dwelling among us." (John 1:14) — the incarnation that Christmas celebrates.

Festivals

Easter and Holy Week

Easter is the most important Christian festival. It celebrates the resurrection of Jesus — the belief that, after being crucified, Jesus rose from the dead. It follows Holy Week, which retells the last days of Jesus' life:

Holy Week: Palm Sunday (Jesus enters Jerusalem) → Maundy Thursday (the Last Supper) → Good Friday (the crucifixion) → Easter Sunday (the resurrection).

Because Christians believe Jesus rose from the dead, they have hope of life after death and celebrate Easter with joyful services, often beginning at dawn on Easter Sunday.

Pilgrimage

Pilgrimage — a special journey

A pilgrimage is a journey to a holy place, made for a religious reason. It is not required for all Christians, but many find it deepens their faith. Popular Christian pilgrimage sites include:

  • Lourdes (France) — a place of healing and prayer, especially for the sick.
  • Iona (Scotland) and Taizé (France) — places of quiet, community and reflection.
  • Jerusalem — where Jesus taught, died and, Christians believe, rose again.
  • Walsingham (England) — a long-established English place of pilgrimage.

Purpose: pilgrims travel to feel closer to God, to pray, to seek healing or forgiveness, and to strengthen their faith through the journey itself and the holy place they reach.

The Church in action

The local church community

The word "Church" means the whole body of Christians, not just the building. In the local community, churches put their faith into action by serving people in need:

  • Food banks — many churches run or support food banks that give food to families in hardship.
  • Street pastors — trained volunteers who care for people out at night in towns and cities.
  • Support such as debt advice, youth groups, warm spaces and help for the homeless.

Why they do it: Christians follow Jesus' teaching to love their neighbour and to serve "the least of these" (Matthew 25:40) — so caring for others is seen as serving Christ himself.

The Church in action

Mission, evangelism and worldwide work

Christians believe the Church has a mission to share the good news of Jesus and to serve the world. Evangelism means sharing the Christian message so others may come to faith.

Worldwide, Christian charities and agencies carry out this mission through practical aid and campaigning for justice:

  • Christian Aid and Tearfund — help people in poverty and respond to disasters.
  • CAFOD — the Catholic agency for overseas development.
  • Such work often includes reconciliation — helping enemies make peace after conflict.

Key idea: the Church is both local (food banks, street pastors) and worldwide (agencies like Christian Aid, CAFOD and Tearfund) — putting the command to love others into action everywhere.

Prayer

Types of prayer

Prayer is communication with God. Christians pray together in church and privately at home. The specification highlights different kinds of prayer:

  • Set prayer — a fixed, well-known prayer, such as the Lord's Prayer, which Jesus taught his followers.
  • Informal (extempore) prayer — prayer in a person's own words, spoken from the heart.

Prayers can also give thanks, ask forgiveness, praise God (adoration), or ask for help for oneself or others (intercession). Private prayer lets a believer speak to God at any time.

Memory hook: set prayer = the same familiar words; informal prayer = your own words, in the moment.

Why it matters

Why these practices matter

Exam questions often ask about the importance of Christian practices. Practices are not just rituals — they express faith and shape daily life:

  • Worship and prayer help Christians feel close to God and strengthen their faith and community.
  • Baptism marks the start of the Christian life and welcomes a person into the Church.
  • The Eucharist reminds Christians of Jesus' death and, for many, feeds them with God's grace.
  • Festivals like Christmas and Easter retell the core story of Jesus each year.
  • Pilgrimage and mission (food banks, street pastors, agencies) turn belief into action and service.

Recap

The practices to know

Worship: liturgical (set form) vs non-liturgical (informal); plus private prayer.

Prayer: set (e.g. the Lord's Prayer) and informal; adoration, thanks, forgiveness, intercession.

Baptism: welcome with water — infant baptism vs believer's baptism.

Eucharist: bread & wine in memory of the Last Supper (Mass / Holy Communion).

Festivals: Christmas (birth of Jesus) & Easter (resurrection); Holy Week.

Pilgrimage: Lourdes, Iona, Jerusalem, Taizé, Walsingham — a special religious journey.

Mission: food banks, street pastors, evangelism; Christian Aid, CAFOD, Tearfund; reconciliation.
