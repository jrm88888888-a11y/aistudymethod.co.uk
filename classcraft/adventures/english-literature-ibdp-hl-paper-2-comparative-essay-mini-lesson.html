Mission 1

Four questions, two works, no texts in the room. How do you choose?

Make a guess: Four questions, two works, no texts in the room. How do you choose?

IB Paper 2 · the comparative essayScreen 1

What Paper 2 asks

  • You answer one of four general questions, comparing and contrasting two works you have studied.
  • Any two literary works from your course, originals or works in translation.
  • It is closed book: no texts in the room, so you bring quotations in your memory.
  • You can't use a work you used for your Individual Oral.
  • Nor can you use a work from your HL essay.
IB Paper 2 · the comparative essayScreen 2

How your exam asks this

  • Paper 2, Comparative essay: 1 hour 45 minutes, 25 marks, 35% at SL.
  • At HL the paper is the same, but it is worth 25% of your grade.
  • From May 2026, five criteria of 5 marks each: A Knowledge, understanding and interpretation; B1 Analysis and evaluation of textual features and/or authorial choices; B2 Comparative analysis; C Focus and organization; D Language.
  • Comparison now has its own criterion (B2), so it is marked separately.
IB Paper 2 · the comparative essayScreen 3

Choosing the question

  • Prepare two or three pairs of works before the exam, each with a stock of short quotations. Track pairings in your learner portfolio.
  • Read all four questions. Underline the key terms in each.
  • Choose the question where both works give you evidence about how (the authors' choices), not just what happens.
  • Check you can find both similarities and differences.

Comparing throughout

  • Block: all of work A, then all of work B. Easy to plan, but comparison can shrink to a final paragraph.
  • Integrated: each paragraph makes one comparative point using both works.
  • Integrated comparison usually serves criterion B2 best, because comparison runs through the essay.
  • Compare choices, not just themes: how does each author use imagery, structure or voice for the idea?
  • Use connectives: whereas, similarly, by contrast, both… but.

Mission 2

Seven concepts: how do you actually use one in an essay?

Make a guess: Seven concepts: how do you actually use one in an essay?

IB concepts · using a conceptScreen 1

Seven concepts, seven questions

ConceptA question it helps you ask
IdentityHow does the text build or challenge who someone is?
CultureHow does it show or question shared values and customs?
CreativityHow does the writer make something new?
CommunicationHow does meaning pass between writer, text and reader?
PerspectiveWhose point of view shapes what we see?
TransformationWhat changes: a character, a reader, an older text?
RepresentationHow does the text depict people, groups or ideas?
IB concepts · using a conceptScreen 2

A lens, not a topic

  • A topic says what: Jane's independence in Jane Eyre.
  • A concept asks how and why: through identity, how does Brontë's first-person narration let Jane claim an identity others deny her?
  • Use one concept as the thread of an argument; don't tour all seven.

Where this counts

  • No criterion marks a concept: concepts help you build an argument.
  • Paper 2 (25 marks): its four general questions may not name a concept, but many turn on ideas like representation or perspective; one concept can link your two works.
  • Individual Oral: a concept can sharpen how both texts present your global issue.
  • HL essay: a concept is a good starting point for a line of inquiry; you don't have to name one.

Mission checkScreen 8

?Seven concepts: how do you actually use one in an essay?

Use it as a lens. Pick the one concept that best opens your text, turn it into a how-question (how does Brontë's narration build Jane's identity?), and let it guide which methods and evidence you analyse. A concept named but not used earns nothing.

Recap

Big ideas

  • Paper 2: one of four general questions, comparing two works studied, closed book, 1 hour 45 minutes, 25 marks from 2026.
  • Choose the question your prepared pair can answer about how, not just what.
  • Compare throughout: comparison now has its own criterion (B2).
  • At HL the paper is the same but worth 25%; works from your IO and HL essay can't be used.
  • The seven concepts: identity, culture, creativity, communication, perspective, transformation, representation.
  • Use one as a lens: it turns a topic into a how-question and an arguable thesis.
  • No criterion marks a concept; it helps Paper 2 and the IO.
  • It helps the HL essay too.

Key quotations to learn

  • The voice of the sea is seductive (Chopin, The Awakening, Ch. VI) The sea as a voice calling Edna to freedom and solitude.
  • abysses of solitude (Chopin, The Awakening, Ch. VI) Freedom imagined as depth and isolation.
  • boats against the current (Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby, Ch. IX) Effort against time: the novel's final image.
  • borne back ceaselessly into the past (Fitzgerald, The Great Gatsby, Ch. IX) The dream lies behind, not ahead.
  • I am no bird; and no net ensnares me (Jane Eyre, Ch. 23) Identity: Jane rejects Rochester's image of her.
  • I am a free human being with an independent will (Jane Eyre, Ch. 23) She names the identity she claims.
  • because I am poor, obscure, plain, and little (Jane Eyre, Ch. 23) Representation: the labels others use for her.
  • I have as much soul as you (Jane Eyre, Ch. 23) Equality claimed across class.
