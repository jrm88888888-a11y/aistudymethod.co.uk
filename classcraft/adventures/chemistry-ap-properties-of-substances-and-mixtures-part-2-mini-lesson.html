Mission 1

Mission 1: A 500 mL IV bag holds 9.00 g of salt per liter. How many moles of salt are in the bag?

Make a guess: A 500 mL IV bag holds 9.00 g of salt per liter. How many moles of salt are in the bag?

Mission 1

What a concentration measures

  • Concentration = amount of solute ÷ volume of solution: c = n / V.
  • Molarity, M = mol of solute ÷ liters of solution. 1 M = 1 mol/L.
  • V is the whole solution, not the water added.
  • Square brackets mean concentration: [NaCl] = 0.100 M.
  • A solution is homogeneous: the same composition everywhere. A heterogeneous mixture varies from place to place.
Mission 1

n = cV and volume units

  • n = M × V, with V in L. 1 L = 1000 mL: divide mL by 1000.
  • 40.0 mL of 0.150 M KOH: n = 0.150 × 0.0400 = 0.00600 mol.
  • Rearranged: 0.0125 mol in 250 mL: M = 0.0125 ÷ 0.250 = 0.0500 M.
Quick check
?A student dissolves 17.1 g of sucrose, C12H22O11 (molar mass 342.3 g/mol), in 40.0 mL of water. The volume of the resulting solution is 50.5 mL. What is the molarity of sucrose in the solution?
Mission 1

Mass concentration and the drip bag

  • Concentration can be a mass per volume: g/L. g/L ÷ molar mass = mol/L (M).
  • The bag: 9.00 g/L ÷ 58.44 g/mol = 0.154 M. V = 500 mL = 0.500 L.
  • n = 0.154 × 0.500 = 0.0770 mol of NaCl.
  • Check another way: the bag holds 9.00 × 0.500 = 4.50 g; 4.50 ÷ M gives the same answer.
Calculate
?A solution contains 4.90 g of sulfuric acid, H2SO4, in 500 mL of solution. Calculate its concentration.
  • Data: H 1.008, O 16.00, S 32.06. Answer in M.
M
Mission 1

Making a standard solution

  • A standard solution has an accurately known concentration.
  • Weigh the solid accurately; reweigh the empty container (weighing by difference).
  • Dissolve it in a little distilled water in a beaker.
  • Transfer to a volumetric flask with a funnel; rinse beaker, rod and funnel into the flask.
  • Add water to the mark: bottom of the meniscus on the line, at eye level. Last drops from a dropping pipet.
  • Stopper and invert several times to mix.
Quick check
?A student prepares 100.0 mL of 0.200 M CuSO4(aq) by placing 3.19 g of CuSO4(s) in a 100.0 mL volumetric flask, dissolving it in some distilled water, adding distilled water to the mark and mixing. The molarity of the solution is later found to be less than 0.200 M. Which of the following could explain this result?
Mission 1

Dilution

  • Adding water changes the volume, not the amount of solute.
  • So c1V1 = c2V2.
  • 25.0 mL of 2.00 M HCl diluted to 250.0 mL: M2 = 2.00 × 25.0 ÷ 250.0 = 0.200 M.
  • Method: pipet the stock solution into a volumetric flask, then make up to the mark and invert.
  • In a particle drawing, the diluted solution shows the same number of solute particles spread through more water.
Quick check
?A student adds 600.0 mL of water to 200.0 mL of 0.500 M NaCl(aq), giving 800.0 mL of solution. What is the molarity of NaCl in the diluted solution?
Mission 1

Concentrations in reactions

  • Use n = cV to get moles, then the equation ratio.
  • 2NaOH(aq) + H2SO4(aq) → Na2SO4(aq) + 2H2O(l).
  • 25.0 mL of 0.100 M NaOH reacts exactly with 20.0 mL of H2SO4(aq).
  • n(NaOH) = 0.100 × 0.0250 = 0.00250 mol. Ratio 2 : 1, so n(H2SO4) = 0.00125 mol.
  • [H2SO4] = 0.00125 ÷ 0.0200 L = 0.0625 M.
  • This is the calculation behind a titration: method and practice come in a later mission.
Mission check

?Seawater contains about 1.29 g of magnesium ions, Mg²⁺, per liter. How many moles of Mg²⁺ are in 250 mL of seawater? Data: Mg 24.31.

Same two steps as the drip bag: mass concentration ÷ M, then × volume in L. The bag holds about 0.077 mol of NaCl.

Mission 2

Mission 2: Why do fireworks come in different colors?

Make a guess: Why do fireworks come in different colors?

Mission 2

Wavelength, frequency, energy

  • Light travels as photons. c = λν and E = hν (ν = frequency).
  • Shorter wavelength → higher frequency → more energy per photon.
  • Energy order: microwave < infrared < visible < ultraviolet. Red is lowest in visible light, violet highest.
  • Sodium's yellow light, λ = 589 nm = 5.89 × 10⁻⁷ m:
  • ν = 2.998 × 10⁸ ÷ 5.89 × 10⁻⁷ = 5.09 × 10¹⁴ s⁻¹. E = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ × 5.09 × 10¹⁴ = 3.37 × 10⁻¹⁹ J per photon.
Mission 2

How atoms give out light

  • Heat or electricity excites an electron to a higher energy level.
  • When it falls back, it emits a photon. Photon energy = the energy gap.
  • The atom's energy falls by exactly the photon's energy. Absorbing a photon raises it by exactly that much.
  • Only certain gaps exist, so only certain colors: an emission line spectrum, bright lines on a dark background.
Quick check
?In a glow stick, a chemical reaction leaves dye molecules with extra energy, and the dye molecules then give out visible light. Which change takes place in a dye molecule as it emits a photon of visible light?
Mission 2

What hydrogen's spectrum proves

  • Background (not required for AP): hydrogen's lines show the energy-level idea in its simplest atom.
  • Hydrogen gives lines, not a continuous band, so its electron has fixed energy levels.
  • Levels get closer together as n rises, so each set of lines converges at high frequency.
  • Falls to n = 1 emit ultraviolet; to n = 2, mainly visible; to n = 3, infrared.
  • One electron, many lines: different transitions happen in different atoms of the sample.
Quick check
?A green laser pointer emits light of wavelength 532 nm. What is the energy of one photon of this light?
Mission 2

How many electrons a level holds

  • Background: AP describes this as shells and subshells.
  • Main levels (shells) are numbered n = 1, 2, 3, 4 outward.
  • Level n holds at most 2n² electrons: 2, 8, 18, 32.
  • The third level holds 18, not 8. "2, 8, 8" is how the first 20 elements fill, not a capacity.
Mission 2

Molecules absorb photons too

  • Molecules have energy levels for rotation, vibration and electrons.
  • Microwave photons → changes in rotational levels.
  • Infrared photons → changes in vibrational levels.
  • UV/visible photons → electronic transitions.
  • The bigger the energy change, the higher the photon energy needed.
Quick check
?Carbon dioxide in the atmosphere absorbs infrared radiation given off by Earth's surface, and the surrounding air becomes warmer as a result. Which reasoning best connects the absorption of infrared photons by CO2 molecules to the warming of the air?
Mission check

?Lithium compounds give a crimson-red flame. Sodium compounds give a yellow-orange flame. Which statement explains the difference?

That's the firework: each metal has its own energy gaps, so its falling electrons emit their own photon energies, and so their own colors.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Why does carbon dioxide trap heat, while nitrogen, most of the air, does not?

Make a guess: Why does carbon dioxide trap heat, while nitrogen, most of the air, does not?

Mission 3

Radiation and molecular motion

  • Each spectral region matches a different kind of change in a molecule:
  • Microwave → rotational levels: the molecule turns faster.
  • Infrared → vibrational levels: bonds stretch and bend more.
  • Ultraviolet/visible → electronic levels: an electron moves to a higher level.
  • A photon is absorbed only if its energy matches the gap between two levels.
Quick check
?A student wants to compare the energies of the bond vibrations in the molecules of two different gaseous compounds. Which measurement is best aligned with this question?
Mission 3

Greenhouse gases

  • The Earth's surface emits infrared. Greenhouse gases absorb it, then re-emit it in all directions, some back down.
  • The absorbed IR raises molecules to higher vibrational levels; collisions pass the energy on and warm the air.
  • A molecule absorbs IR only if a vibration changes its dipole.
  • CO2 is nonpolar, but its asymmetric stretch and bend change the dipole: it absorbs IR.
  • N2 and O2: stretching never makes them polar, so they don't absorb IR.
  • Background: the dipole rule answers the opening question; it is not tested on the AP exam.
Quick check
?A student proposes this model: "Any gas absorbs infrared radiation, because the particles of the gas undergo transitions to higher vibrational energy levels." The student applies the model to Ar(g) and to CO(g). Which evaluation of the model is correct?
Mission check

?An analyzer measures carbon monoxide in car exhaust by how much infrared radiation the gas absorbs. Which statement best describes what happens when a CO molecule absorbs an infrared photon?

Infrared ↔ vibration. That is why CO2 and CO absorb the infrared the Earth gives off.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Why do the dyes in one drop of ink travel different distances up the same strip of paper?

Make a guess: Why do the dyes in one drop of ink travel different distances up the same strip of paper?

Mission 4

Two phases

  • Chromatography separates the components of a mixture between two phases.
  • Mobile phase: moves (a liquid solvent, or a gas).
  • Stationary phase: stays put (paper, a coated plate, a packed column).
  • Each component keeps switching: dissolved in the mobile phase ↔ held by the stationary phase.
  • More time in the mobile phase → it moves further (or comes out sooner).
  • The hold comes from intermolecular interactions with the stationary phase's surface, competing with interactions with the mobile phase.
Mission 4

Running a TLC or paper chromatogram

  • Draw the origin line in pencil (ink would itself separate).
  • Spot the mixture and reference samples on the line; let the spots dry.
  • Stand the plate in solvent below the origin (or the spots dissolve away into the solvent).
  • Cover the tank: the air inside stays saturated with solvent vapor.
  • Remove before the solvent reaches the top; mark the solvent front at once.
  • Colorless spots: view under UV light (plates with a fluorescent coating), or use iodine vapor or a locating spray.
  • Safety: solvents are often flammable and harmful: no flames, use a fume hood; never look into the UV lamp.
Mission 4

Why some components move less

  • Silica (TLC) and paper both have many polar –OH groups on their surface (paper: cellulose, with water held on it).
  • Polar components are held strongly (dipole–dipole forces, hydrogen bonds): they move less.
  • Less polar components spend more time in a less polar solvent: they move further.
  • So the order of spots tells you the relative polarity of the components.
  • Mass is not the main cause: a small polar molecule can stay behind a large nonpolar one.
Quick check
?The dyes in a black ink are separated by paper chromatography. Which difference between the dyes is mainly responsible for the separation?
Quick check
?A mixture of two compounds, X and Y, is spotted on a thin-layer chromatography plate coated with silica, a polar stationary phase, and the plate is developed with hexane, a nonpolar mobile phase. The diagram shows the developed plate (X about 80% of the way to the solvent front, Y about 20%). Which conclusion is best supported by the result?
Mission 4

Chromatography in a column

  • Column chromatography: solvent moves down a column packed with a solid (e.g. silica); components come out (elute) at different times; collect them in separate flasks.
  • On a polar packing, the least polar component comes out first.
Mission check

?A student runs TLC of a mixture of two polar compounds on a silica plate, using hexane as the solvent. Both spots barely leave the origin. Which change is most likely to make the spots move further up the plate and separate?

Every component shares its time between the two phases. Change that balance, and you change how far it goes.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Two glasses of a blue sports drink look the same. How could a beam of light tell which holds more dye?

Make a guess: Two glasses of a blue sports drink look the same. How could a beam of light tell which holds more dye?

Mission 5

Why a solution looks colored

  • White light contains every visible wavelength.
  • A colored solution absorbs some wavelengths and transmits the rest.
  • You see the transmitted light. A blue solution absorbs mainly orange and red light.
  • More dye in the beam's path: more light absorbed.
Mission 5

Measuring absorbance

  • A spectrophotometer: lamp → chosen wavelength → sample in a cuvette → detector.
  • First, zero the instrument with a cuvette of the solvent alone (the blank).
  • The meter reads absorbance, A: how much light the sample absorbs. No units.
  • More light absorbed → less reaches the detector → higher absorbance.
Mission 5

Choosing the wavelength

  • Set the wavelength of maximum absorbance, λmax, read from the absorbance spectrum.
  • Here, a small change in concentration gives the biggest change in absorbance: maximum sensitivity.
Quick check
?A student measures the absorbances of five standard solutions of a dye at 620 nm, the dye's wavelength of maximum absorbance, and obtains a straight-line calibration graph that passes through the origin. A second student measures the same standard solutions in the same cuvette with the spectrophotometer set to 540 nm. How will the second student's calibration graph compare with the first student's graph?
Mission 5

The Beer–Lambert law

  • A = εbc
  • ε: molar absorptivity, M⁻¹ cm⁻¹. How strongly the species absorbs at that wavelength.
  • b: path length, cm: the distance the light travels through the solution.
  • c: concentration, M.
  • b and c set how many absorbing particles sit in the beam.
  • Same cuvette, same wavelength: ε and b are fixed, so A ∝ c.
Mission 5

Worked example

  • A blue dye: ε = 1.30 × 10⁵ M⁻¹ cm⁻¹ at 630 nm. Cuvette b = 1.00 cm. Measured A = 0.520.
  • Rearrange: c = A ÷ (εb).
  • c = 0.520 ÷ (1.30 × 10⁵ M⁻¹ cm⁻¹ × 1.00 cm)
  • c = 4.00 × 10⁻⁶ M
  • The volume of solution in the cuvette never enters the calculation.
Quick check
?A cuvette with a path length of 0.500 cm holds 3.00 mL of a 2.00 × 10⁻⁵ M solution of a dye. At the dye's wavelength of maximum absorbance, the absorbance of the solution is 0.300. What is the molar absorptivity of the dye at this wavelength?
Mission 5

The calibration graph

  • Make standard solutions of known concentration from one stock solution.
  • Measure each one's absorbance: same filter or wavelength, same cuvette.
  • Plot absorbance (y) against concentration (x). Here it is a straight line through the origin.
  • Measure the unknown the same way and read its concentration off the graph.
  • Example: copper(II) standards 0.020–0.100 M give A = 0.12–0.60. An unknown with A = 0.33 reads 0.055 M.
  • The graph does the work: you don't need to know ε.
Quick check
?The absorbance of a solution of a dye, measured at its wavelength of maximum absorbance in a 1.00 cm cuvette, is 0.90. A student mixes 20.0 mL of this solution with 10.0 mL of distilled water. Assuming that the volumes are additive, what is the absorbance of the diluted solution, measured in the same way?
Mission check

?A green solution of nickel(II) ions is taken from a lab waste container. Its absorbance is measured under the same conditions as these nickel(II) standards.
Standards (M : A): 0.050 : 0.16 · 0.100 : 0.32 · 0.150 : 0.48 · 0.200 : 0.64. The sample gives A = 0.40. What is its nickel(II) concentration, in M?

Your eyes can't tell the two glasses apart, but a detector can. The drink with more dye absorbs more orange-red light; read each absorbance off a calibration graph, and you know exactly how much more dye it holds.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • M = mol/L of solution; divide mL by 1000. Dilution keeps moles the same: M1V1 = M2V2.

Mission 2

  • An electron falling between levels emits a photon; photon energy = the gap. Fixed gaps → fixed colors.
  • c = λν, E = hν; microwave–rotation, IR–vibration, UV/visible–electronic.

Mission 3

  • Microwave ↔ rotation; infrared ↔ vibration; UV/visible ↔ electrons. IR is absorbed only when a vibration changes the dipole.

Mission 4

  • Components separate because each is attracted differently to the stationary phase and the mobile phase.
  • On a polar stationary phase, the more polar component travels less.

Mission 5

  • Colored species absorb light; absorbance rises in step with concentration. A calibration graph turns absorbance into concentration.
  • A = εbc: with b and the wavelength fixed, A ∝ c. Measure at λmax.
