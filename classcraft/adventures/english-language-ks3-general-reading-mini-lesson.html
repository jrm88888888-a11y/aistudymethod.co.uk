Reading

This mini-lesson builds the reading skills you use in every English lesson: how to skim, scan and read closely, how to infer (read between the lines), how to spot a theme, how to name language techniques and their effect, and how to write about a text using PEE.

Work through each screen, answer the questions as you go and collect ⭐ stars. Press Start when you're ready.

Three ways to read

Skim, scan, or read closely

Good readers change speed on purpose. Pick the mode that suits the job:

  • Skimming — reading fast to get the gist (the overall idea). Your eyes glide over headings, first lines and key words.
  • Scanning — hunting for one specific thing (a name, date or fact). You ignore everything else.
  • Close reading — slow, careful re-reading of a short section to analyse how the writer creates meaning.

Watch out: you can't analyse a text properly while skimming. To answer "how does the writer…?" questions you must slow down and read closely.

Understanding & critical reading

Reading between the lines

Some meaning is stated directly on the page — that is literal meaning. But writers also imply things without saying them. Working these out is called inference (or deduction).

Worked example

"Sam pushed the untouched plate away and stared at the door."

Literal: Sam moved the plate and looked at the door.

Inference: Sam isn't hungry and may be worried or waiting for someone — the food is "untouched" and the eyes are on the "door".

Key idea: an inference is a sensible guess backed by clues in the text — not a wild one. Always ask "what in the text makes me think that?"

Themes, ideas & information

Spotting a theme

A theme is a big idea a text explores again and again — like friendship, courage, power, loss or growing up. It is not the same as the plot (what happens).

  • Plot: a boy trains hard and finally wins the race.
  • Theme: perseverance — the idea that effort over time pays off.

Find a theme by asking: what idea keeps coming back? Repeated words, images or situations are your clues.

Watch out: a theme is usually one abstract idea ("courage"), not a full sentence retelling the story.

Analysing language

Language techniques

Writers choose language on purpose to create effects. Learn to name these techniques:

  • Simile — compares using like or as: "as quiet as a shadow".
  • Metaphor — says one thing is another: "the classroom was a zoo".
  • Personification — gives human traits to a non-human thing: "the wind whispered".
  • Alliteration — repeated first sounds: "the slow, silent snow".
  • Adjectives — describing words that add detail: "a cracked, crooked door".
Technique → effect

Name the effect, not just the device

The mark isn't for spotting a metaphor — it's for explaining its effect: what it makes the reader think, feel or picture.

Worked example

"The classroom was a zoo."

Technique: metaphor.

Effect: it makes the room feel wild, noisy and out of control, as if the pupils are unruly animals — far more vivid than simply "it was noisy".

Misconception: writing "the writer uses a simile" earns little on its own. Always add "…which makes the reader feel/imagine…".

Writing about a text

The PEE structure

When you write about reading, use PEE so every idea is proven:

Point → Evidence → Explainmake a point · quote the text · explain the effect
PEE in action

A full PEE paragraph

Text: "The last leaf clung to the bare branch."

Model answer

P (Point): The writer suggests the leaf is struggling to survive.

E (Evidence): The leaf "clung" to the branch.

E (Explain): The verb "clung" makes the leaf seem desperate and determined, as if it is holding on for life — this creates a feeling of fragility and makes the reader want it to survive.

Notice: the quotation is short and dropped straight into the sentence, and the Explain step focuses on the effect of one word.

Using evidence

Embed short quotations

Good evidence is short and slotted smoothly into your own sentence, inside quotation marks. This is called embedding.

  • ❌ Too long / clumsy: The writer says "The last leaf clung to the bare branch as the cold wind blew across the empty garden all afternoon."
  • ✅ Embedded: The leaf "clung" to the branch, hinting at its desperate fight to survive.

Misconception: longer quotes are not better. Quote only the key word or phrase you are going to explain.

Form & structure

More than just words

Meaning also comes from form (the type of text and its shape) and structure (the order ideas are put in).

  • Sentence structure: a short sentence after long ones can create shock or emphasis — "…and then it stopped. Silence."
  • Text types: a newspaper report, a poem, a play script and a letter each have their own conventions (features you'd expect).
  • Tone: the writer's attitude — playful, angry, formal — shown through word choice.

Tip: when comparing two texts, look at form and structure too, not just the words — e.g. a chatty blog vs a formal report on the same topic.

Critical comparison

Comparing texts

A critical comparison looks at how two texts are similar or different in what they say and how they say it. Use linking words:

  • Similarly / likewise / both — for points in common.
  • Whereas / in contrast / however — for differences.
Worked example

Both writers describe the sea, but whereas Text A calls it "gentle" and "silver", Text B calls it "a raging beast" — so Text A feels calm whereas Text B feels dangerous.

Tip: always compare the same thing across both texts (e.g. how each describes the sea), and back each side with a short quote.

Put it all together

Read, infer, explain

Use everything so far on this short extract:

"The new pupil sat alone, her eyes fixed on the door, her lunch untouched."

Notice the clues: "alone", "eyes fixed on the door", "untouched". Together they let us infer she feels nervous or unsettled on her first day — and we can prove it with a short quote like "untouched".

Remember the routine: read closely → find a clue → make an inference → prove it with a short embedded quote → explain the effect.

Recap

The skills to remember

Read the right way: skim for gist, scan for a fact, read closely to analyse.

Infer: read between the lines — a sensible guess backed by a clue.

Themes: the big recurring idea, not the plot.

Technique + effect: name the device, then what it makes the reader think/feel.

PEE: Point → Evidence → Explain.

Quotes: keep them short and embedded.
