Mission 1: Can a smile count as charity? What makes Muslim giving more than a donation?

Make a guess: Can a smile count as charity? What makes Muslim giving more than a donation?

Mission 1

Sadaqah: voluntary giving

  • Sadaqah is voluntary charity: any amount, at any time, to anyone in need, including non-Muslims.
  • The word is linked to sidq, "truthfulness": giving shows that faith is sincere.
  • It need not be money. The Prophet taught that every good deed is sadaqah, and that smiling at your brother is sadaqah (hadith, paraphrase).
  • Sadaqah jariyah ("ongoing charity"): giving that keeps on helping, e.g. a well, a school, planting a tree, or useful knowledge. The Prophet said its reward continues after death (Sahih Muslim, paraphrase).
  • Zakah is the compulsory 2.5%; sadaqah is everything given beyond it.
Mission 1Read the source

Giving in secret and openly

"If ye publish your almsgiving, it is well, but if ye hide it and give it to the poor, it will be better for you, and will atone for some of your ill-deeds." (Qur'an 2:271, Pickthall)

"Those who spend their wealth by night and day, by stealth and openly, verily their reward is with their Lord" (Qur'an 2:274, Pickthall)

  • 2:215: give to parents, near kindred, orphans, the needy and the wayfarer (paraphrase).

Q1 Which kind of giving does 2:271 call "better"?

Show answer

Giving in secret, directly to the poor.

Q2 Does 2:274 forbid open giving?

Show answer

No: it praises giving "by stealth and openly"; the reward is with Allah either way.

Mission 1

Why Muslims give

  • Wealth belongs to Allah: humans are trustees (57:7) and must share with those in need.
  • Reward multiplies: those who spend in Allah's way are like a grain that grows seven ears, each with a hundred grains (2:261, paraphrase).
  • Giving does not reduce wealth, the Prophet taught (Sahih Muslim, paraphrase); it purifies and brings blessing.
  • Kindness matters more than the gift: "A kind word with forgiveness is better than almsgiving followed by injury." (Qur'an 2:263, Pickthall)
  • The ummah is one body: when one part hurts, the whole body feels it (hadith, paraphrase).

Mission 1

Zakah or sadaqah?

  • Zakah: compulsory (a pillar) · fixed 2.5% of savings above nisab · once a year · only the eight groups in 9:60, mostly Muslims · cannot fund mosque building or go to your own parents or children.
  • Sadaqah: voluntary · any amount, any form · any time · anyone in need, Muslim or not · can fund mosques, schools, wells, hospitals and disaster relief.
  • Both: given for Allah's sake, ideally discreetly, without boasting or hurting the receiver (2:263–264).
Mission 1

Islamic Relief

  • Founded in 1984 in Birmingham by Dr Hany El-Banna and fellow students, to help victims of famine in Sudan. Its first donation was 20p.
  • Now one of the largest Muslim charities in the world, working in many countries.
  • In the UK: food parcels and food banks, support for people who are homeless or in fuel poverty, and Ramadan and winter appeals.
  • Worldwide: emergency relief after disasters and war; Qurbani meat at Eid-ul-Adha; water, health, education; orphan sponsorship; long-term projects so people can earn a living.
  • Why: "inspired by faith": zakah, sadaqah and 5:32: "whoso saveth the life of one, it shall be as if he had saved the life of all mankind" (Qur'an 5:32, Pickthall).
  • Helps people whatever their faith, as sadaqah allows.

Mission 1Evaluate

"It is always better to give in secret."

  • Reasons for: 2:271 says hidden giving is "better for you"; secrecy protects the receiver's dignity and the giver's sincerity; the Prophet praised the one who gives so secretly that the left hand does not know what the right hand gives (hadith, paraphrase).
  • Reasons against: 2:271 also says open giving "is well", and 2:274 praises giving "openly"; public appeals by charities raise far more and encourage others; zakah is often paid openly through institutions so it can be checked.
  • Another view: what matters is intention (niyyah): giving for Allah's sake, not for praise, can be open or secret.
  • Judge it by: What exactly does 2:271 say? Whose interests matter most: the giver's soul, the receiver's dignity, or the number helped? Can large charities work in secret?

Mission 2

Sawm: the fourth pillar

  • Sawm means fasting (literally "to abstain"). It is one of the Five Pillars of Islam.
  • Ramadan is the ninth month of the Islamic lunar calendar. It begins when the new crescent moon is sighted.
  • From dawn to sunset, adult Muslims give up: food, drink (including water), smoking and sexual relations.
  • They also try to avoid lying, anger, gossip and bad deeds: the Prophet said Allah has no need of someone giving up food and drink who does not give up false speech (Sahih al-Bukhari, paraphrase).
  • The lunar year is about 11 days shorter than the solar year, so Ramadan moves through the seasons over about 33 years.
Mission 2Read the source

Fasting is prescribed

"O ye who believe! Fasting is prescribed for you, even as it was prescribed for those before you, that ye may ward off (evil)" (Qur'an 2:183, Pickthall)

"The month of Ramadan in which was revealed the Qur'an, a guidance for mankind" (Qur'an 2:185, Pickthall)

  • "(Fast) a certain number of days; and (for) him who is sick among you, or on a journey, (the same) number of other days" (Qur'an 2:184, Pickthall)
  • 2:187 sets the times: eat and drink "until the white thread becometh distinct to you from the black thread of the dawn. Then strictly observe the fast till nightfall" (Qur'an 2:187, Pickthall)
  • The passage also teaches that Allah is near and answers those who call on Him (2:186), and that new moons are "fixed seasons" for people (2:189): Ramadan begins with the crescent (paraphrase).

Q1 According to 2:183, what is the purpose of fasting?

Show answer

To ward off evil: to develop taqwa, awareness of Allah and self-restraint.

Q2 Why is Ramadan the month chosen for fasting?

Show answer

It is the month in which the Qur'an was revealed, 2:185.

Mission 2

Origins and the Prophet's example

  • 2:183 says fasting was given to earlier peoples too: Muslims see Sawm as continuing the practice of earlier prophets.
  • In Makkah the Prophet fasted and spent nights in prayer; the Qur'an was first revealed to him in Ramadan.
  • In Madinah, in the second year after the Hijrah (624 CE), fasting the whole month of Ramadan was made compulsory.
  • The Prophet's example: he ate suhur before dawn, saying there is blessing in it (Sahih al-Bukhari, paraphrase); he broke the fast at once at sunset with fresh dates or water; he was most generous in Ramadan.
  • He did not allow followers to harm themselves: he forbade fasting day after day without breaking at night.
Mission 2

A day in Ramadan

  • Intention (niyyah): made each night or at the start of the month.
  • Suhur: a meal before dawn, then Fajr prayer.
  • The day: work, school and salah as usual; extra Qur'an reading. Many read the whole Qur'an over the month (in 30 parts).
  • Iftar at sunset (Maghrib): often dates and water, then Maghrib prayer and a meal, often shared with family, the mosque or neighbours.
  • Night: Sunni Muslims pray tarawih (extra night prayers) in the mosque. Shi'a Muslims do not pray tarawih in congregation but keep other night prayers and du'as.

Mission 2

Who is excused, and how they make up

  • Excused from fasting:
  • children before puberty (many practise with half-days);
  • the ill, and travellers on a long journey (2:184);
  • women who are pregnant, breastfeeding, menstruating or recovering from childbirth;
  • the elderly or anyone whose health would be harmed; people with serious mental illness.
  • Why: "Allah desireth for you ease; He desireth not hardship for you" (Qur'an 2:185, Pickthall).
  • Making up: missed days are fasted later (qada). Those who can never fast feed a poor person for each day (fidyah, 2:184).
Mission 2

Benefits for the self and the ummah

  • Taqwa: awareness of Allah and self-restraint (2:183). A person who can control hunger can control anger and greed.
  • Obedience and reward: the Prophet taught that whoever fasts Ramadan with faith, hoping for Allah's reward, has past sins forgiven (Sahih al-Bukhari, paraphrase).
  • Empathy: hunger helps Muslims feel what the poor feel, and leads to giving.
  • Gratitude for food and water usually taken for granted.
  • Ummah: Muslims worldwide fast in the same month; iftars bring families and communities together.
  • Giving: many pay zakah and give sadaqah (voluntary charity) in Ramadan, as the Prophet was most generous then. Zakat al-Fitr is paid before Eid prayer so the poor can celebrate too.
Mission 2

Laylat al-Qadr, the Night of Power

  • The night in 610 CE when, Muslims believe, the angel Jibril brought the first verses of the Qur'an to Muhammad in the cave of Hira:
  • "Read: In the name of thy Lord Who createth, Createth man from a clot." (Qur'an 96:1–2, Pickthall)
  • "The Night of Power is better than a thousand months." (Qur'an 97:3, Pickthall)
  • It falls on one of the odd nights of the last ten nights of Ramadan; many Sunni Muslims mark the 27th. Shi'a Muslims stress the 19th, 21st and 23rd (the 21st is also the anniversary of Imam Ali's death).
  • Today: Muslims stay up in prayer, Qur'an reading and du'a, asking forgiveness. It reminds them that the Qur'an is guidance from Allah.

Mission 2Diversity

Fasting in Britain and a changing world

  • Long days: when Ramadan falls in midsummer, the fast in Britain runs from before 3 a.m. to after 9 p.m.; in winter it is much shorter.
  • Different rulings: most British Muslims follow local dawn and sunset; some scholars allow those in very long days to follow the times of Makkah or of a nearer moderate city.
  • Start dates: some mosques follow a local moon sighting, others Saudi Arabia's announcement, others calculation: so Ramadan and Eid can begin on different days in one town.
  • School and work: exams, sport and long shifts while fasting; many schools and employers make adjustments.
  • In a Muslim country: working hours often shorten and public life runs late; in Britain, Muslims are a minority, which can feel isolating or can strengthen identity.
Mission 2Evaluate

"Sawm benefits the ummah more than the individual."

  • Reasons for: the whole ummah fasts together; shared iftars and tarawih build community; Zakat al-Fitr and Ramadan giving help the poor; empathy with the hungry leads to action.
  • Reasons against: 2:183 names the purpose as taqwa, a quality of each heart; forgiveness of sins is personal; Allah judges each person's intention; someone fasting alone (e.g. a traveller making up days) gains the same spiritual benefit.
  • Another view: the two cannot be separated: individuals who grow in self-control and gratitude make a stronger ummah.
  • Judge it by: What does the Qur'an say fasting is for? Can a benefit to the ummah exist without benefits to individuals? How do Muslims who fast alone, e.g. new converts in Britain, experience it?
Mission check

?Bilal, 16, has type 1 diabetes. His doctor says fasting would put his health at serious risk. His grandmother, 82, is now too frail to fast without becoming ill. Both want to take part in Ramadan. According to the Qur'an and most Muslim scholars, what should they do?

Sawm is a duty, not a punishment: Allah "desireth for you ease". Those who cannot fast still take part through prayer, the Qur'an and giving.

Mission 3: Muslims have two great festivals each year. Why is one of them marked by a sacrifice?

Make a guess: Muslims have two great festivals each year. Why is one of them marked by a sacrifice?

Mission 3

Two Ids, one moon

  • Id (Arabic 'Id) means a festival: literally a day that returns every year.
  • Islam has two Ids that every Muslim group keeps:
  • Id-ul-Fitr: the festival of breaking the fast, at the end of Ramadan.
  • Id-ul-Adha: the festival of sacrifice, during the Hajj.
  • Both follow the lunar Islamic calendar, so they move about 11 days earlier each year in the solar calendar.
  • A new month starts when the new crescent moon is seen.
  • The Prophet set these two festivals in Madinah, in place of older festival days (hadith in Sunan Abu Dawud, paraphrase).
  • Your spec spells them Eid-ul-Fitr and Eid-ul-Adha.
Mission 3

Id-ul-Fitr: why Muslims celebrate

  • When: 1 Shawwal, the day after Ramadan ends.
  • Thanks: Muslims thank Allah for the strength to complete the fast.
  • The Qur'an asks fasting Muslims to "complete the period, and that ye should magnify Allah for having guided you, and that peradventure ye may be thankful" (Qur'an 2:185, Pickthall).
  • Remembering the Qur'an: Ramadan is the month the Qur'an was first revealed (see Mission 2).
  • Purpose: joy after self-discipline; forgiveness; the whole ummah celebrating together.
  • Zakat-ul-Fitr (fitrah): a fixed gift of food, or its value, paid for each family member before the Id prayer, so the poor can celebrate too.
Mission 3

Id-ul-Fitr: how Muslims celebrate

  • Wash (ghusl), wear new or best clothes, eat something sweet such as dates.
  • Recite the takbir on the way: "Allahu akbar" (God is the Greatest).
  • Id prayer in congregation: two rak'ahs with extra takbirs, then a sermon. Held in mosques, halls or parks.
  • Greet: "Id Mubarak" (blessed Id). Embrace and forgive.
  • Visit family and friends; share a festive meal and sweets.
  • Children receive gifts or money; cards are sent.
  • Some families visit the graves of relatives and pray for them.

Mission 3

Id-ul-Adha: Ibrahim's test

  • When: 10 Dhu al-Hijjah, the day pilgrims on Hajj sacrifice at Mina. Muslims everywhere join them.
  • Origin: the Qur'an tells how Ibrahim (Abraham) dreamed that Allah commanded him to sacrifice his son.
  • The son agreed. Both submitted to Allah. Muslims believe that at the last moment Allah stopped Ibrahim and provided a ram instead.
  • The Qur'an does not name the son. Most Muslims believe he was Isma'il (Ishmael).
  • Muslim tradition adds that Shaytan tried three times to tempt Ibrahim to disobey; he drove Shaytan away with stones. Pilgrims stone the pillars at Mina in memory.
  • Meaning: total submission to Allah, trust, and Allah's mercy.
Mission 3Read the source

"A clear test"

> "O my dear son, I have seen in a dream that I must sacrifice thee. So look, what thinkest thou? He said: O my father! Do that which thou art commanded. Allah willing, thou shalt find me of the steadfast." (Qur'an 37:102, Pickthall)

> "Lo! that verily was a clear test. Then We ransomed him with a tremendous victim." (Qur'an 37:106–107, Pickthall)

  • Verse 103: "they had both surrendered (to Allah)" (Qur'an 37:103, Pickthall).
  • Pilgrims who join the 'Umrah to the Hajj offer an animal sacrifice; anyone who cannot find one fasts ten days instead (Qur'an 2:196, paraphrase).

Q1 How does the son respond to his father?

Show answer

He accepts Allah's command and promises to be steadfast.

Q2 What does "ransomed" tell Muslims about Allah?

Show answer

Allah did not want the boy's death: He tested obedience, then showed mercy by providing an animal.

Mission 3

Id-ul-Adha: how Muslims celebrate

  • Id prayer in congregation in the morning, with takbir.
  • Qurbani (Arabic udhiyah): a sheep, goat, cow or camel is sacrificed after the prayer, in Allah's name. A healthy animal of a set minimum age.
  • The meat is usually shared in three parts: the family, relatives and friends, and the poor.
  • Many Muslims pay for qurbani abroad through a charity, so meat reaches people in need.
  • Family visits, a festive meal, new clothes, gifts.
  • The Qur'an stresses the inner meaning: "Their flesh and their food reach not Allah, but the devotion from you reacheth Him." (Qur'an 22:37, Pickthall)

Mission 3Diversity

One festival, different ways

  • Sunni and Shi'a Muslims both keep Id-ul-Fitr and Id-ul-Adha with Id prayer, charity and family meals.
  • In Shi'a Islam these two Ids sit beside other key days: Id-ul-Ghadeer and the mourning of Ashura (see Mission 4). Shi'a Muslims also pay Zakat-ul-Fitr, often called fitrah.
  • Qurbani: the Hanafi school treats it as a duty (wajib) for every adult Muslim who can afford it; the other Sunni schools and Shi'a scholars call it a strongly recommended sunnah.
  • Moon-sighting: some communities wait to see the moon locally; others follow Saudi Arabia's announcement or astronomical calculation. So Id can fall on different days, even in one city.
  • Culture: sweet dishes and new clothes for Id-ul-Fitr in many places, such as Turkey and South Asia; long journeys home in Indonesia; animal markets before Id-ul-Adha in Pakistan.
  • Different groups share the same core meaning: thanks, submission to Allah, and care for the poor.
Mission 3

Id in Britain and worldwide

  • Muslim-majority countries: Id is usually a public holiday, often of several days; shops, schools and offices close.
  • Britain: not a public holiday. Workers may need to ask for leave; schools in England must authorise absence on the day of Id itself, and any extra days are for the head teacher to decide.
  • Large Id prayers in parks and sports grounds; some councils host Id festivals.
  • Qurbani in Britain must by law take place in a licensed slaughterhouse, so families order it through a butcher or pay a charity to sacrifice abroad.
  • Moon-sighting differences can mean British Muslims celebrate on different days.
  • Changes: Id cards, online greetings, shop displays and Id-themed products; families spread across countries celebrate by video call.

Mission 3Evaluate

"Id-ul-Adha is more important than Id-ul-Fitr."

  • For: Muslim scholars often call it the greater Id (al-'Id al-Kabir). It is joined to Hajj, a pillar of Islam. It remembers Ibrahim, a model of total submission. It lasts longer: qurbani may continue on the days after Id (to the 12th or, for some schools, the 13th of Dhu al-Hijjah).
  • For: the sacrifice is an act of worship in itself, and feeds the poor across the world.
  • Against: Id-ul-Fitr crowns a whole month of fasting, prayer and Qur'an reading. It is often the more widely celebrated festival in families, especially for children.
  • Against: the Prophet set both Ids together; each teaches a different side of Islam: gratitude after self-discipline, and submission after testing.
  • Judge it by: link to the Five Pillars? Which shapes daily life more? Which is celebrated more in Britain today? Can festivals with different purposes be ranked?

Mission 4

The tenth of Muharram

  • Muharram is the first month of the Islamic year: one of the four sacred months.
  • Ashura comes from the Arabic for "ten": the 10th day of Muharram.
  • The day carries two memories:
  • Musa (Moses) and the Israelites saved from Pharaoh: kept by most Sunni Muslims with a voluntary fast.
  • The martyrdom of Husayn, the Prophet's grandson, at Karbala in 680 CE: mourned by Shi'a Muslims.
  • Ashura is not an Id: no new clothes or celebration for most Muslims.
  • Your spec spells his name Hussayn.
Mission 4

Ashura in Sunni Islam: Musa and fasting

  • In Madinah the Prophet found Jews fasting on this day, to thank God for saving Musa. He said Muslims have a closer claim to Musa, fasted, and told Muslims to fast too (Sahih al-Bukhari, paraphrase).
  • When Ramadan became compulsory, the Ashura fast became voluntary (Sahih al-Bukhari, from Aisha, paraphrase).
  • Many Sunni Muslims fast on the 9th and 10th, following the Prophet's wish to fast the 9th as well (Sahih Muslim, paraphrase).
  • Meaning: thanks for Allah's rescue of the oppressed; hope of forgiveness for the past year (Sahih Muslim, paraphrase).
  • Some local customs: in Turkey families cook aşure, a sweet pudding shared with neighbours, linked to Nuh's ark; in Morocco children receive gifts.
  • Most Sunni Muslims also honour Husayn as a martyr from the Prophet's family.
Mission 4

Karbala, 680 CE

  • Husayn (Husayn ibn Ali): son of Ali and Fatimah, grandson of the Prophet; for most Shi'a Muslims the third Imam.
  • In 680 Yazid became caliph. Husayn refused to pledge loyalty to him, seeing his rule as unjust.
  • Invited by Muslims in Kufa, he set out with family and companions: a small group, women and children among them.
  • Yazid's army stopped them at Karbala, in present-day Iraq, and cut them off from the river: days without water.
  • On 10 Muharram 61 AH (680 CE) Husayn and his companions were killed, including his half-brother Abbas and his infant son.
  • The women, including his sister Zaynab, were taken captive to Damascus. Zaynab spoke out at Yazid's court.
  • His shrine in Karbala is one of the most visited places of Shi'a pilgrimage.
Mission 4Read the source

Why Husayn stood up

  • Before leaving Madinah, Husayn wrote to his brother that he did not go out for pride or power, but to seek reform in the community of his grandfather, to command the good and forbid the evil (Husayn's letter to Muhammad ibn al-Hanafiyyah, Shi'a historical sources, paraphrase).
  • Shi'a Muslims read the Qur'an's words on martyrs as true of Husayn:

> "Think not of those, who are slain in the way of Allah, as dead. Nay, they are living. With their Lord they have provision." (Qur'an 3:169, Pickthall)

  • At news of loss, Muslims say: "Lo! we are Allah's and lo! unto Him we are returning." (Qur'an 2:156, Pickthall)

Q1 What reason does Husayn give for his journey?

Show answer

Reform: to stand for good and against wrong, not for power.

Q2 Why does 3:169 give mourners hope?

Show answer

Martyrs are alive with Allah; Husayn's death is not the end.

Mission 4

How Shi'a Muslims commemorate Ashura

  • The first ten days of Muharram are a time of mourning: black clothes, no weddings or parties.
  • Majlis (gatherings): the story of Karbala is retold, with sermons and mournful poetry.
  • Matam: rhythmic beating of the chest in grief, often while chanting.
  • Processions carry standards (alam) and models of Husayn's shrine through the streets.
  • Water and food are given freely to anyone, recalling Husayn's thirst.
  • In Iran, ta'ziyeh plays re-enact the events.
  • A minority cut themselves (tatbir). Many senior Shi'a scholars discourage or forbid this, and many communities hold blood donation drives instead.
  • Arba'een: 40 days later, huge crowds walk to Karbala on pilgrimage.

Mission 4

Ashura in Britain and worldwide

  • Britain: majalis in Shi'a centres (often called Husayniyyas) every evening of early Muharram.
  • London: a large mourning procession through central London on Ashura, with chanting, banners and water stalls.
  • Many British Shi'a run blood donation and charity campaigns, presenting Husayn as a model of justice and sacrifice.
  • Some explain Ashura to neighbours and schools, so the processions are understood.
  • Worldwide: public holiday and mass mourning in Iran, Iraq, Pakistan and Bahrain; huge crowds in Karbala.
  • Where Shi'a are a minority, processions can need police protection, and some have been attacked.
  • Issues in non-Muslim countries: time off school or work; permission for street processions; explaining matam to others.
Mission 4

Id-ul-Ghadeer

  • When: 18 Dhu al-Hijjah, eight days after Id-ul-Adha.
  • What: returning from his Farewell Hajj in 632, the Prophet stopped at the pool of Khumm and spoke to the pilgrims about Ali.
  • Shi'a Muslims celebrate this as Ali's appointment as the Prophet's successor: an Id, a festival of joy.
  • Your spec links it to the appointment of Ali and the Prophet's last sermon.
  • Practices: special prayers and du'a, recommended fasting, visiting Ali's shrine in Najaf, gifts, celebratory meals, weddings, and pledges of brotherhood between friends.
  • Some Shi'a hadith call Ghadir the greatest Id of Allah.
  • A public holiday in Iran.
Mission 4Read the source

"This day have I perfected your religion"

  • The Prophet took Ali's hand: "Of whomever I am mawla, Ali is his mawla" (Hadith of Ghadir Khumm, paraphrase). Both Sunni and Shi'a collections record it.

> "This day have I perfected your religion for you and completed My favour unto you, and have chosen for you as religion al-Islam." (Qur'an 5:3, Pickthall)

  • Shi'a interpretation: revealed at Ghadir, after Ali's appointment. Religion was "perfected" when leadership after the Prophet was settled.
  • Sunni interpretation: revealed at Arafat during the Farewell Hajj (Sahih al-Bukhari, report of Umar). Religion was perfected as the revelation of law came to completion.
  • Mawla: Shi'a read "master and guardian"; most Sunni read "friend and helper".

Q1 Why does the place where 5:3 was revealed matter?

Show answer

If at Ghadir, it links the completion of Islam to Ali's appointment.

Q2 Why do most Sunni Muslims not keep Ghadir as a festival?

Show answer

They read the event as honouring Ali, not appointing a successor.

Mission 4Diversity

Same days, different meanings

  • Ashura, Shi'a: the central day of mourning for Husayn; a lesson in standing against injustice.
  • Ashura, Sunni: a voluntary fast of thanks for Musa's rescue; Husayn honoured as a martyr, without public mourning in most places.
  • In South Asia many Sunni Muslims, especially in Sufi traditions, also hold Muharram gatherings and join processions.
  • Some Sunni scholars criticise mourning rituals as bid'ah (innovation); some Shi'a scholars criticise tatbir.
  • Some Shi'a fast part of Ashura day until the afternoon in grief, rather than as a fast of thanks.
  • Ghadir: an Id for Shi'a Muslims; for Sunni Muslims, an honour for Ali, not a festival.
  • A popular Shi'a saying: "Every day is Ashura, every land is Karbala": Husayn's stand applies everywhere.

Mission 4Evaluate

"Ashura is the most important day of the year for Shi'a Muslims."

  • For: Karbala is central to Shi'a identity and to belief in the Imams. Mourning lasts many days and leads to Arba'een. It teaches sacrifice and resisting injustice in every age.
  • Against: the two Ids were set by the Prophet for all Muslims. Ramadan and Laylat al-Qadr mark the revelation of the Qur'an.
  • Against: some Shi'a hadith call Ghadir the greatest Id.
  • Another view: Ashura is a commemoration, not a festival, so it is important in a different way: it is not in competition with the Ids.
  • Judge it by: what the Prophet commanded; what shapes Shi'a identity most; how much of the year the day affects; whether mourning and celebration can be ranked.

?A Shi'a youth group in Birmingham organises a blood donation drive during Muharram under the slogan "Give blood, not tears alone". Which belief about Ashura most explains this choice?

The same tenth of Muharram means thanks for Musa's rescue to most Sunni Muslims and grief for Husayn to Shi'a Muslims.

Mission 5

Mission 5: Many people think "jihad" means holy war. Why do most Muslims say its main meaning is a struggle inside?

Make a guess: Many people think "jihad" means holy war. Why do most Muslims say its main meaning is a struggle inside?

Mission 5

Jihad means "striving"

  • Jihad comes from the Arabic root j-h-d: to strive, to make an effort, to struggle.
  • In Islam it means striving in the way of Allah (fi sabil Allah): doing what Allah wants against whatever pulls you away.
  • It is not the Arabic for "holy war". The words for war and fighting are harb and qital.
  • "As for those who strive in Us, We surely guide them to Our paths, and lo! Allah is with the good." (Qur'an 29:69, Pickthall)
  • Two kinds are usually taught: greater jihad (the inner struggle) and lesser jihad (the outward struggle, including defending Islam and, in strict conditions, fighting).
Mission 5

Greater jihad: the daily struggle

  • The struggle against the nafs (the selfish self): laziness, anger, greed, pride, temptation.
  • Examples: getting up for Fajr; keeping the fast; paying zakah; being honest when lying is easier; controlling your temper; avoiding gossip; studying the Qur'an.
  • The Prophet told a man who wanted to join a military campaign to do his jihad by caring for his parents (Sahih al-Bukhari, paraphrase).
Mission 5

Why most Muslims put greater jihad first

  • A widely quoted saying has the Prophet return from battle and say, "We have returned from the lesser jihad to the greater jihad" (paraphrase). Many hadith scholars class this report as weak, but its idea is widely accepted.
  • A reliable hadith teaches that the true striver (mujahid) is the one who strives against his own self for Allah (Jami' at-Tirmidhi, paraphrase).
  • Reasons Muslims give: greater jihad is for every Muslim, every day; lesser jihad is rare and conditional; anyone who fights without a pure heart has failed the greater jihad first.
  • Another hadith: the best jihad is a word of truth before an unjust ruler (Sunan Abi Dawud, paraphrase).
  • OCR: the precedence of greater jihad over lesser jihad.

Mission 5

Origins of lesser jihad

  • Makkah (610–622): Muslims were mocked, boycotted, beaten and some killed. For thirteen years they were told to be patient and not to fight back.
  • 622: the Hijrah to Madinah.
  • Madinah: permission to fight came, for self-defence: 22:39 is traditionally seen as the first verse permitting it.
  • Battles with the Makkans followed: Badr (624), then the Makkan attacks on Madinah at Uhud (625) and the Trench (627).
  • 628: the Treaty of Hudaybiyyah: a peace treaty the Prophet accepted though its terms seemed unfavourable.
  • 630: the Muslims entered Makkah almost without fighting; the Prophet pardoned its people.
Mission 5Read the source

Permission and limits

"Sanction is given unto those who fight because they have been wronged; and Allah is indeed Able to give them victory" (Qur'an 22:39, Pickthall)

"Fight in the way of Allah against those who fight against you, but begin not hostilities. Lo! Allah loveth not aggressors." (Qur'an 2:190, Pickthall)

  • "And if they incline to peace, incline thou also to it, and trust in Allah." (Qur'an 8:61, Pickthall)

Q1 According to 22:39, why are Muslims given permission to fight?

Show answer

Because they have been wronged: it is a response to injustice.

Q2 Which phrase in 2:190 limits the fighting?

Show answer

Mission 5

Conditions for lesser jihad

  • Just cause: self-defence, defending the oppressed (4:75), or protecting freedom to worship (22:40). Never for land, wealth, revenge or to force conversion (2:256).
  • Right authority: declared by a legitimate leader, not by an individual or a self-appointed group.
  • Last resort: after peaceful ways have failed.
  • Right intention: for Allah and justice, not for glory or hatred.
  • Conduct: no attacks on non-combatants. The first caliph Abu Bakr told his army not to kill women, children or the elderly, not to harm monks at worship, and not to cut down fruit trees or destroy buildings (paraphrase).
  • Proportion and an end: fight only as much as needed; stop when the enemy stops (2:192–193); accept peace when offered (8:61).

Mission 5

Ma'ruf and munkar

  • Ma'ruf: what is good and right. Munkar: what is wrong and harmful.
  • The Qur'an calls for a community "who invite to goodness, and enjoin right conduct and forbid indecency" (Qur'an 3:104, Pickthall).
  • Amr bil-ma'ruf wa nahy anil-munkar ("commanding good and forbidding wrong") is a duty for all Muslims; for Shi'a Muslims these are two of the Ten Obligatory Acts.
  • The Prophet taught: whoever sees a wrong should change it with the hand; if unable, with the tongue; if unable, then hate it in the heart, the weakest faith (Sahih Muslim, paraphrase).
  • OCR wording: lesser jihad as the removal of evil from society, with the goal of making the world an abode of peace. Most of this is done by speaking, campaigning, charity and good example, not fighting.
Mission 5Diversity

Different understandings

  • Most Muslims: greater jihad is central; lesser jihad is mainly peaceful effort, and armed jihad only defensive, under strict conditions, declared by legitimate authority.
  • Sunni and Shi'a: many Twelver Shi'a scholars teach that a war started by Muslims (not defence) needs the presence of the Imam; while the Twelfth Imam is hidden, only defensive jihad is allowed.
  • Classical law: most medieval jurists also allowed a caliph to wage war beyond defence; most scholars today teach that only defence is permitted.
  • Peace-centred views: some Muslims argue that armed jihad has no place today, as conflicts are handled by states and international law; the struggle now is for justice through peaceful means.
  • Extremist claims: a small number of groups claim "jihad" for terrorism. In 2014 over 120 Muslim scholars signed an Open Letter to the leader of the group calling itself "Islamic State", setting out from the Qur'an and hadith why its actions were forbidden.
  • If and when the "holy war" aspect may be waged: all mainstream views require just cause, authority and conduct rules; they differ on whether offensive war could ever be justified.
Mission 5Evaluate

"Greater jihad is more important than lesser jihad."

  • Reasons for: every Muslim faces it daily; it underlies all other worship; the saying about the "greater jihad" (though weak) and the reliable hadith about striving against the self; lesser jihad without a pure heart is worthless.
  • Reasons against: lesser jihad can save lives and defend the oppressed (4:75; 22:39–40); the Qur'an praises those who strive with wealth and lives; for a community under attack, defence is urgent; the greater/lesser terms are not in the Qur'an itself.
  • Another view: they are not rivals: outward struggle for justice is only right when inner jihad has purified the intention.
  • Judge it by: Which affects more Muslims? Which is more often in the Qur'an and hadith? Does importance depend on the situation? How reliable is the "greater jihad" saying?

For most Muslims, jihad looks like Yusuf's week: the struggle to live as Allah wants, and armed struggle is a rare, strictly limited exception.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Sadaqah is voluntary charity of any kind, to anyone; sadaqah jariyah keeps on benefiting (Qur'an 2:271–274; 2:261).
  • Islamic Relief (1984, Birmingham) works in the UK and worldwide, inspired by zakah, sadaqah and 5:32.

Mission 2

  • Sawm is fasting from dawn to sunset in Ramadan (Qur'an 2:183–185); its aim is taqwa, with suhur before dawn and iftar at sunset.
  • The ill, travellers, children, pregnant women and the elderly are excused; missed days are made up (qada) or replaced by feeding the poor (fidyah).
  • Laylat al-Qadr, in the last ten nights, marks the first revelation (Qur'an 96:1–5) and is "better than a thousand months" (97:3).

Mission 3

  • Id-ul-Fitr ends Ramadan: thanks to Allah, Zakat-ul-Fitr, Id prayer, new clothes, food, gifts and family.
  • Id-ul-Adha remembers Ibrahim's test (Qur'an 37:102–107) during Hajj: Id prayer, qurbani shared with family, friends and the poor; devotion, not meat, reaches Allah (22:37).
  • In Britain Id is not a public holiday; qurbani is done through licensed slaughterhouses or charities abroad, and moon-sighting differences shape the date.

Mission 4

  • Ashura, 10 Muharram: most Sunni Muslims fast voluntarily, remembering Musa's rescue; Shi'a Muslims mourn Husayn's martyrdom at Karbala (680 CE) with majalis, matam, processions and charity.
  • In Britain, Shi'a Muslims hold majalis, a mourning procession in London, and blood donation and charity drives.
  • Id-ul-Ghadeer (18 Dhu al-Hijjah) is a Shi'a Id for Ali's appointment; Shi'a and Sunni Muslims read Surah 5:3 and the Ghadir hadith differently.

Mission 5

  • Jihad means "striving" in the way of Allah. Greater jihad is the daily inner struggle against the nafs; most Muslims see it as more important.
  • Lesser jihad began in Madinah as self-defence (Qur'an 22:39, 2:190): just cause, legitimate authority, last resort, right intention, no harm to non-combatants, and peace when offered.
  • Commanding good (ma'ruf) and forbidding wrong (munkar) (3:104) is a peaceful outward striving.
