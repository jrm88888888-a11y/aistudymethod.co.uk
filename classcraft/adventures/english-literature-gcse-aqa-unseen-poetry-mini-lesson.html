Unseen Poetry

This mini-lesson drills the skills and terminology you need for AQA GCSE English Literature Paper 2, Section C: analysing a poem you've never seen before, then comparing it with a second unseen poem.

Be honest about what this is: this game builds the analysis framework and terminology — subject, tone, structure, language, sound — and the key exam-technique facts. It does not write the finished essay for you; that skill you practise on real unseen poems in timed writing.

Work through each screen, answer the questions as you go and collect ⭐ stars. Press Start when you're ready.

How the exam works

Two questions, one section

Section C gives you a poem you have never studied. There are two tasks:

  • Question 1 (24 marks): analyse ONE unseen poem in detail.
  • Question 2 (8 marks): compare that poem with a second unseen poem, focusing on the poets' methods.

The key exam-technique fact: Unseen Poetry rewards AO1 and AO2 — your reading of the poem plus the poets' methods and their effects. Question 1 (24 marks) is marked on AO1 and AO2; the Question 2 comparison (8 marks) is AO2 only. There is no AO3: you don't need (and can't be credited for) context, because nobody expects you to know the background of a poem you've never seen.

A way in

A framework for reading a new poem

Facing an unfamiliar poem, work through the same five lenses every time:

  • Subject: what is the poem actually about? Read it twice before deciding.
  • Tone / mood: how does it feel — angry, wistful, celebratory, anxious?
  • Structure / form: stanza shape, line length, rhyme scheme, enjambment, a volta (turn) in mood or argument.
  • Language & imagery: word choice, metaphor, simile, personification.
  • Sound: alliteration, sibilance, rhythm, onomatopoeia.

Always push further: for every method you spot, ask "what effect does this have on the reader?" A list of devices with no effect earns very little.

The toolkit

Terminology worth knowing cold

  • Metaphor — says one thing IS another (not "like" or "as").
  • Simile — compares two things using "like" or "as".
  • Personification — giving human qualities to something non-human.
  • Enjambment — a sentence runs on past the line break with no pause.
  • Caesura — a pause or break in the middle of a line, often marked by punctuation.
  • Sibilance — repeated soft "s" or "sh" sounds.
  • Alliteration — repeated initial consonant sounds.
  • Volta — a turn or shift in the poem's mood or argument.
  • Onomatopoeia — words that imitate the sound they describe.

Rhyme and rhythm (metre) matter too — a broken rhyme scheme or a stumbling rhythm can mirror a broken or anxious subject.

AO2 · method & effect

Method → effect, every time

Naming a device earns almost nothing. Marks come from linking the method to the effect it creates on the reader:

method + evidence → effect on the readere.g. the personification in "the wind clawed at the window" (method) gives the wind a violent, predatory quality, creating a threatening, unsettled mood (effect)

This is what separates a strong analytical comment from feature-spotting: feature-spotting just names a device ("this is a metaphor"); analysis explains what the device does to the reader's understanding or feeling.

Golden rule: never stop at "this is personification." Finish the sentence: "…which suggests / creates / makes the reader feel …".

What examiners reward

Strong analysis vs feature-spotting

Compare these two responses to the invented line "the sea whispered its secrets":

❌ Feature-spotting (weak)

"This is personification because the sea is given a human action."

✅ Method → effect (strong)

"The personification of the sea as a figure who 'whispered' suggests something secretive and almost human about nature, creating an intimate, hushed tone that draws the reader in."

The difference: the strong version still names the method, but then explains the effect — what it suggests, and how it shapes the reader's feeling or understanding.

Question 2 · 8 marks

Comparing two unseen poems

Question 2 gives you a second unseen poem and asks you to compare it with the first, focusing on the poets' methods:

  • Compare on the same dimensions each time — tone, imagery, structure, sound — rather than jumping between different features for each poem.
  • Use comparative connectives: whereas, similarly, however, by contrast, both.
  • Because the second poem is also unseen, the same rule applies: this is AO2 only. You compare methods and effects, not context.

Example connective use: "Whereas the first poet uses gentle sibilance to create calm, the second poet's harsh plosives create a violent, aggressive tone."

Tone, mood & structure

Reading the shape of a poem

Structure carries meaning just as much as word choice. Look for:

  • A volta — a turn in mood or argument, often signalled by a word like "but" or "yet", or a shift at a stanza break.
  • A change in tone across the poem — does it start angry and end resigned? Start calm and end frantic?
  • Enjambment creating flow or urgency, versus caesura creating hesitation or interruption.
  • A broken or irregular rhyme/rhythm that mirrors disorder, or a steady one that mirrors control.

Always ask: does the poem's shape on the page match, or fight against, its subject and tone? That tension is often where the best analysis lives.

Avoid these traps

Slips to avoid

  • Adding context: don't guess at the poet's biography or historical background — Unseen Poetry is never assessed on AO3, and invented "context" earns nothing.
  • Feature-spotting: naming a device without explaining its effect earns very little.
  • Simile vs metaphor: a simile compares using "like" or "as"; a metaphor says one thing simply IS another.
  • Ignoring structure: don't only discuss language — form, rhyme, and shifts in tone (voltas) are just as valid for AO2.
  • Comparing unevenly: in Question 2, compare both poems on the same dimension at once, rather than describing one fully then the other separately.

Fix: get the terminology exactly right, then always push a method into method → effect, and use connectives when comparing.
