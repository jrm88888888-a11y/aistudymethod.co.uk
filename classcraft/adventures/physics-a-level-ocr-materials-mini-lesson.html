Mission 1

Mission 1: Why are the markings on a spring weighing scale evenly spaced?

Make a guess: Why are the markings on a spring weighing scale evenly spaced?

Mission 1

Hooke's law

  • Stretch a spring: the force it exerts is proportional to its extension.
  • This holds only up to a limit.
  • Extension = stretched length − original (natural) length. Not the whole length.
  • F = kx. k is the force constant of the spring or wire.
  • Unit of k: N m⁻¹. Larger k: stiffer spring.
  • Works for compression too: the extension is then the decrease in length.
  • Stretching is tensile deformation; squashing is compressive deformation.
Quick check
?In a lab, a student models a spring as a light spring that obeys Hooke's law. Which statement describes how the spring's force behaves in this model?
Mission 1

Worked example

  • A spring hangs with natural length 12.0 cm. A 4.0 N weight stretches it to 17.0 cm.
  • Extension = 17.0 − 12.0 = 5.0 cm = 0.050 m.
  • k = force ÷ extension = 4.0 ÷ 0.050 = 80 N m⁻¹.
  • Hang 6.0 N instead: extension = 6.0 ÷ 80 = 0.075 m = 7.5 cm.
  • New length = 12.0 + 7.5 = 19.5 cm. Always add the extension back to the natural length.
Mission 1

The force–extension graph

  • Plot force against extension. Hooke's law: a straight line through the origin.
  • Gradient = k.
  • Beyond the limit of proportionality the line curves.
  • Below the elastic limit: remove the load and it returns to its original length.
  • Beyond the elastic limit: it is permanently stretched.
Mission 1

Investigating force–extension (PAG2)

  • Clamp the spring (or rubber band, or polythene strip) above a vertical metre rule.
  • Add masses one at a time. Read the pointer at eye level each time; record the length.
  • Extension = length − original length. Plot force (weight of masses) against extension.
  • Unload and measure again: is it back to its original length?
  • Spring: straight line. Rubber band: a curve, and unloading gives a different curve. Polythene: stretches permanently.
Quick check
?The graph shows the magnitude F of the force exerted by a light spring that obeys Hooke's law, as a function of the spring's length L, from its natural length to a length of 20 cm. What is the force constant of the spring?
Mission check

?A spring that obeys Hooke's law is 15.0 cm long when it supports a 3.0 N load and 18.0 cm long when it supports a 6.0 N load. What is its natural (unloaded) length?

Extension ∝ force, so each extra newton adds the same extension: evenly spaced markings.

As long as the spring stays within its limit of proportionality.

Mission 2

Mission 2: A thin steel wire and a thick steel cable carry the same load. Why does the wire stretch more?

Make a guess: A thin steel wire and a thick steel cable carry the same load. Why does the wire stretch more?

Mission 2

Stress and strain

  • Tensile stress = force ÷ cross-sectional area. Unit: Pa (N m⁻²).
  • Tensile strain = extension ÷ original length. No unit (a ratio).
  • σ = F/A; ε = x/L.
  • Compressive stress and strain: the same, for a squashing force and a decrease in length.
  • Ultimate tensile strength (UTS): the maximum stress a material can withstand before it breaks.
  • Same load on a thinner wire → larger stress.
Mission 2

The Young modulus

  • Young modulus = tensile stress ÷ tensile strain. Unit: Pa.
  • E = σ/ε, which gives E = FL/Ax.
  • Valid only where Hooke's law holds (up to the limit of proportionality).
  • It is the gradient of the straight part of a stress–strain graph.
  • It belongs to the material. The force constant (Young modulus × area ÷ length) belongs to the object.
  • Steel: about 2 × 10¹¹ Pa. Rubber: about 10⁶ Pa.
Mission 2

Worked example

  • Steel wire: length 2.50 m, diameter 0.40 mm, load 20 N, E = 2.0 × 10¹¹ Pa. Extension?
  • A = π(d/2)² = π(0.20 × 10⁻³)² = 1.26 × 10⁻⁷ m². (Radius, not diameter.)
  • Stress = F/A = 20 ÷ 1.26 × 10⁻⁷ = 1.6 × 10⁸ Pa.
  • Strain = stress/E = 1.6 × 10⁸ ÷ 2.0 × 10¹¹ = 8.0 × 10⁻⁴.
  • Extension = strain × original length = 8.0 × 10⁻⁴ × 2.50 = 2.0 × 10⁻³ m (2.0 mm).
Quick check
?A metal wire of length 1.80 m and diameter 0.30 mm is loaded, and a graph of force F against extension is plotted. The straight part of the graph has a gradient of 7.9 × 10³ N m⁻¹. What is the Young modulus of the metal?
Mission 2

Measuring the Young modulus

  • A long, thin wire: a large extension from a modest load, so its percentage uncertainty is small.
  • Clamp one end. Run it over a pulley, or hang it beside an identical reference wire (Searle's apparatus).
  • Diameter: micrometer, several places and directions; average. Length: metre rule to the marker.
  • Add masses in steps; read the extension from a marker against a scale (or a vernier). Unload to check it returns: still elastic.
  • Plot force against extension. Young modulus = gradient × original length ÷ area. (Or plot stress against strain: gradient = Young modulus.)
  • The reference wire cancels temperature changes and sag of the support. Wear eye protection: the wire may snap.
Quick check
?A student repeats the Young modulus experiment with the same loads on a wire of the same metal and diameter, but only one tenth as long. The extension is read with the same scale. What is the main effect?
Mission check

?Two cables are made of the same aluminium alloy and carry the same load. Cable Q is 3 times as long as cable P and has 3 times its diameter. Both obey Hooke's law. What is the extension of Q divided by the extension of P?

Same steel means the same Young modulus. The thin wire has a smaller area, so the same load gives a larger stress, so a larger strain and a larger extension.

Mission 3

Mission 3: Stretch a spring twice as far. Does it store twice the energy?

Make a guess: Stretch a spring twice as far. Does it store twice the energy?

Mission 3

Work done stretching = area

  • Stretching a spring or wire takes work: force × distance moved.
  • The force is not constant: it rises from 0 to its final value as the extension grows from 0.
  • Work done = area under the force–extension graph.
  • In the elastic region this work is stored as elastic strain energy (also called elastic potential energy). Unload, and you get it back.
Mission 3

Energy stored = ½ × force × extension

  • Hooke's law region: the graph is a straight line, so the area is a triangle.
  • E = ½Fx (F = final force, x = extension).
  • Use F = kx: E = ½(kx)x = ½kx².
  • Double the extension → four times the energy.
Mission 3

Worked example

  • Spring: k = 80 N m⁻¹, stretched by 0.050 m.
  • Force: F = kx = 80 × 0.050 = 4.0 N.
  • Energy: E = ½Fx = ½ × 4.0 × 0.050 = 0.10 J.
  • Check: ½kx² = ½ × 80 × 0.050² = 0.10 J.
  • Stretched to 0.10 m: ½ × 80 × 0.10² = 0.40 J. Twice the extension, four times the energy.
  • Not ½ × 4.0 × 0.10: the force is now 8.0 N, not 4.0 N.
Quick check
?A steel wire obeys Hooke's law. A tension of 60 N gives an extension of 1.2 mm. How much elastic strain energy does the wire store?
Mission 3

Curved graphs: use the area

  • Beyond the limit of proportionality the graph curves. ½ × force × extension no longer works.
  • Find the area: count squares, or split it into triangles and trapezia.
  • Energy of one square = (force per square) × (extension per square).
  • Plastic region (the sample stays permanently stretched when unloaded): the work done is not all stored. Most becomes internal energy (the sample warms); the wire stays longer.
Quick check
?The graph shows an idealised force–extension graph for a metal wire. The force rises linearly from 0 to 30 N over the first 4.0 mm, then stays at 30 N as the extension increases to 10.0 mm. How much work is done stretching the wire to 10.0 mm?
Mission 3

Spring energy becomes kinetic and potential energy

  • A launcher spring, k = 200 N m⁻¹, compressed by 0.050 m, fires a 0.020 kg ball.
  • Stored: E = ½kx² = ½ × 200 × 0.050² = 0.25 J.
  • Fired horizontally, all to kinetic energy: ½mv² = 0.25 → v = √(2 × 0.25 ÷ 0.020) = 5.0 m s⁻¹.
  • Fired straight up, all to gravitational potential energy at the top: Δh = 0.25 ÷ (0.020 × 9.81) = 1.3 m above the release point.
  • Real launchers lose some energy to friction and air resistance: the ball rises less.
Quick check
?The spring in a toy dart launcher has a spring constant of 400 N m⁻¹. It is compressed by 0.030 m and then released, firing a dart of mass 0.012 kg horizontally. All the elastic potential energy is transferred to the dart's kinetic energy. What is the speed of the dart?
Mission check

?A spring obeys Hooke's law. When it is compressed by 3.0 cm it stores 0.45 J. How much energy does it store when it is compressed by 4.5 cm?

Energy = ½k × (extension)², so twice the stretch stores four times the energy: twice the extension, at twice the final force.

Mission 4

Mission 4: Bend a paperclip a little and it springs back. Bend it further and it stays bent. Why?

Make a guess: Bend a paperclip a little and it springs back. Bend it further and it stays bent. Why?

Mission 4

Force–extension graph of a metal wire

  • Limit of proportionality (P): up to here, extension ∝ force (Hooke's law). Straight line.
  • Elastic limit (EL): the largest force after which the wire still returns to its original length.
  • Beyond the elastic limit: plastic region. Then the wire breaks.
Mission 4

Elastic and plastic

  • Elastic deformation: removed load → returns to its original length.
  • Plastic deformation: removed load → permanent extension.
  • The limit of proportionality and the elastic limit are different points.
  • A metal loaded into the plastic region unloads along a line parallel to the original straight line. It ends up permanently longer.
Quick check
?A wire is loaded until its force–extension graph has just started to curve. The load is then removed, and the wire returns exactly to its original length. What can you conclude about the load?
Mission 4

Three kinds of stress–strain curve

  • Ductile (copper, mild steel): straight, then a long plastic region. Can be drawn into wires. Thins (necks) before it breaks.
  • Brittle (glass, cast iron): straight right up to fracture. No plastic region. Snaps suddenly.
  • Polymeric (rubber): low stiffness, very large strain, curved. (Polythene: stretches plastically, cold-drawing.)
  • Ultimate tensile strength (UTS): the highest stress on the curve.
Quick check
?Two materials are tested to destruction. Material X: the stress–strain graph is straight all the way to fracture, at a strain of 0.001. Material Y: the graph is straight to a strain of 0.002, then curves and continues to a strain of 0.40 before fracture. Which describes the materials?
Mission check

?A copper wire is loaded beyond its elastic limit. At the maximum load of 30 N its extension is 6.0 mm. The straight first part of its force–extension graph has a gradient of 20 N per mm. The load is then removed completely. What is the permanent extension of the wire?

A small bend stays below the elastic limit: elastic, so the clip springs back. A bigger bend passes the elastic limit: the deformation becomes plastic, and a permanent bend remains.

Recap

The big ideas

Mission 1

  • Extension (not length) is proportional to force: gradient of the force–extension graph = k, in N m⁻¹.
  • This holds up to the limit of proportionality.
  • Beyond the elastic limit the stretch is permanent.

Mission 2

  • Stress = F/A (Pa); strain = extension/original length (no unit); Young modulus = stress/strain, the gradient of the linear part of a stress–strain graph.
  • Measure the Young modulus with a long, thin wire: Young modulus = gradient of force against extension × original length ÷ area.

Mission 3

  • Elastic strain energy = area under the force–extension graph = ½Fx = ½kx² while Hooke's law holds; for a curved graph, find the area.

Mission 4

  • Limit of proportionality: end of the straight line. Elastic limit: beyond it, deformation is plastic and permanent.
  • Ductile: long plastic region; brittle: straight to fracture; polymeric: large strain, low stiffness.
  • UTS = maximum stress on the stress–strain curve.
